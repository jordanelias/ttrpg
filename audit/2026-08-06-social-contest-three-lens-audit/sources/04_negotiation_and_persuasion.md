# Negotiation and Persuasion
## Fourth document. Classical rhetoric, modern behavioural science, and the systems that follow from them.

**The pivot.** Documents 1–3 modelled argument as an attack on *propositions*: claims with premises, warrants, and provenance, defeated by undermining, rebutting, or undercutting. That is adjudication. Persuasion is not that. Persuasion is an operation on *minds* — a counterpart's, an audience's, a jury's — and the mind does not evaluate claims one at a time. It builds a story and picks the one it likes best.

That single shift, which §2.3 establishes empirically, is the most consequential thing in this document, because it supplies a **sourced replacement for the one piece of the prior design that was invented rather than derived** (§6.2 of document 3, the warrant/attack matrix, correctly identified in the last audit as fabricated and formatted to look rigorous).

**Three targets, not one.** The design must distinguish:

| Target | Who decides | Persuasion is | Governing research |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Counterpart** | The person you are talking to | Moving a reservation value | Negotiation analysis: anchoring, logrolling, alternatives |
| **Audience** | A body that will act, and whose members have their own aims | Building a coalition of minds | Rhetoric; ELM; Cialdini; Reacting to the Past |
| **Jury** | A body that must reach a verdict and then talks to itself | Winning the story, then surviving deliberation | Story model; deliberation dynamics |

**Method.** Same as the session began. Research first, from named sources, tier-tagged, with the strongest reachable tier stated. Then a survey of what existing games model. Then primitives, attached to the M1–M14 vocabulary from document 3 rather than a fourth parallel catalogue. Then adversarial audit, reporting survivals honestly.

## §1 — Classical research

### 1.1 Aristotle: the two kinds of proof, and why the distinction is a game mechanic

Aristotle divides means of persuasion (*pisteis*) into **atechnic** (inartistic — used by the speaker, not invented by him) and **entechnic** (artistic — found through the art). The atechnic list is short and concrete: **laws, witnesses, contracts, tortures, and oaths** `[T1 — SEP, *Aristotle's Rhetoric*; Silva Rhetoricae, citing Rhet. 1.2.2–3]`. The entechnic are three, distinguished by where they operate:

- **Ethos** — through the speaker: his credibility. Aristotle's position is that ethos is achieved *by what one says in the speech*, not by prior reputation `[T2]`.
- **Pathos** — through the hearer: the audience's emotional state. Book II holds that emotions cause people to change their judgments, and treats each emotion as having specific causes and effects `[T1]`.
- **Logos** — through the argument itself, in the form of the **enthymeme**, the rhetorical deduction, drawn from probabilities and signs, and located using **topoi** `[T1 — SEP]`.

**The mechanically important part is the atechnic/entechnic split**, and prior documents missed it entirely. The atechnic proofs are *inventory* — objects you hold, produce, and lose, exactly the Ace Attorney reading. The entechnic proofs are *actions* — things you do in the moment. A persuasion system needs both and must keep them separate: a witness is not a move, and an appeal to pity is not an item.

**The three genres**, each with its own end and its own tense: **judicial** (accusation and defence, past events, aiming at just/unjust); **deliberative** (exhortation and dissuasion, future events, aiming at advantageous/harmful); **epideictic** (praise and blame, present, aiming at honourable/shameful) `[T1 — SEP]`. Design consequence: **the genre determines which appeals are admissible.** An argument from advantage lands in a deliberative setting and looks like corruption in a judicial one.

A scholarly qualification worth keeping. On one reading, logos is the primary form and ethos and pathos are secondary — they can take enthymematic form but do not bear on the issue; in an ideal setting with good procedure and rational judges logos dominates, and it is in the *imperfect* real setting that ethos and pathos prevail `[T2 — academic re-examination of the triad]`. That is not a footnote. It is a parameter: **how corruptible the forum is determines which channel wins.**

**The epilogue's four jobs** (Rhet. III.19): dispose the hearer favourably toward the speaker and unfavourably toward the opponent; amplify and minimize; move the hearer to emotion; remind him of the main points `[T2]`. Four distinct closing moves, each a different operation. That is a menu.

### 1.2 Cicero and Quintilian: the canons, the parts, and the three styles

**Five canons** — *inventio* (finding the arguments), *dispositio* (arrangement), *elocutio* (style), *memoria* (memory), *pronuntiatio* (delivery). Organized by Cicero in *De Inventione* (c. 84 BC) and expanded by Quintilian in the *Institutio Oratoria* (c. AD 95) `[T1 — corroborated across five independent sources; Quintilian's own formulation quoted: the whole art consists of five parts]`.

Design reading: three of the five are already in the corpus as something else. *Inventio* is claim selection. *Memoria* is the record archive. *Dispositio* and *pronuntiatio* are the two that are genuinely missing and genuinely mechanical — **the order you present in, and the register you present in.** §4 makes both into moves.

**Six parts of the oration** — *exordium* (introduction, securing goodwill and establishing that the speaker is qualified), *narratio* (statement of facts), *partitio* (outline of what will be proved), *confirmatio* (proof), *refutatio* (answering counter-arguments), *peroratio* (conclusion, summarizing and making the final appeal) `[T1 — Cicero, *De Inventione* XIV–XV, LII; corroborated by four independent accounts]`.

**This is a turn structure, not a template.** Each part does a different job on a different target: *exordium* attacks disposition, *narratio* builds the story, *partitio* frames what counts as winning, *confirmatio* and *refutatio* attack propositions, *peroratio* moves emotion. A speech that skips *narratio* leaves the audience to build its own story from the evidence — which §2.3 shows is exactly how you lose.

**Three levels of style**, each suited to one of the three duties of the orator: plain (*docere*, to instruct), middle (*delectare/conciliare*, to please and win over), and grand (*movere*, to move) `[T1 — Quintilian; note one secondary source maps middle→moving, which conflicts with the standard mapping. [CONFLICT: register-to-duty mapping — resolved toward the standard plain=docere / middle=delectare / grand=movere on the weight of the tradition]`.

Register is a **choice with a cost**: the grand style moves an audience and destroys the speaker's ethos if the occasion does not warrant it. That is *decorum*, and it is the best available model of "the right move at the wrong moment fails."

### 1.3 What carries forward from documents 1–2

Unchanged and still load-bearing: **stasis theory** as the ordered question stack; the **burden token** as the thing whose removal turns a hearing into a negotiation; **recorded defeat** (*senatus auctoritas*); the Venetian **standing report** as cumulative intelligence; the *relazione* and the ambassadorial mandate gap. All are re-used below without re-derivation.

## §2 — Modern psychology and behaviour research

### 2.1 Dual-process: which channel works on which mind

The **Elaboration Likelihood Model** (Petty & Cacioppo, formalized 1986) holds that persuasive messages are processed along a continuum from low to high thought, and that two routes result. On the **central route**, the recipient scrutinizes argument quality. On the **peripheral route**, attitude change is driven by cues in the context — source status, attractiveness, message length, apparent consensus. Which route dominates is set by two variables: **motivation** (personal relevance, accountability, need for cognition, mood) and **ability** (cognitive resources, prior knowledge, absence of distraction, comprehensibility) `[T0 — Petty & Cacioppo 1986, author's own PDF; T1 — multiple independent syntheses]`.

Two results matter more than the model itself:

1. **Central-route attitudes are more persistent, more resistant to counter-persuasion, more temporally stable, and more predictive of behaviour** than peripheral-route attitudes `[T1]`. Persuasion has a *durability* dimension that no game in the prior survey modelled at all.
2. **Petty, Cacioppo & Goldman (1981), the exam-policy study**: highly involved participants were swayed by argument strength regardless of the speaker's status; less-involved participants followed the speaker's prestige instead `[T1]`.

That second finding is a complete mechanic on its own. **The same speech, delivered to two audiences, is resolved by different statistics.** Argument quality moves the involved; ethos moves the uninvolved; and reducing an audience's ability — distraction, haste, complexity — is a way of *forcing* the peripheral route. That is a legitimate, historically attested tactic and it is now a rule.

Note the theoretical alignment: ELM's central route is Aristotle's logos; the peripheral route is where ethos and pathos do their work. The re-examination in §1.1 — logos dominates with good procedure and rational judges, ethos and pathos prevail otherwise — is the same claim in fourth-century language `[T2]`.

### 2.2 The compliance levers

Cialdini's principles, six from *Influence* and a seventh added in 2016: **reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, unity** `[T1 — corroborated across six independent sources including Cialdini's own institutional account]`.

The design-relevant detail is Cialdini's own characterization of the seventh: he had always seen unity as **an accelerator of the other principles** — with unity present, scarcity works better, social proof works better `[T2 — Cialdini quoted, W. P. Carey News, 2025]`.

> **Unity is a multiplier, not a sixth addend.** Shared identity scales every other lever. That is a two-tier structure and it should be encoded as one.

These are peripheral-route moves. They work best precisely where elaboration is low, which is why §4 gates them on the ELM state rather than listing them alongside argument moves.

### 2.3 The story model — the replacement for the fabricated matrix

Pennington and Hastie's **explanation-based model of juror decision making** (1986, 1988, 1992, 1993) holds that jurors do not update belief item by item. They construct a **narrative** from three ingredients: the evidence presented, their own world knowledge of similar events, and their expectations about what makes a complete story `[T1 — Sage *Encyclopedia of Psychology and Law*; Pennington & Hastie 1992, *JPSP* 62:189–206]`.

Decision proceeds in three steps: **story construction**, **learning the verdict alternatives**, and **matching the accepted story to the best-fitting alternative** `[T1]`.

Acceptance and confidence are governed by named, computable properties:

- **Coverage** — how much of the evidence the story accounts for. More coverage, more likely accepted, and higher confidence if accepted; less coverage, less likely accepted `[T1]`.
- **Coherence**, itself three things: **consistency** (no internal contradictions, and none with the evidence), **plausibility** (fit with the hearer's world knowledge), and **completeness** (the story has all its structural parts — initiating events, physical state, psychological state, actions, consequences) `[T1 — Pennington & Hastie 1992, 1993]`.
- **Uniqueness** — whether more than one story fits. If several do, confidence falls `[T1]`.

The experimental support is unusually direct for design purposes. Varying the *ease of constructing a story* changed verdicts and changed how much credibility evidence mattered, while memory for the evidence stayed equivalent across conditions — implying the story structure, not recall, mediated the decision `[T1 — Pennington & Hastie 1992, Exp. 1]`. And when subjects gave a global judgment at the end, their process followed the story model rather than Bayesian or linear updating; when forced to judge item-by-item after each evidence block, they behaved like anchor-and-adjust instead `[T1 — same, Exps. 2–3]`.

**The design consequences are large enough to restate plainly:**

1. **The target of a persuasion move is not the opponent's claim. It is the audience's story.** Evidence matters through what it lets a story cover.
2. **Order of presentation is a mechanic, not flavour.** Pennington & Hastie 1988 varied chronological "story order" against witness order; the ease of story construction moved verdicts `[T1]`. *Dispositio* has an experimental basis.
3. **A model that forces item-by-item judgment produces a different decision process.** If your interface makes the audience evaluate each claim as it lands, you have built anchor-and-adjust, not narrative judgment — and you will have modelled the wrong thing.
4. **This replaces the invented warrant/attack matrix.** Coverage, coherence, and uniqueness are sourced, computable, and do the same job — differentiating moves by what they change about the state — without a fabricated payoff table.

A caution the literature itself raises: coherence, completeness, and uniqueness are **not formally defined** by Pennington and Hastie, which makes the model more an account than a predictor, and multiple readings of any given story are possible `[T2 — critique accompanying the ECHO connectionist reimplementation]`. For a game this is a feature — the designer supplies the formal definitions — but it means the numbers are ours, not theirs.

### 2.4 Resistance: reactance and inoculation

Two opposed mechanisms, and a persuasion system needs both or pressure becomes monotonically good.

**Reactance** (Brehm): a perceived threat to behavioural freedom produces motivation to restore it, expressed as movement *away* from the advocated position `[T1 — standard; not re-verified this session, [UNVERIFIED] as to specific formulation]`. Over-pressure is not merely wasted, it is negative.

**Inoculation** (McGuire): pre-exposure to a weakened form of a counter-argument, together with refutation, confers resistance to the full-strength version later. Contemporary work distinguishes **active** inoculation (the recipient generates the counter-arguments themselves) from **passive** (they are supplied), and identifies **post-inoculation talk** as a spreading mechanism. A recent adolescent trial found active inoculation produced significant change across several outcomes where passive produced change on only one `[T1 — experimental study, PMC12412721, 2025]`.

> **Active inoculation is stronger than passive.** Mechanically: pre-empting your opponent's argument by *making the audience generate the objection* is worth more than stating it for them. That is a distinct move from *refutatio* and it is worth its own verb.

### 2.5 Negotiation: anchors, alternatives, and the trade

**First offers anchor.** Across three experiments, whichever party made the first offer obtained the better outcome, and first offers strongly predicted final settlements `[T0 — Galinsky & Mussweiler 2001, *JPSP*]`. A meta-analysis reports a correlation of **.497** between initial offers and final outcomes `[T1 — Orr & Guthrie 2005, via a process-model review]`.

**And the counter is precise.** When the party who did *not* make the first offer focused on information inconsistent with its implications — **the opponent's alternatives** (Exp. 1), **the opponent's reservation price** (Exp. 2), or **one's own target** (Exp. 3) — the first-offer advantage was *eliminated* `[T0 — Galinsky & Mussweiler 2001]`. Framing the negotiation in terms of losses also lessens the anchor's pull `[T2]`.

That is a defensive move with three named variants and an experimentally attested effect size of "eliminated." It is the cleanest single mechanic in this document.

**First-mover disadvantage exists and is specific.** Loschelder, Swaab, Trötschel & Galinsky (2014, 2016) identify cases where opening first backfires: when the offer reveals private information about **compatible** preferences that the recipient can then exploit. They formalize this as the Information-Anchoring Model `[T1]`.

> **The first offer is a trade: it anchors, and it leaks.** Anchoring power against information disclosed. That is a decision, not a dominant strategy.

**Logrolling.** Offers that trade across issues valued differently by each side produce significantly better joint outcomes — and are rated as **less attractive** than distributive first offers `[T1 — Moran & Ritov 2002, *JBDM* 15:101–124]`.

> **The move that creates value looks worse than the move that claims it.** This is a superb mechanic and almost no game has it.

**Outcome and satisfaction diverge.** Negotiators whose first offer was immediately accepted were *less satisfied* even when they achieved better outcomes, explained by counterfactual thought `[T1 — Galinsky et al. 2002]`. Learning and using the extreme-anchor strategy produced worse long-term psychological *and* economic results `[T2 — Maaravi et al.]`.

> Track two numbers: what was won, and how it felt. They are not the same, and a character-driven game should let them diverge.

### 2.6 Deliberation: what an audience does after you stop talking

This is the body of research that makes a jury a *system* rather than a scoreboard.

**Faction size dominates.** A meta-analysis found the likelihood of a faction capturing the verdict rises with each additional member at the start of deliberation `[T1 — Devine et al. 2001]`. First-ballot distribution is the strongest single predictor `[T1 — corroborated by field studies using retrospective first-vote reports]`.

**Two influence channels, asymmetrically available.** Majorities exert both **informational** influence (articulated facts, interpretations, arguments) and **normative** influence (pressure to conform, avoidance of the social cost of deviance). **Minorities have only the informational channel** `[T1]`.

**Snowball and momentum.** Juries move away from their initial distribution slowly at first and then with increasing speed as the majority grows. And once movement begins in either direction it rarely reverses — the key event underlying any reversal is **the first juror who crosses to the minority** `[T1]`.

> Three mechanics fall straight out: a **first-ballot** state, an **acceleration curve**, and a **single high-value target** — the first defector. Persuading one specific person at one specific moment is worth more than persuading anyone else at any other.

**Leniency asymmetry is genuinely contested and should be a parameter, not a constant.** MacCoun & Kerr's meta-analysis of thirteen simulation studies found evenly-split mock juries acquitted roughly four times as often as they convicted `[T1]`. Devine et al. (2004) found no leniency bias in field data — 75% of juries without a two-thirds first-ballot majority convicted `[T1]`. Kerr's later re-analysis argues the field studies mis-coded undecided jurors as pro-acquittal, and after correction finds a leniency asymmetry that is real but weaker than in mock juries `[T1]`. `[CONFLICT: MacCoun & Kerr vs Devine — resolved by recency and by the identified coding bias; carried as a tunable parameter rather than a fixed rule]`

## §3 — What games already model

### 3.1 Griftlands (Klei, 2019–2021) — the most developed negotiation system in a videogame, and a warning

Klei built negotiation as a **second deckbuilder**, structurally parallel to combat but with its own deck and vocabulary `[T2 — PC Gamer; Game Informer; T1-equivalent for mechanics: the game's own wiki]`:

- **Core argument** with **resolve** — the health analogue. Reduce the opponent's core resolve to zero to win. The player's resolve **carries between negotiations** and must be restored by rest, drink, or victory.
- **Composure** — the defence analogue, but **entirely lost at the end of each round**. Temporary shielding, not a durable stat.
- **Arguments** — persistent entities on the board with their own resolve, playable by either side, attackable and defendable like the core. Excess damage on a destroyed argument spills to the core.
- **Influence** (green) maximizes the damage range of influential attacks; **dominance** (red) raises the range of hostile attacks by one per stack and **decays one per turn**.
- **Interrogation / Planted Evidence** — a worked example of the system's depth: an action creates a self-inflicted vulnerability that the opponent's Interrogation argument attacks; successfully defending it leaves the opponent Flustered and weakens their attacks `[T2 — PC Gamer]`.

**The designer's account of what they tried first is the most important sentence in this entire survey.** Klei's Kevin Forbes: their original conversation challenges were based on *spending resources to influence random rolls*, and in execution it was "a lot less fun than fighting." Cards fixed it, because cards present a limited, randomized subset of the player's total available actions, forcing hedging and improvisation `[T2 — Uppercut, developer interview]`.

> **This is a direct hit on documents 1–3.** M5/M6/M7 — play a claim, choose an attack type, roll modified by standing — *is* spending resources to influence random rolls. It is the design Klei built, played, and abandoned as unfun before shipping. §7 treats this as the audit's leading finding.

A documented failure mode too: an encounter whose core argument was invulnerable with no resolve, resolvable only by one specific card, produced eleven turns of a player with nothing to target and nothing to do `[T2 — Steam discussion]`. Persistent-argument boards can deadlock when the win condition is not reachable by the available verbs.

### 3.2 Dogs in the Vineyard (Baker, 2004) — escalation as the core idea

One unified resolution system covers **talking, physical, fighting, and gunfighting**, with a different pair of stats rolled in each arena `[T2 — RPGnet review; Wikipedia]`. The structure:

- **Stakes are set first**, in terms of the ultimate goal, never the method. The method emerges from play `[T2]`.
- **Raise and See.** The active side puts forward dice with a description of what they are doing. The receiving side must match. Matching with **two dice is a block**; needing **three or more is taking the blow**, which generates **Fallout** later `[T2]`.
- **Traits and relationships add dice** when the player can argue they are relevant `[T2]`.
- **Escalation.** The losing side may always change arena rather than accept the outcome — and escalating brings in a **new dice pool**. The order talking → physical → fighting → gunfighting is usual but not required; de-escalation is legal and rare `[T2]`.
- **Give.** Concede at any time; the stakes go to the other side.

> **Escalation is the answer to "why not just walk away."** A losing negotiation does not end; it becomes a different kind of conflict, with different resources and different consequences. Fallout is scaled to the arena — a fistfight sends you to a doctor, only a gunfight kills.

**Its documented flaw is instructive.** Play analysis concludes that when you are losing you should Give immediately, and that the system's presentation does not lean into this — leaving a rational strategy the rules do not acknowledge `[T2 — Adept Play session report]`. A concede option must be *priced*, not merely available.

### 3.3 Reacting to the Past (Carnes, Barnard, from the late 1990s) — the audience as players

An academic role-immersion pedagogy in which students take historical roles and must persuade each other, with outcomes decided by votes rather than a script `[T1 — Barnard's own programme description; adopted at 250+ institutions]`. The structural device that matters:

- Students are divided into **factions** with shared victory objectives, **and indeterminates**.
- **The indeterminates' victory objectives are "incomplete, imprecise, or undisclosed"** `[T1 — RTTP Pedagogy Manual]`. They must act plausibly within an assigned persona while expressing some measure of their own judgment.
- Indeterminates cannot win alone; they must ally with factions, and factions court them with deals and alliances `[T1 — AAS account]`.
- In one published game the *commissioners* — the deciding body — are the indeterminates, and some characters can meet their objectives through secret deals, provided journalists do not expose them `[T2 — Schiffman, on the FCC game]`.

> **The audience is not a meter. It is a set of agents with hidden, partial aims, who must be courted individually.** This is the single most transferable idea for persuasion before a body, and it is the exact opposite of a persuasion bar.

### 3.4 Carried forward from the earlier survey

- **Burning Wheel, *Duel of Wits*** — declared stakes before mechanics; simultaneous secret scripting; compromise scaled to what victory cost; rules that bind *public performance*, not belief. Its collapse to two dominant manoeuvres remains the standing warning `[T0/T2]`.
- **Ace Attorney / Danganronpa** — evidence as inventory; contradiction as a puzzle with one solution. Good tension, no political modelling `[T2]`.
- **Diplomacy** — negotiation with no enforcement whatever `[T1]`.
- **Disco Elysium** — internal voices as interlocutors with agendas `[T2]`.

### 3.5 The honest null

Nothing in this survey models **durability of persuasion**. Every system resolves an encounter and moves on. ELM's central finding — that attitudes formed through scrutiny persist and resist counter-persuasion while attitudes formed through cues decay — is absent from every game examined across all four documents. `[NULL: durability of attitude change across the surveyed corpus — examined Griftlands, Burning Wheel, Dogs in the Vineyard, Ace Attorney, Suzerain, Victoria 3, Crusader Kings III, Reacting to the Past; found none]`

That is the open space. It is also a risk: an empty space in a well-explored field is sometimes empty because it is not fun.

## §4 — Primitives

Eleven new mechanisms, numbered N-series, each attached to the M1–M14 vocabulary from document 3. Nothing here creates a fourth parallel catalogue.

**N1 · Mind** — *extends the M-substrate*
The unit of persuasion. Not a meter. Each mind holds: an **aim** (possibly hidden, possibly partial), a **story** (N2), an **elaboration state** (N3), **lever susceptibilities** (N4), a **pressure tolerance** (N5), and a **faction membership or indeterminacy** (N9).
*From:* RTTP indeterminates `[T1]`; ELM individual differences `[T1]`.
*Fails when:* an audience is one number. Then persuasion is a bar to fill and no move is more interesting than any other.

**N2 · Story** — *replaces M6's warrant/attack matrix as the resolution object*
Each mind holds one or more candidate narratives. Each has:
- `coverage` — fraction of presented evidence the story accounts for
- `consistency` — contradictions with evidence or within itself
- `plausibility` — fit with this mind's world knowledge (per-mind, not global)
- `completeness` — presence of the five structural slots: initiating events, physical state, psychological state, actions, consequences
- `uniqueness` — how many candidate stories fit comparably
Acceptance is a function of coverage and coherence; **confidence** is a function of uniqueness.
*From:* Pennington & Hastie `[T1]`.
*Why it replaces the matrix:* it differentiates moves by what they change about the state, which was the requirement, and it is sourced rather than invented.
*Fails when:* the five slots are decorative. A story missing its psychological slot must be *visibly* weaker or completeness does nothing.

**N3 · Route** — *gates every appeal*
Per mind: `elaboration = f(motivation, ability)`. Motivation rises with personal relevance, accountability, and disposition; ability falls with distraction, haste, complexity, and fatigue.
- High elaboration → **argument quality** decides, source status does not.
- Low elaboration → **peripheral cues** decide, argument quality does not.
- Change made at high elaboration is **durable and resistant**; change made at low elaboration **decays**.
*From:* Petty & Cacioppo `[T0]`; the exam-policy study `[T1]`.
*The tactic this creates:* **forcing the route.** Rushing a body, overwhelming it with technicality, or scheduling the vote late are all moves that push minds to the peripheral route, where ethos wins. That is a real historical practice and now a legal one.

**N4 · Appeal** — *the move channels*
Three, with the levers nested under two of them:
- **Logos** — enthymeme from probabilities and signs; operates on story coverage and consistency. Effective only at high elaboration.
- **Ethos** — speaker credibility; carries **authority**, **liking**, **commitment/consistency**. Effective mainly at low elaboration.
- **Pathos** — hearer's emotional state; carries **reciprocity**, **social proof**, **scarcity**. Effective mainly at low elaboration.
- **Unity** is not a lever. It is a **multiplier on the other six**, per Cialdini's own account.
*From:* Aristotle `[T1]`; Cialdini `[T1/T2]`; gated by ELM `[T0]`.

**N5 · Resistance** — *reactance and inoculation, one mechanism*
Each mind has a `pressure tolerance`. Persuasive force exceeding it produces **reactance**: the mind moves *away* from the advocated position, and the mover loses standing with it.
Two pre-emptive moves reduce a mind's susceptibility to a *later* appeal:
- **Passive inoculation** — state the opponent's argument in weakened form and refute it. Cheap, modest effect.
- **Active inoculation** — put a question to the audience that makes it generate the objection itself. Costlier, larger effect, and it spreads through the body between sessions.
*From:* Brehm `[T1, UNVERIFIED as to formulation]`; McGuire and the active/passive distinction `[T1]`.
*Why both:* without reactance, pressure is monotonically good and the optimal play is maximum force every turn.

**N6 · Anchor** — *extends M13 Settlement*
The first offer sets a reference point that pulls the counteroffer and the settlement toward it.
**Three named counters, each of which eliminates the advantage:** focus on the opponent's alternatives; focus on the opponent's reservation value; focus on your own target. Framing in losses also weakens the pull.
**The cost of anchoring:** an extreme first offer on an issue where preferences are secretly *compatible* leaks that information and can be exploited.
*From:* Galinsky & Mussweiler `[T0]`; Loschelder et al. `[T1]`.

**N7 · Logroll** — *extends M13 Settlement*
A multi-issue offer trading across issues the two sides value differently. Produces better joint outcomes and is **rated less attractive** than a comparable distributive offer.
*From:* Moran & Ritov `[T1]`.
*The mechanic:* the counterpart's *acceptance probability* and the *joint value* move in opposite directions. The player must decide whether to be liked or to be right.

**N8 · Arena and Escalation** — *new; connects persuasion to the rest of the game*
Arenas: **Talk** → **Status** (shame, precedence, exposure) → **Coercion** (threat, seizure, bond forfeiture) → **Force**.
The losing side may **escalate** rather than accept: a new arena brings a new resource pool, a new resolution statistic, and a different **fallout** class. De-escalation is legal and rare. **Concession is always available and always priced** — the surrendered stakes plus a standing cost.
*From:* Dogs in the Vineyard `[T2]`, with the concede-pricing added to answer its documented flaw.

**N9 · Body** — *factions and indeterminates*
An audience is a set of N1 Minds partitioned into **factions** (shared aims, coordinated) and **indeterminates** (aims incomplete, imprecise, or undisclosed; must be courted individually; cannot win alone).
*From:* RTTP `[T1]`.
*Design instruction:* indeterminates are the content. Their partial aims are what the player investigates, and investigating them is M4 Probe pointed at a person instead of a fact.

**N10 · Deliberation** — *what the body does after the speaking stops*
1. **First ballot.** Record the distribution. It is the strongest predictor of the outcome.
2. **Influence.** Majority factions apply both informational and normative pressure; **minorities may apply informational only**.
3. **Acceleration.** Movement is slow initially and speeds up as the majority grows.
4. **Momentum.** Once movement starts in a direction it rarely reverses. The one event that can reverse it is **the first crossing to the minority**.
5. **Asymmetry.** A tunable parameter favouring one verdict class over another, defaulting weak rather than absent.
*From:* Devine et al.; MacCoun & Kerr; Kerr's re-analysis `[T1, with the conflict logged in §2.6]`.
*The mechanic this creates:* **the highest-value persuasion target in the entire design is one specific person at one specific moment** — a mind close enough to the line that flipping them starts a reversal. Everything else is worth less.

**N11 · Arrangement and Register** — *the two canons that are actually mechanical*
- **Arrangement.** The speech has six slots — *exordium*, *narratio*, *partitio*, *confirmatio*, *refutatio*, *peroratio* — each operating on a different target: disposition, story, framing, coverage, opponent coverage, emotion. A speech is a **choice of which slots to spend on**, under a finite budget.
- **Register.** Plain (instruct), middle (win over), grand (move). Register scales the effect and is checked against **decorum**: the wrong register for the occasion succeeds at nothing and costs ethos.
- The *peroratio* has four distinct closing operations (Rhet. III.19): turn them toward you, turn them from the opponent, amplify or minimize, and remind.
*From:* Cicero and Quintilian `[T1]`; Aristotle `[T2]`; story-order effects `[T1]`.

## §5 — Three systems

All three run on the same substrate. They differ in who decides and whether the deciders talk to each other afterwards.

### 5.1 The Table — negotiation with a counterpart

```
Approach -> Mandate -> [Anchor?] -> Exchange -> {Overlap | Impasse} -> Instrument -> Record
                                       |            |
                                  [Logroll]    [Escalate arena]
```

| Phase | Moves | Governing primitive |
|---|---|---|
| **Mandate** | Set reservation; identify your alternative as a *concrete thing*, not a number | M13, N6 |
| **Anchor** | Open first (pulls the settlement, leaks compatible preferences) or hold | **N6** |
| **Counter-anchor** | Name their alternative · name their reservation · state your own target — each eliminates the pull | **N6** |
| **Exchange** | Distributive offer (attractive, low joint value) or **logroll** (unattractive, high joint value) | **N7** |
| **Pressure** | Push past their tolerance → reactance, they move away and you lose standing | **N5** |
| **Impasse** | Cannot be broken at the table. Change an alternative elsewhere — or **escalate the arena** | M13, **N8** |
| **Close** | Terms scaled to what winning cost; binding only if a bond is lodged | M13 |
| **After** | Record two values: **outcome** and **satisfaction**. Immediate acceptance lowers the second | **N11**, §2.5 |

**What this argues:** that the way to win a negotiation is to have already done something else. Alternatives are built off-table; the table only prices them.

### 5.2 The Floor — persuasion before a body

Runs inside M8 Floor. The body is an N9 set of factions and indeterminates; the player spends a finite number of speech slots.

```
Survey the body -> Build the speech -> Deliver -> Read the room -> Deliberation
     (M4 Probe)     (N11 slots)      (N4 gated   (updated N2      (N10)
                                      by N3)      per mind)
```

**Turn structure.**

1. **Survey.** M4 Probe pointed at people: reveal one attribute of one mind — its aim, its elaboration state, its lever susceptibility, or which faction it truly answers to. Indeterminates are the highest-value targets because their aims are the ones authored as partial.
2. **Build.** Allocate a finite budget across the six *dispositio* slots and choose a register. This is the speech's shape and it is a real decision: spending on *narratio* raises story coverage for everyone; spending on *exordium* raises disposition only.
3. **Deliver.** Each slot resolves per mind, **gated by that mind's N3 route**. The same *confirmatio* that convinces the involved does nothing to the distracted; the same *exordium* that carries the distracted is noise to the involved.
4. **Read.** Minds update their N2 stories. Reactance fires where pressure exceeded tolerance.
5. **Deliberate.** N10 runs. The body persuades itself, and the player watches a distribution move that they can no longer touch.

**Two tactics the research licenses that the design should make explicit:**

- **Forcing the route.** Scheduling the question late, burying it in technicality, or creating haste pushes minds peripheral, where ethos and the levers win and argument quality does not. Legitimate, historical, and now a rule.
- **Active inoculation.** Ask the body a question that makes it generate your opponent's objection for itself, one session before your opponent raises it. Costs a slot now, blunts their whole speech later.

### 5.3 The Verdict — persuasion before a jury

The Floor plus two additions: the deciders must reach a verdict, and their conclusion is a **story**, not a tally.

```
Voir dire -> Openings -> Evidence -> Closings -> First ballot -> Deliberation -> Verdict
 (shape N9)  (N2 seed)  (coverage)  (N11 pero)   (N10 step 1)    (N10 2-5)
```

| Stage | What is actually happening | Primitive |
|---|---|---|
| **Voir dire** | Shaping the composition of the deciding body before any argument | M14, N9 |
| **Opening** | Seeding a story. Whoever narrates first sets the frame the evidence must fit | **N2** |
| **Evidence** | Each item raises coverage for one story and may create inconsistency in another. Atechnic proofs — witnesses, documents, oaths — are **inventory**, spent and lost | **N2**, Aristotle's atechnic list |
| **Story order** | Presenting chronologically rather than by witness makes construction easier and moves verdicts | **N2**, `[T1]` |
| **Closing** | The *peroratio*'s four operations, plus a last push on uniqueness — leave them with only one story that fits | **N11** |
| **First ballot** | The distribution is now the strongest predictor of the outcome | **N10** |
| **Deliberation** | Faction size dominates; the minority holds only the informational channel; movement accelerates; momentum rarely reverses | **N10** |

**The design payoff.** Because momentum reverses only at the first crossing, and because minorities can use only informational influence, the player's craft is: (a) get one specific mind close enough to the line before the ballot, and (b) arm that mind with an *argument* rather than a feeling, because normative pressure is unavailable to it.

**That is a targeted, legible, sourced win condition** and it is far better than reducing a persuasion bar to zero.

## §6 — Encoding

```
Mind {
  id, factionId?                       // null => indeterminate
  aim          : Aim                   // partial or undisclosed for indeterminates
  disclosure   : FULL | PARTIAL | HIDDEN
  motivation   : 0..1                  // relevance, accountability, disposition
  ability      : 0..1                  // resources, knowledge, absence of distraction
  elaboration  : derived = f(motivation, ability)
  tolerance    : 0..1                  // pressure above this triggers reactance
  levers       : Map<LEVER, 0..1>      // per-mind susceptibility
  unity        : Map<ActorId, 0..1>    // multiplier on all levers
  inoculated   : Map<ArgumentId, {strength, source: ACTIVE|PASSIVE}>
  stories      : [Story]
  ballot?      : VerdictId
}

Story {
  claimSet     : [ClaimId]
  slots        : {initiating, physical, psychological, actions, consequences}  // each filled | empty
  coverage     : evidenceExplained / evidencePresented
  consistency  : 1 - (contradictions / claimSet.size)
  plausibility : per-mind fit with worldKnowledge          // NOT global
  completeness : filledSlots / 5
  coherence    : g(consistency, plausibility, completeness)
  acceptance   : h(coverage, coherence)
  confidence   : k(uniqueness)                             // falls as rival stories fit
}

Speech {
  budget       : int                                       // finite slots
  parts        : Map<EXORDIUM|NARRATIO|PARTITIO|CONFIRMATIO|REFUTATIO|PERORATIO, int>
  register     : PLAIN | MIDDLE | GRAND
  decorumCheck : register vs occasion -> ok | costsEthos
}

Offer {
  issues       : [{issueId, value, myWeight, theirWeightEstimate}]
  kind         : DISTRIBUTIVE | LOGROLL
  isFirst      : bool
  leaks        : [issueId]                                 // compatible prefs revealed
}

Arena  = TALK | STATUS | COERCION | FORCE
Fallout: Map<Arena, SanctionClass>                          // scaled to arena, per DitV
```

**Resolution sketches.**

```
deliver(speech, body):
  for each mind in body:
     route = mind.elaboration >= threshold ? CENTRAL : PERIPHERAL
     for each part in speech.parts:
        effect = part.channel matches route ? full : negligible
        effect *= registerScale(speech.register) * decorum
        if part.channel in {ETHOS, PATHOS}:
           effect *= mind.levers[lever] * mind.unity[speaker]
        if effect > mind.tolerance:
           REACTANCE: mind moves away; speaker loses standing with mind
        else:
           update mind.stories                              // coverage, consistency, slots
     mind.changeDurability = route == CENTRAL ? PERSISTENT : DECAYING
```

```
counterAnchor(offer, mode):                                 // mode in {THEIR_ALT, THEIR_RESERVATION, MY_TARGET}
  anchorPull = 0                                            // eliminated, per Galinsky & Mussweiler
  cost = 1 segment
```

```
deliberate(body):
  ballot = firstBallot(body)
  loop:
    for each faction f:
       informational = f.arguments                          // available to all
       normative     = f.size >= majority ? f.size : 0      // MAJORITY ONLY
    pressure = accelerate(pressure, majorityFaction.size)   // snowball
    if aMindCrosses(): momentum.lock(direction)             // rarely reverses after
    if firstCrossingIsToMinority(): momentum.reverse()      // the one reversal event
  return verdict
```

**Two encoding rules.**

1. **Never let the audience judge item-by-item.** If the interface forces per-claim evaluation, the underlying process becomes anchor-and-adjust rather than narrative construction — a result Pennington & Hastie demonstrated experimentally `[T1]`. Present evidence; resolve stories at closing.
2. **`plausibility` is per-mind and reads that mind's world knowledge.** A global plausibility value collapses N1 back into a meter and discards the whole point.

## §7 — Adversarial audit

`[SELF-AUTHORED — bias risk]` Same process built §§1–6 and attacks them here.

**Verdict: the research is the strongest gathered in this session and the systems built on it are still the wrong kind of object. §§1–3 found a sourced replacement for the corpus's one fabricated mechanic and a direct refutation of its core resolution model. §§4–6 acted on the first and not the second.**

### Severe

**A1 — Klei built the prior design, played it, and threw it away. This document names that and does not fix it.**
Forbes's account is unambiguous: conversation challenges based on *spending resources to influence random rolls* were "a lot less fun than fighting" `[T2]`. That is M5/M6/M7 exactly, and §6's `deliver()` — allocate budget across parts, multiply by register, decorum, lever, and unity, resolve — is the same shape wearing better sources. The fix Klei found was **structural, not numerical**: cards present a limited, randomized subset of the player's total available actions, forcing hedging and improvisation. This document adopts none of that. **Naming the refutation is not answering it.**

**A2 — The fabricated matrix has been replaced by a sourced but undefined function.**
§2.3 is a real improvement in provenance: coverage, coherence, and uniqueness are Pennington & Hastie's, not mine. But the literature itself says these terms are **not formally defined**, which is why the model is charged with being an account rather than a predictor `[T2]`. §6 supplies `g()`, `h()`, and `k()` as placeholders. The invention has therefore moved from a visible table into three function bodies where it is harder to see. That is not obviously progress and it should not be reported as closure.

**A3 — The authoring cost is uncosted and may be prohibitive.**
`plausibility` is per-mind and reads that mind's world knowledge — which means every mind needs authored world knowledge, and every story-evidence pair needs a fit value against it. A twelve-person jury with two candidate stories is 24 story objects, 120 slot states, and a plausibility judgment per evidence item per mind. The code is trivial; the *content* is not, and no document in this corpus has ever costed content.

### High

**A4 — The historical sources contributed almost nothing, and the instruction was therefore only half satisfiable.**
The brief said to use the same sources. Venice, Sengoku, the Carolingians, and imperial China appear nowhere in §4. This is a genuine finding rather than an omission: **those sources are about institutions, not minds.** They tell you who may speak and in what order; they are silent on what happens inside the hearer. Only Aristotle, Cicero, and Quintilian carried across, and they carried across because they were already psychology. Worth stating plainly rather than papering over with a decorative citation.

**A5 — The entire modern layer is laboratory work, and its own literature shows it not generalizing.**
ELM's canonical demonstration is an undergraduate exam-policy study. The story model is mock juries. Anchoring is negotiation exercises. And §2.6 records the visible symptom: MacCoun & Kerr's meta-analysis found evenly-split mock juries acquitting four times as often, while Devine's field data found no such bias at all `[T1]`. If these findings do not reliably survive contact with real juries, the confidence they lend a game design is smaller than the tier tags suggest. `[T1]` marks provenance, not transferability.

**A6 — N3 creates a dominant strategy while trying to prevent one.**
If reducing an audience's ability reliably pushes it to the peripheral route, and the peripheral route is decided by ethos and levers, then any player with high standing should *always* rush, complicate, and fatigue the body. "Make them stupid, then charm them" is a single move that wins every Floor encounter. §5.2 presents this as a licensed tactic; it is a hole. The counter has to be that peripheral-route change **decays**, which §4 states and §5 never uses.

**A7 — Single-player load falls on the counterpart AI at exactly the hardest point.**
Counter-anchoring automates trivially. **Logrolling does not**: to construct a package that trades across differently-weighted issues, the AI must model the player's issue weights from their behaviour. That is the hardest inference problem anywhere in four documents and it is unmentioned.

### Medium

**A8 — Escalation is borrowed with its flaw only half-repaired.** Document 3's fix was to price concession, which §4 does. But **escalation itself is unpriced**: if changing arena always grants a new resource pool, escalating is free power and the optimal play is to escalate the moment you are behind. Dogs in the Vineyard controls this through fallout severity; §4 gestures at fallout classes and specifies none.

**A9 — N3 and N5 may be the same field twice.** Elaboration is "how much scrutiny this mind applies"; tolerance is "how much pressure it accepts before recoiling." Both are receptivity. They may be genuinely orthogonal — a highly engaged mind can be easily offended — but the document asserts orthogonality without arguing it.

**A10 — Nothing here handles deception.** The persuasion literature assumes sincere advocacy. A Renaissance political game is substantially about lying. A *false* story with high coverage and high coherence is the most dangerous object in the design, and N2 has no `truth` field, no detection rule, and no consequence for exposure. Given that the corpus's own historical research is full of sealed denunciations and withheld evidence, this is a strange gap.

**A11 — Register and decorum are asserted with no measured basis.** The three styles are well attested as a *doctrine*. No source consulted gives an effect size, a failure condition, or evidence that mismatched register costs credibility. It is plausible and it is unevidenced, and §4 states it as flatly as it states the Galinsky result.

### What the attack failed to break

- **N6 counter-anchoring.** Attacked as too tidy. It holds: three named manipulations, three experiments, an effect reported as *eliminated*, each implementable as one action `[T0 — Galinsky & Mussweiler 2001]`. The best-sourced discrete mechanic in four documents.
- **N10's first-defector target.** Attacked as over-reading a summary. It holds: the deliberation literature states directly that movement rarely reverses and that the key event underlying reversal is the first juror crossing to the minority `[T1]`. It converts a bar into a person.
- **N7's inverted attractiveness.** Attacked as a curiosity. It holds: logrolling offers produce better joint outcomes and are rated less attractive `[T1 — Moran & Ritov]`. A mechanic where the good move looks bad is rare and valuable.
- **The atechnic/entechnic split.** Attacked as pedantry. It holds: separating evidence-as-inventory from appeals-as-actions is a real structural distinction that documents 1–3 missed entirely, and it is Aristotle's own `[T1]`.
- **N9 indeterminates.** Attacked as content-expensive — correctly, see A3 — but it survives on the merits: across four surveys it is the **only** alternative to a persuasion meter that anyone has found, and it comes from a system that has run at 250+ institutions.

### What an independent reviewer would add

1. **Four documents, nothing played.** The last audit's highest-value recommendation was a paper prototype. It was not done, and this document is the fourth in a row to end with the same advice.
2. **The player never speaks.** Every "persuasion" move here is a menu selection. The research is entirely about what *words* do, and words are the one thing a player of this game will not produce. Whether the translation from utterance to menu preserves anything is the central design question and is nowhere addressed.
3. **A1 points at a specific answer that keeps not getting built.** Klei's fix was cards. The evidence for cards is the strongest empirical evidence in this survey — a studio built the alternative, shipped the card version, and said why. Four documents have now declined to follow the one piece of direct playtest evidence available.

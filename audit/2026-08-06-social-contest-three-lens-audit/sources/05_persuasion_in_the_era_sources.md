# Persuasion in the Era Sources
## Fifth document. Correcting finding A4, and the primitives that follow from doing so.

**The correction.** Document 4's finding A4 said the historical sources contributed almost nothing to persuasion because "those sources are about institutions, not minds." That was wrong, and it was wrong in a specific and diagnosable way: **I searched the European rhetorical tradition, found it audience-facing, and concluded the era had nothing to say about the individual counterpart.** In fact the era has an entire counterpart-facing tradition — it is simply Chinese, and I did not look.

Seven new searches. Six findings, of which two are systems-grade and one supplies a mechanic that nothing in four prior documents contained.

## §1 — Era research

### 1.1 Guiguzi 鬼谷子 — the counterpart-facing tradition, and the answer to A4

The *Guiguzi*, compiled between the late Warring States and the end of the Han, some 6,000–7,000 characters, ascribed to a master active near the fourth century BC and traditionally the teacher of the two great persuaders Su Qin 蘇秦 and Zhang Yi 張儀. Associated with the School of Diplomacy (*zonghengjia*), later absorbed into the Daoist canon. `[T1 — chinaknowledge.de; Chinese Text Project; SIU Press critical translation (Hui Wu, *"Guiguzi," China's First Treatise on Rhetoric*)]`

The comparative point, made directly by the scholarship, is the one that matters:

> European rhetoric, originating in Greece, is mainly concerned with persuading **large audiences in public forums** — the assembly, the courtroom. The Guiguzi's strategies focus on **involvement with an individual counterpart**. Listening to and assessing the particular type of opponent, and then taking advantage of his individual preferences, is what decides success. The Guiguzi does not teach how to compose a perfect piece of rhetorical art conforming to a commonly shared system of persuasive logic. `[T1 — Gentz, "Rhetoric as the Art of Listening: Concepts of Persuasion in the First Eleven Chapters of the Guiguzi," *Asiatische Studien* 68.4 (2014), 1001–1019]`

**That is exactly the gap document 4 declared unfillable from the era sources.** Aristotle, Cicero and Quintilian are audience-facing because their institutions were. The Guiguzi is counterpart-facing because its institution was the ruler's private chamber.

**The chapter titles are a named-technique catalogue** — which is to say, a primitives list written in the fourth century BC `[T1 — SIU Press table of contents, Hui Wu translation]`:

| | Chinese | Rendered | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 捭闔 Bai He | Open-Shut | Alternating disclosure and closure; when to draw out and when to seal |
| 2 | 反應 Fan Ying | Reflect-Respond | Say something to provoke a reply, and read the reply |
| 3 | 內揵 Nei Jian | Affect-Fortify | Fastening yourself to the counterpart's inner disposition |
| 4 | 抵巇 Di Xi | Mend-Break | Finding the crack — and choosing to seal it or widen it |
| 5 | 飛箝 Fei Qian | Captivate-Capture | "Flying and pinching": praise that raises, then a grip that holds |
| 6 | 忤合 Wu He | Resist-Reconcile | Opposing in order to align |
| 7 | 揣 Chuai | Weighing | Estimating the counterpart's real situation |
| 8 | 摩 Mo | Gauging | Testing his reaction covertly |
| 9 | 權 Quan | Assessing | Judging the balance of forces |
| 10 | 謀 Mou | Deploying | Planning the approach |
| 11 | 決 Jue | Decision-Making | Committing |

Two doctrinal points from the text itself, both directly mechanical:

- **Keep yourself hidden and silent so the counterpart cannot see what you are doing** `[T2 — Chinese Text Project summary]`.
- **Ideally the counterpart should be convinced that his decision was made entirely by himself** `[T2 — same]`.

That second line is a *win condition*, and it is a better one than reducing a resolve bar to zero. It also predicts, in the fourth century BC, the modern findings on reactance and on the durability of self-generated attitude change.

Note the internal ordering: chapters 7–9 (*Chuai*, *Mo*, *Quan*) are all assessment, and they sit *before* 10–11 (deploy, decide). **Reading precedes speaking, and reading is most of the work.** Gentz's title — rhetoric as the art of *listening* — is the whole thesis.

### 1.2 Han Feizi, "The Difficulties of Persuasion" (說難 *Shui Nan*)

One of the most important of the fifty-five chapters of the *Han Feizi* (Han Fei, c. 280–233 BC), and a treatise on the psychology of persuading a single powerful person `[T1 — standard; T2 for the detailed summary, Baidu encyclopedia rendering]`.

Its opening principle: the difficulty lies not in the sufficiency of one's knowledge, nor in one's eloquence, nor in daring to speak — **but in knowing the mind of the one being persuaded, so that one's argument can be matched to it** `[T2]`.

Three requirements follow: study the ruler's adverse psychological reactions to advocacy; accommodate his affections, aversions, and degrees of favour; and above all —

> **Never stroke the reverse scale.** (逆鱗 *nilin* — the dragon's single inverted scale. Touch it and it kills you.)

Two dangers named in the text are counter-intuitive and excellent design material `[T2]`:

- Danger when the persuasion touches on **a matter the ruler secretly harbours in his heart**.
- Danger when the persuader is seen to have **divined the ruler's true intentions** behind his stated ones.

**Being seen to understand your target is itself lethal.** That is the exact inverse of every persuasion system in the surveyed corpus, where knowing more is monotonically good. Here, knowledge must be *acted on without being displayed*, which is a real and playable constraint.

Han Fei's own end is the case study: he was sent to prison at the direction of Li Si and forced to take poison. The author of the treatise on persuading rulers failed to persuade one `[T1]`.

### 1.3 Rome — the jury as a political prize, and character as evidence

The *quaestiones perpetuae* were standing jury courts, the first established by the *lex Calpurnia de repetundis* in 149 BC for provincial extortion, later joined by courts for *maiestas*, *ambitus*, *peculatus*, *falsum*, *de sicariis et veneficiis*, and *vis*. Unlike trials before a popular assembly, **the courts were always open and any citizen could bring a charge** `[T1 — Wikipedia, *Quaestio perpetua*, citing Vervaet; corroborated by Nótári and the Dickinson commentary]`.

Jurors (*iudices*) were drawn from an *album* compiled annually by the urban praetor, most often by amending his predecessor's list `[T2 — Nótári]`. And the composition of that list was **an extremely sensitive political question for some fifty years** after Gaius Gracchus's changes of 122 BC: Sulla reversed the Gracchan use of equestrian jurors; the *lex Aurelia iudiciaria* of 70 BC then split the panel into thirds — senators, *equites*, and *tribuni aerarii*, 300 of each, 900 in all — leaving the substantive law untouched and changing only who sat `[T1 — *Lex Aurelia iudiciaria*; corroborated by the Verres commentary]`.

> **The composition of the deciding body was contested for half a century while the law itself stayed the same.** That is the strongest possible statement of a principle document 3 encoded as `Body.selection` and document 4 mentioned in one line under *voir dire*: **who sits is a larger contest than what is said, and it is fought in a different arena and on a different timescale.**

On advocacy itself, two facts that reframe the jury system:

- The advocate was a *patronus*. A case was taken to discharge an obligation or to create one — **never out of charity or public duty** `[T2 — survey of the defence speeches]`.
- **Roman trials put less weight on evidence and much more on the character of the people involved.** A legal speech often spent more time assassinating the character of the accused or the accuser than querying the facts — and this extended to the character of the advocate himself `[T2 — same. Partisan-flavoured secondary source; the underlying claim is standard in the scholarship but the phrasing is not authoritative. [TIER-FLOOR: T2]]`

### 1.4 The Carolingian and post-Carolingian world — supplication as a binding performance

Koziol's *Begging Pardon and Favor: Ritual and Political Order in Early Medieval France* (Cornell, 1992) reconstructs supplication — entreaty in a distinctive language, accompanied by gestures of humility, kneeling and prostration — as the commonest political ritual of the tenth and eleventh centuries `[T1 — Koziol 1992; reviewed in *AHR* and *JEH*]`.

His central argument, as the *American Historical Review* summarized it: **political order in both the secular and ecclesiastical spheres depended closely on ritual — kings governed, antagonists were reconciled, and monks had property rights confirmed, through grand gestures of humble supplication** `[T1]`.

Two refinements that matter mechanically:

- Supplication rituals were **not routinized gestures insensitive to context**; they remained meaningful by adapting to different political communities, and they varied by region `[T1]`.
- Over the tenth to twelfth centuries they shifted **from emphasizing hierarchical distance toward preserving honour and expressing equality** between the parties `[T2 — summary of Koziol p. 307]`.

> **Public self-abasement is a move that binds the person abased to.** A properly performed supplication is expensive for the supplicant and *more* expensive to refuse, because refusal in public damages the superior's claim to rule by grace. That is an offer the recipient cannot decline without paying — and nothing in four documents contained one.

A recorded criticism, logged rather than hidden: Koziol's focus on northern France may limit how far the findings generalize `[T2]`.

### 1.5 Sengoku Japan — the self-cursing oath, and its inflation

The *kishōmon* 起請文 is a written oath in two parts: **a statement (A), and an anathema clause (B)** in which the swearer curses himself, by named deities, if what he says is untrue. The form is attested from the twelfth century at the latest; earlier documents in the same shape begin "I seek heavenly judgment" `[T1 — *Medieval Worlds*, "Before Written Oaths (kishōmon)"; Chijiwa; Satō]`.

The physical object is as interesting as the text:

- Oaths were written on **talismanic paper** — *gōō hōin*, bearing the treasured seal of the Kumano or Nachi shrines, and from the fifteenth century elaborate Ox-head talismans composed of anywhere from 42 to 72 crow figures, the crows being messengers of the deities. These printed talismans came to be understood as an essential element of the oath, and some older plain documents were **retrospectively stamped** `[T1 — "The Gods Are Watching: Talismans, Oaths, and Political Allegiance in Medieval Japan"]`.
- Oaths were **split in half**, one part kept by each party `[T1 — the five Princeton *kishōmon* from the Saji family archive, Kōga district, Ōmi]`.
- Kōga's local warriors were organized in **collectives (*sō*) that served as mediators in local disputes**, and the surviving oaths document exactly that mediation `[T1 — same]`.

And the finding that turns this into a mechanic rather than a prop:

> Ogino posits that the **increasing use of signatures written in human blood** during the Warring States period indicates **decreasing trust in oaths** `[T2 — Ogino, cited in the Princeton article]`.

**The credibility of a costly signal inflates.** As oaths are broken, the form escalates — plainer paper to talisman, seal to blood — and the escalation is itself a public reading of how little the oath is now worth. A game that lets the player observe the *form* of an oath and infer the *state of trust in the world* has something no persuasion system in the survey has.

### 1.6 Renaissance Italy — two theories of what persuasion is for

The weakest of the seven searches, and reported as such rather than padded. `[GAP: Renaissance Italian negotiation practice — the search returned general humanism rather than practice. Merchant manuals (Cotrugli, 1458), the Datini correspondence, and conclave procedure remain unexamined and are the obvious next targets.]`

The one usable finding is nonetheless a real dichotomy. Humanist political thought centred less on republican liberty than on **how to make the ruling class virtuous and prudent**, and its answer was a classical education in virtue and wisdom together with **"new social technologies of persuasion"** — an answer Machiavelli comprehensively rejected in favour of *modi e ordini* that prioritized power over moral virtue `[T2 — Hankins, "Machiavelli, Civic Humanism, and the Humanist Politics of Virtue"]`.

> **Two persuasion economies, from the same city, fifty years apart.** Humanist persuasion forms character slowly and permanently. Machiavellian persuasion moves a decision now and leaves the person unchanged. That is the same distinction as the central and peripheral routes of the Elaboration Likelihood Model, arrived at four and a half centuries earlier and argued as a moral question rather than an empirical one.

Document 4 proposed durability as an unmodelled space in games. The era supplies the *argument* about it: the two routes are not merely different mechanisms, they are rival accounts of what a political actor is for.

## §2 — Primitives N12–N20

Continuing document 4's series, attached to the same M1–M14 vocabulary.

**N12 · Read Before Speaking** — *makes M4 Probe a prerequisite, not an option*
A counterpart cannot be moved until they have been assessed. Three graded assessment moves, from the Guiguzi's own ordering:
- **Weigh** (*chuai*) — estimate the counterpart's real situation: resources, pressures, standing.
- **Gauge** (*mo*) — test covertly. Say something calibrated and read the reply. This is *Fan Ying*, Reflect-Respond: the probe is disguised as a statement.
- **Assess** (*quan*) — judge the balance of forces before committing.
Persuasion moves attempted before assessment resolve at a large penalty, or blind.
*From:* Guiguzi 2, 7–9 `[T1]`; Han Feizi's "the difficulty is knowing the mind" `[T2]`.
*Why it changes the design:* documents 1–4 made investigation optional and rewarding. The era tradition makes it **the majority of the activity**. Chapters 7, 8 and 9 are all assessment; only 10 and 11 are action.

**N13 · Open-Shut** — *disclosure as a move*
Alternate between **opening** (drawing the counterpart out, offering information, inviting elaboration) and **shutting** (sealing, withholding, going silent). Opening gathers; shutting protects and forces the other side to fill the silence.
*From:* Guiguzi 1, *Bai He* `[T1]`; the doctrine of keeping oneself hidden `[T2]`.
*Pairs with:* document 4's N6 anchoring trade — the first offer anchors *and leaks*. Open-Shut generalizes that trade to every utterance.

**N14 · Reverse Scale** — *the per-mind lethal topic*
Every mind has one hidden subject that ends the encounter catastrophically if touched, regardless of standing, evidence, or every other state. Discoverable only by N12 assessment or by prior relationship.
*From:* Han Feizi, *nilin* 逆鱗 `[T2]`.
*Why it is worth its own primitive:* it converts assessment from optimization into risk. Without it, probing is something you do until you have enough; with it, probing is something you do while walking near a cliff.

**N15 · Concealed Comprehension** — *knowing must not be shown*
Two dangers, both from *Shui Nan*: touching on what the target secretly harbours, and being seen to have divined his true intentions behind his stated ones.
*Mechanic:* every move carries a **legibility** value alongside its effect. A high-effect, high-legibility move reveals that you have read your target — which in an autocratic or paranoid forum is itself the harm.
*From:* Han Feizi `[T2]`.
*Consequence:* it inverts the corpus's standing assumption that information is monotonically good.

**N16 · Attribution** — *the win condition worth having*
Persuasion outcomes carry a flag: was the decision **attributed to the persuader**, or does the target believe **he reached it himself**? Self-attributed change is durable and generates no resentment. Persuader-attributed change decays and accrues a grievance.
*From:* Guiguzi — ideally the counterpart is convinced the decision was made all by himself `[T2]`; converging with document 4's N3 (central-route durability) and N5 (reactance).
*Design instruction:* **make attribution, not compliance, the scored outcome.** It is the era's own success criterion and it is strictly more interesting than a depleted bar.

**N17 · Supplication** — *the offer that cannot be refused for free*
A public performance of humility — entreaty in fixed language plus gestures of abasement — that costs the supplicant standing and costs the recipient **more** to refuse, because refusal in public damages his claim to rule by grace. Regionally variable in form; the wrong form for the community fails.
*Ladder:* the same act performed to express **hierarchical distance** or to express **equality between parties**, with different costs and different aftermaths.
*From:* Koziol `[T1]`.
*Why it matters:* it is the only move found in five documents that **binds the more powerful party**, and it does so without any enforcement mechanism at all.

**N18 · Self-Cursing Bond** — *and its inflation*
An oath in two parts, a statement and an **anathema clause** naming the deities who will punish the swearer if he lies. Physically instantiated: written on a specified talismanic form, **split in half** with one part held by each party.
- **Form escalates as trust falls** — plain paper → talismanic paper → seal → blood signature. The current prevailing form is a **public reading of the world's trust level**, visible to any player who looks.
- Third parties (local collectives) hold and mediate.
*From:* the *kishōmon* corpus `[T1]`; Ogino's blood-signature inference `[T2]`.
*Replaces:* document 3's P35/M13 bond, which was a flat "costly signal" with no state and no history.

**N19 · Composition Contest** — *the prior game*
Who sits on the deciding body is settled before anything is said, on a timescale of years to decades, in a different forum from the trial.
*From:* the *album iudicum* compiled annually by the urban praetor; fifty years of contest over the panel from Gracchus (122 BC) through Sulla to the *lex Aurelia* (70 BC), which changed **only who sat** and left the substantive law untouched `[T1]`.
*Design instruction:* the persuasion system's difficulty should be **set** by a contest the player fought in a previous chapter. This is where document 3's `Body.selection` and M14 earn their place, and it is a campaign-scale loop rather than a scene-scale one.

**N20 · Advocate as Evidence** — *the speaker is part of the case*
The advocate's own character is admissible and attacked. Taking a case is an **obligation transaction**: cases are taken to discharge a debt or create one, never disinterestedly. A speech may spend more effort on the character of the accused, the accuser, **or the advocate** than on the facts.
*From:* Roman *patronus* practice `[T2, TIER-FLOOR]`.
*Mechanic:* the player's M1 Standing in the body is not a modifier on the case — it **is** part of the case, and the opposing side may attack it directly instead of attacking the claims.

## §3 — What this changes about the systems

### 3.1 The Table (document 4 §5.1) is rebuilt around assessment

Its old shape was mandate → anchor → exchange → close. The Guiguzi ordering inverts the weight:

```
Weigh -> Gauge -> Assess        [N12 — the majority of the encounter]
   |        |
 Open-Shut across all of it     [N13]
   v
Deploy -> Decide                [the minority]
   |
   +-- avoid the Reverse Scale  [N14]
   +-- keep comprehension hidden [N15]
   v
Close -> attribution check      [N16 — self-attributed or persuader-attributed]
   v
Bond? -> form escalates with world trust  [N18]
```

**The scored outcome is attribution, not concession.** A counterpart who agrees and knows you moved him is a worse result than one who agrees believing it was his own idea, even when the terms are identical.

### 3.2 The Verdict (document 4 §5.3) gains a prior act

`Composition contest (N19) → advocacy (N20) → openings → evidence → closings → ballot → deliberation`

The first stage runs at campaign scale, not scene scale. A player who spent three chapters getting *equites* onto the album arrives at the trial having already won most of it — which is exactly what the fifty-year Roman fight over the panel says was true.

### 3.3 Supplication becomes the third resolution, beside argument and exchange

Document 4 had two ways to move a person: argue (N2–N5) and trade (N6–N7). N17 adds a third that is neither — **perform**. It costs standing, it binds the powerful, it has no enforcement, and its efficacy depends on the form matching the community. Nothing in the corpus did this.

### 3.4 The corrected map of traditions

| Target | Tradition | Primary sources | Character of the art |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Counterpart** | Chinese diplomatic | Guiguzi; Han Feizi *Shui Nan*; Zhanguo ce | Listening, assessment, concealment, attribution |
| **Audience** | Greco-Roman deliberative | Aristotle; Cicero; Quintilian | Composition, arrangement, register, appeals |
| **Jury** | Roman forensic | *quaestiones perpetuae*; the *patronus* | Panel composition, character, pathos |
| **Superior** | Post-Carolingian ritual | Koziol | Public performance that binds by shaming |
| **Peer under distrust** | Sengoku documentary | *kishōmon* corpus | Self-curse, split token, escalating form |

**This table is the answer to A4.** The era does not have one theory of persuasion that fails to address the individual mind. It has *five* traditions, each shaped by the institution it was practised in, and document 4 examined one of them.

## §4 — Adversarial audit

`[SELF-AUTHORED — bias risk]`

**Verdict: the research is the strongest single haul in the session and the primitives derived from it are the most exposed to over-reading. Two of the six findings are systems-grade; two rest on single secondary sources; one search failed and is reported as failed.**

### Severe

**B1 — Guiguzi and Han Feizi were read entirely through secondary summaries and a table of contents.**
The chapter list in §1.1 is a **publisher's contents page** for Hui Wu's translation. The doctrinal claims — hiddenness, self-attribution — come from a database summary. The *Shui Nan* material, including the reverse scale and both dangers, is from a Baidu encyclopedia rendering. I have not read a translation of either text. Gentz (2014) is properly T1 and carries the comparative thesis, but **every specific technique in N12–N16 rests on a paraphrase of a paraphrase.** `[TIER-FLOOR: T2 — primary texts not consulted; Hui Wu's translation and Gentz's article are the obvious next reads and would likely revise the technique list]`

**B2 — I have replaced one over-claim with another.**
Document 4 said the era sources had nothing on minds. This document says they have five traditions. Both are conclusions drawn from a handful of searches, and the second is drawn from *fewer sources per claim* than the first. The five-tradition table in §3.4 is a tidy structure imposed after the fact on six unrelated searches; the categories were not derived, they were arranged. It is probably right and it is not demonstrated.

### High

**B3 — N14 Reverse Scale may be unplayable as specified.**
A hidden topic that ends the encounter catastrophically, discoverable only by assessment, is a landmine. Against a human opponent, stepping on it is a story. Against an AI in a single-player game, it is a save-scum trigger: the player will reload. Either it must be **inferable** — signalled by the target's history, kin, and prior records — or it must be **survivable**, and the document specifies neither.

**B4 — N15 Concealed Comprehension inverts the corpus's information economy without checking what breaks.**
Documents 1–4 are built on information being valuable: M3 Concealed Value, M4 Probe, the *relazione*, the inspection circuit. N15 says displaying comprehension is itself harmful. These are compatible in principle — know privately, act without revealing — but every existing probe in the corpus is either public (circuit) or sealed (denunciation), and none has a *legibility* value. Retrofitting one is a change to M4, not an addition beside it, and the document does not say so.

**B5 — The Renaissance search failed and the game is Renaissance-inspired.**
§1.6 is honest about this and it is still the largest hole. The primary inspiration for the whole project contributed one dichotomy borrowed from a secondary article about political theory, not practice. Cotrugli's merchant manual, the Datini letters, conclave procedure, and *condotta* negotiation are all unexamined.

### Medium

**B6 — N17 Supplication has no failure state.** As written it is an offer the powerful cannot refuse for free. If so, the optimal play is to supplicate constantly. Koziol's own material contains the counter — the form must match the community, and it varies regionally — but the document mentions this and does not price it. **A supplication performed in the wrong form should fail and humiliate.**

**B7 — N18's trust inflation is a world-state model with no world-state system behind it.** "The prevailing oath form reads the world's trust level" presupposes a global trust value that no document has ever defined. It is a lovely idea resting on nothing.

**B8 — N19 solves a problem by moving it.** Making panel composition a campaign-scale contest is historically right and it means the trial's difficulty is set by play that happened elsewhere, possibly hours earlier. That is either the best pacing idea in the corpus or a design where the interesting scene is decided before it starts, and I cannot tell which from here.

**B9 — Ogino's blood-signature inference is one scholar's reading, cited in one article, and I built a primitive on it.** The article itself notes that Ōkouchi criticizes the conventional "death of the oath" narrative for lacking serious research on Tokugawa-period oaths `[T2]`. The inflation mechanic may rest on a contested reading.

### What the attack failed to break

- **Gentz's comparative thesis.** Attacked as a convenient framing. It holds: it is a peer-reviewed T1 statement that European rhetoric is forum-facing and the Guiguzi is counterpart-facing, and it is precisely the distinction document 4's A4 got wrong. The correction stands even if every technique in §1.1 turns out to be misreported.
- **N16 Attribution.** Attacked as a restatement of reactance. It holds and is better than reactance: it is a *scoring rule*, not a penalty, and having the era's own success criterion converge with modern findings on durability from an entirely independent direction is the strongest corroboration in five documents.
- **N19's fifty-year panel fight.** Attacked as an anecdote. It holds: the *lex Aurelia* changed the composition and left the substantive law untouched, after half a century of contest `[T1]`. That is about as clean an empirical statement of "who sits matters more than what is said" as history offers.
- **N17 Supplication.** Attacked for having no enforcement. That is the point and it survives: a move that binds the powerful through public cost alone is exactly what the corpus lacked, and Koziol's central argument is that political order *depended* on it `[T1]`.

### What an independent reviewer would add

1. **Five documents, still nothing played.** The recommendation has been the same since document 3.
2. **Each document has found the previous one's central claim wrong.** Document 3 found document 1's matrix fabricated; document 4 found document 3's resolution model refuted by Klei; document 5 finds document 4's A4 wrong. That is either healthy iteration or a process that will keep producing corrections indefinitely because correcting is easier than committing.
3. **The primitive count is rising again.** Fourteen mechanisms became fourteen plus eleven, and now plus nine. The distillation of document 3 has been quietly undone.

# Standing, Persuasion and the Actor

### Eight findings from setting the pre-modern literature of political, forensic and diplomatic proceeding against modern evidence on what a person can do

---

## What this is

A study of what the written traditions of Greece, Rome, Byzantium, the Carolingian West, Persia and the Islamic world, the Ottoman court, China, Japan and the Latin Renaissance say about conducting oneself in a proceeding — a trial, an embassy, a council, an assembly, an audience with a sovereign — and of what modern outcome research says about whether their advice works and whether a person can acquire what it demands.

It is not a survey. A survey of that literature was written first and is superseded here. What follows is the argument that became visible only after the corpus had been set against outcome evidence and then interrogated backwards, from failure to requirement. **Part I is that argument.** Parts II and III are the evidence and the operational residue. Parts IV to VII are the disputes, the sources, the reading and the apparatus.

## How to read it

**Part I alone is a complete account of the findings.** A reader who stops at the end of it has every finding, every withdrawal and every limit. Parts II and III supply the trees, tables and figures against which the argument can be checked; Part I points to them and does not reproduce them.

A reader who wants the operational material rather than the argument should go directly to **Part III**, which is a table of steps, the failure the corpus names at each, the capacity that failure implies, and whether modern evidence says the capacity can be acquired.

A reader who wants the diagrams should go to **`09-figures.md`**, which collects all twenty-seven of them. Five carry the argument on their own and are the place to start: the pincer, which is why this study takes the form it does; the discretion gate, which joins the scenario to the actor; the capacity map; the recoverability mapping, which shows where effort belongs; and the five canons set against the capacity set, which shows why the corpus does not say so itself.

## Conventions

**▣** marks an evidential note: modern work bounds, reconciles or explains a claim of the corpus.
**⚠** marks a point at which modern work contradicts the corpus's stated reasoning.
Absence of either marker means no evidence was located, not that the claim is sound.

Throughline references are **S1** to **S8**. Requirement-table rows are numbered **0** to **14**. Capacities are numbered **1** to **9**, with one further entry left unnumbered because neither tradition nor evidence names it.

Chapter and section references to primary texts are given only where confidence is high; where a doctrine is certain and its exact place is not, the doctrine is stated and the place omitted. Contested scholarly readings are marked contested. Claims that are the author's synthesis rather than any source's are graded separately in VII.3.

Spelling is Canadian.

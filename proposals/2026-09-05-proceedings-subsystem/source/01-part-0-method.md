# PART 0 — Method, and what this evidence can support

---

## 0.1 The question

What makes a good politician, negotiator, diplomat or orator; how one convinces a tribunal, a jury, a judge, an audience, one's peers or a parliament; how one holds position under attack and attacks without loss to oneself; what constitutes credibility, dignity and standing, and what erodes them; how much adaptability matters; how much reading the room and the opponent matters; when to bluff, when to conjecture, when to produce evidence; and what mastery over social proceedings actually requires of a person.

The pre-modern literature does not treat these as separate questions and neither does this study. The range is roughly 500 BC to 1650, with François de Callières' *De la manière de négocier avec les souverains* (1716) taken as the tradition's terminus. The traditions are Greece, Rome, Byzantium, the Carolingian West, Persia and the Islamic world, the Ottoman court, China, Japan and the Latin Renaissance.

---

## 0.2 What agreement between traditions can and cannot establish

Recurrence identifies problems, not solutions — and it cannot see the actor at all.

An earlier version of this study argued that where widely separated traditions reach the same conclusion, the conclusion is likely true. That inference is withdrawn, for two reasons.

First, the traditions are not independent. Greek rhetoric was translated into Arabic and commented on by al-Fārābī, Ibn Sīnā and Ibn Rushd. Sasanian counsel literature passed through Arabic and Persian into Ottoman advice writing. The Chinese canon and Chinese administrative offices shaped Japanese ones. Where a doctrine could have travelled, its recurrence is inheritance and establishes nothing.

Second, even where independence holds, convergence evidences a shared **problem** and shared human equipment. It does not certify the **solution**. Two societies may arrive at the same device because they face the same constraint and think with the same apparatus, and both may be wrong about whether it works.

What survives is more modest and more useful: **recurrence identifies the problems that are structural rather than local.** When Han Feizi and Plato separately insist that the persuader's real task is knowing the mind in front of him, that tells us the difficulty is general. Whether either remedy works is a different question, and this study marks where the traditions disagree about remedies rather than smoothing it over.

Independent recurrences, with no plausible transmission before the date of both. The column is titled for what the evidence licenses — the same problem addressed twice — not for agreement on a remedy, and the third row records two societies reaching *opposite* devices for one problem, which is the clearest case of all.

| Pair | Same problem, independently addressed | Same remedy? |
|---|---|---|
| Chinese *fu shi* and *feng jian* × Greco-Roman figured speech | conveying what the hearer will punish (S3) | similar device, **different guarantee** — the Chinese form rests on a shared canon and the Roman on inference alone |
| Han Feizi on the ruler's mind × Plato's *psychagogia* | the hearer must be read before he is addressed (S5) | both diagnose; neither remedy validated |
| Japanese consensus-without-recorded-division × Carolingian secret counsel | protecting a dissenter from public defeat (D4) | **opposite mechanisms** — open agreement with no division recorded, against concealed decision with public ratification |

An earlier draft also listed Zeami's *riken no ken* beside Cicero's *decorum* as a convergence, and then set the same pair in opposition when discussing whether adaptability can be acquired. Both entries were wrong. Seeing oneself from where the audience sits is not fitness of style, matter and self to occasion; they are adjacent doctrines, neither the same claim nor rival answers to one question. The row is deleted.

**There is a third limit, and it is the reason this study is organized as it is.** Filtering by recurrence keeps what recurs across traditions. Structures recur; persons do not. A method built on recurrence is therefore a filter that passes the scenario and stops the actor, and the earlier version of this study, organized entirely by recurrence, produced a document about situations without anyone deciding that it should be one. Part I is organized by finding rather than by recurrence for exactly this reason.

## 0.3 The borrowed category

"Face" is not a settled analytic term, and this study does not use it as one.

An earlier draft conceded that it treated face as a stable category across two millennia and eight civilizations when the pre-modern West has no such unified concept, handling the material instead under *dignitas*, *auctoritas*, *existimatio*, *fama*, *verecundia* and *pudor*. That concession was true and it named the wrong problem.

The larger problem is that the category is not settled in the field it was borrowed from. Goffman took the term from Chinese usage; Brown and Levinson (1987) built it into a universal theory of positive and negative face; and that universalization was attacked on precisely cross-cultural grounds — Matsumoto (1988) and Ide (1989) on Japanese honorifics and discernment, Gu (1990) and Mao (1994) on a Chinese orientation toward a publicly negotiated social identity rather than an individual's want to be unimpeded. The discursive turn that followed, in Eelen (2001) and Watts (2003), abandoned the second-order universal construct in favour of first-order politeness as participants themselves evaluate it. **This study is therefore not applying a settled sociological category awkwardly to the pre-modern West; it would be applying a category that pragmatics has spent thirty-five years arguing is not portable.**

The remedy is to work first-order, from the terms each tradition uses, and the Chinese material shows why that pays. *Mianzi* — prestige accumulated through success, position and display — and *lian* — the community's confidence in one's moral character — have been distinguished at least since Hu (1944), and they behave differently. **Mianzi can be lost and rebuilt; lian, once forfeited, is not recoverable by the same means.** The distinction does work throughout:

- What oblique remonstrance protects in a ruler is *mianzi*.
- What the Confucian insistence on *xin* puts at hazard is *lian*.
- The circle of justice is valuable precisely because it costs the sovereign neither.
- Liudprand's seating below the Bulgarian envoy was a *mianzi* injury, which is why writing the *Relatio* afterwards was a plausible repair. Aeschines' failure at the trial over the crown was nearer a *lian* injury, and he left Athens.

**The rule this study follows.** "Face" appears only in quotation from a source, or in the fixed phrase "face-preserving." Everywhere else the specific thing at stake is named: *mianzi* or *lian*, *dignitas* or *existimatio*, *kurai*, *fama*, *auctoritas*.

## 0.4 What the corpus records

The corpus records failure types exhaustively and failure frequencies not at all — and an earlier version of this study read past the first half of that sentence entirely.

**On frequencies.** No source in this survey reports a base rate, a failure count, a comparison case or a counterfactual. The literature consists of advice that was written down, copied, taught, translated and preserved, and those filters select for memorability, institutional usefulness, the prestige of the author and the interests of copyists. None of them selects for whether the advice worked. The general form of the problem — inference from a sample that undersamples failure — is described in the organizational-learning literature (Denrell 2003). Two illustrations from inside the survey: Han Feizi's chapter on the difficulties of persuasion is the flattest statement anywhere of the problem, and its author was forced to take poison in Qin in 233 BC after failing to persuade, and the doctrine was preserved regardless. Liudprand's *Relatio* is poor history and excellent evidence of practice, which is the right verdict and one that generalizes.

**On types.** The corpus is a fault catalogue. Nearly every major text contains a section that enumerates what goes wrong, named and classified, and an earlier version of this study built itself almost entirely from the move-catalogues sitting beside them:

| Tradition | Fault catalogue |
|---|---|
| Greece | *Rhetoric* III.3 on frigidity; II.24 on apparent enthymemes; the whole of the *Sophistical Refutations*; *Topics* VIII on who is blamed when an argument fails |
| Rome | *Ad Herennium* I.11 on the faulty exordium; Quintilian VIII.3 on *vitia*, VI.3 on the faults of wit, VI.4 on faults in the rapid exchange, XI.3 on faults of delivery |
| Byzantium | *Strategikon* XI as a catalogue of ways to mishandle each people; Liudprand as a first-person failure narrative |
| Carolingian | Hincmar cc. XIII–XVI on the fault of leaked counsel |
| Persia and Islam | *Qābūs-nāma* on faults of speech; *balāgha*'s fault of speech unfitted to the listener; Nizām al-Mulk's anecdotes, most of which are failure narratives |
| Ottoman | the *nasīhatnāme* genre, which is institutional fault-finding by design |
| China | Han Feizi 3 and 12; *Analects* 1.3 on clever words and an ingratiating expression; the inaptly chosen ode |
| Japan | Zeami on the false flower and on performing above one's attained rank; Article 10's named fault of resenting difference |
| Renaissance | *affettazione*; Guicciardini's *Ricordi*, which is largely a fault catalogue; Machiavelli to Girolami on the envoy's faults |

**The consequence.** The corpus supports **description** and it supports **requirement analysis**. It cannot support **procedure**. Knowing what can go wrong at a step, and what an actor must be able to do to avoid it, is available from a type inventory. Knowing which move to make, and whether that move works, is not.

**The three rules of the requirement analysis**, which govern Part III:

1. A step earns a requirement only if failure at it is **observable and attributable**. If one cannot tell that it went wrong, or cannot tell it was this step, there is no requirement to state.
2. The requirement derives from the **failure mode**, never from the ideal. "Be persuasive" is not a requirement; it is the outcome renamed.
3. The requirement must be a **capacity**, not a disposition. "Prudent" is a summary; "able to specify a reservation point before entering" is a capacity.

## 0.5 What Part I is, and what the tables are

Part I states eight findings that no single stage of this work produced.

Each was admitted only if it passes two tests. It must be derivable from at least two of the three sources used here — the pre-modern corpus, modern outcome evidence, the step-level interrogation — and it must **withdraw** something an earlier version asserted. A finding that merely restates a doctrine of the corpus is a quotation, not a result. Each of the eight accordingly records an error corrected, and the corrections are collected in VII.1.

The tables in Parts II and III are of two kinds and neither is an instruction.

**Fault tables** set out what the corpus names as going wrong at a point, with the source in every cell. **The requirement table** in Part III sets out a step, the fault, the capacity that fault implies, whether modern evidence says the capacity can be acquired, how recoverable the failure is, and how sensitive the step is to the discretion the arrangement allows. An earlier version rendered this material as decision trees in the imperative, which claimed a warrant that 0.2 had already withdrawn. Nothing here is written in the imperative.

**One admission that cannot be argued away.** This study holds that judgment is what decides proceedings and that nearly every tradition surveyed declares it irreducible to rule — and then presents a list of capacities in a table. The form asserts something the content denies. Two things mitigate it and neither dissolves it. A requirement claim is falsifiable where a prescription is not: "clearing this step requires the ability to detect hostility before speaking" can be wrong in checkable ways, while "open obliquely into a hostile room" cannot. And the acquisition column reports what is known about whether each capacity can be had, including where nothing is known. The residual problem stands.

## 0.6 Two standing cautions from the outcome literature

**Field beats laboratory, and this matters more than it looks.** The research designs most used to test whether individual differences affect outcomes are strong situations by construction — fixed scripts, payoff matrices, assigned roles — and strong situations suppress person variance. The meta-analysis of individual differences in negotiation found effects stronger in field data than in the laboratory (Sharma, Bottom & Elfenbein 2013). Anyone looking for evidence about the actor in the places where it is cheapest to look is looking in the places designed to remove him.

**A null mean is weak evidence against a doctrine written for the tail.** The corpus was composed by and for people at the far end of a distribution, and typical-performance paradigms are not built to detect what distinguishes them. The researchers running the largest geopolitical forecasting tournament say so directly: tournament incentives illuminate aspects of judgment that would not appear in laboratory work focused on typical performance (Mellers et al. 2015). The distributional version of the argument — that individual performance is Paretian rather than Gaussian across 633,263 people including politicians (O'Boyle & Aguinis 2012) — is **contested**, with Beck, Beatty & Sackett and Spain, Credé & Harms arguing that the extreme departures are substantially artefactual. It is mentioned and not leaned on.

**And a note on transfer.** The outcome literatures used here measure negotiation, executive performance and the effects of national leaders. Nothing was located that measures person-effects in a tribunal, an embassy or a parliament specifically. The transfer to those settings is inference, stated once here and not repeated.

## 0.7 Confidence conventions

Chapter and section references are given only where confidence is high; where a doctrine is certain and its exact place is not, the doctrine is stated and the place omitted. Contested scholarly readings are marked contested rather than reported as settled.

Three categories are graded separately in VII.3 because they are the entries most likely to be wrong:

- **Synthesis-glosses** — claims that are this study's rather than any source's. The anatomy of Part II, the requirement table, the three concealment tests, the two-axis diagram in D4, the eloquence-and-consequence pairing, and the forensic intermediate case are all of this kind.
- **Capacity claims** — each graded *evidenced*, *tradition-only* or *double-absent*.
- **Modern empirical claims** — separated into those verified against the published abstract or primary text and those adopted from a critique or from established familiarity and marked `[UNVERIFIED]`.

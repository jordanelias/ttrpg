# PART II — What the scenario fixes

Three things are settled before an actor speaks: the standing he brings, the latitude the arrangement allows him, and the evidence that exists. This part sets them out with the trees and figures that Part I points to. The argument is in Part I; what follows is the material against which it can be checked.

---

## II.1 Standing

Standing is contested procedurally, before any content, and its erosion is better predicted by how it was acquired than by what kind it is.

The sharpest statement in the corpus is a parable. Mi Zixia, a favourite of the ruler of Wei, once took the ruler's carriage without leave to visit his sick mother, and once handed the ruler a peach he had already bitten into. While he stood high, the first was praised as filial devotion worth a severed foot and the second as love that would share what was sweet. When his looks faded and favour went, the same two acts were produced as evidence of insolence and he was punished for them. The facts did not change. What changed was the position from which they issued.

The Western material arrives at the same place along institutional lines. Roman senators were called for their opinion in rank order, so precedence was a public ruling delivered without a word. Liudprand of Cremona, sent to Constantinople by Otto I in 968, found himself seated at table below a Bulgarian envoy and understood correctly that the seating was the negotiation — a determination of the relative standing of two sovereigns, made before any proposal was heard. He records that he said nothing at the time because of the pain in his heart.

Ibn Khaldūn supplies the only causal account in the corpus. Prestige is not a personal possession but a share in the solidarity of a group, and it decays on a schedule of roughly four generations as luxury dissolves the solidarity and the descendants retain only the memory of the claim. On that account most of what looks like personal standing is borrowed and expires whether or not its holder makes a single mistake.

### Provenance

```
STANDING
├─ A1 ASCRIBED — birth, office, rank, precedence
│    ▸ contested procedurally, before any content: seating, order of speaking,
│      forms of address, who is held by the arms, who remains seated
│    ▸ Liudprand's lesson: a position lost here is lost before a word
├─ A2 ACCUMULATED — the record of offices, obligations owed, consistency
│    ▸ Roman auctoritas as the residue of a life; Quintilian's claim that the
│      orator's own conduct is evidence in every case he pleads
├─ A3 PERFORMED — what this room sees now
│    ▸ Aristotle's insistence that the art produce credit within the speech
│    ▸ practical wisdom, virtue and goodwill toward the hearer: the three
│      grounds on which a hearer credits a speaker. Aristotle does not claim
│      these exhaust the ways persuasion fails, and an earlier version of this
│      study wrongly said he did
└─ A4 STAGED — ceremony, setting, retinue, controlled access
     ▸ the Byzantine order of reception; the Ottoman silent audience; and the
       exact inverse, Charlemagne hearing envoys while dressing
```

### The cut that does the causal work

Provenance is useful for arranging sources and it is not what predicts behaviour. Deference has two routes. **Prestige** is freely conferred for demonstrated competence; **dominance** is extracted by fear and the capacity to impose costs. Both attain rank and influence, by different means and with different nonverbal signatures (Cheng, Tracy, Foulsham, Kingstone & Henrich 2013). The cut runs across all four provenances: staged ceremony can serve either; an accumulated record usually serves prestige; ascribed office can be pure dominance. Sorted this way, the four positions in D1 stop looking like rival theories.

### Erosion

Inconsistency. Visible self-interest attached to advice. Visible effort, which is the fault Castiglione names. Claiming more than one's place warrants. And the **discovered** lie specifically — not the lie. Machiavelli's instruction to his envoy is that if a fact must be concealed with words, it should be done so that it is not noticed.

### ▣ Two bounds the corpus does not state

Reception is a function of position, and it is bounded by **how hard the room is thinking**: source credibility operates as a peripheral cue where motivation or ability to process is low, and biases processing rather than replacing it where they are high. Position dominates an inattentive room and merely tilts an attentive one.

It is also bounded **in time**. The suppression a discounting cue imposes can dissipate while the message survives; recipients of a discounting cue were more persuaded at delay than immediately, most strongly where the arguments had real initial impact and where recipients had higher ability or motivation to think (Kumkale & Albarracín 2004). **Low standing suppresses reception now and does not reliably suppress it later.** A speaker with no standing may still place an argument that outlives the discount on its source.

### Standing as a stock

The whole of the above describes a constraint only because an encounter freezes the clock. A2 is the sediment of prior choices, and the corpus knows it: Machiavelli's memorandum of October 1522 is about filling the reputation that will later present itself as a given. The actor's contribution to this layer is made before the layer begins, and Part IV's first dispute turns on whether that stock can be spent, rebuilt, or is not a stock at all.

## II.2 Arrangement and discretion

Every device below reduces the latitude of the person occupying the role, and that is what they are for.

### The interposition catalogue

**A person.** The Roman patron system is the cleanest instance: the principal does not plead, and an advocate can be defeated without the client being humiliated. It is why states negotiate through envoys, and why Ottoman business passed through the grand vizier while the sultan said little.

**An office.** The censor, the remonstrance official, the appointed critic. A formal warrant to criticize converts a personal affront into the discharge of a duty — the same manoeuvre as the advocate, applied to criticism rather than pleading. Wu Jing's compilation on the governance of Tang Taizong is the casebook: what Wei Zheng said, in what form, and how narrowly he survived saying it.

**A document.** The Ottoman *telhīs* — the grand vizier's written summary and recommendation, returned with a written ruling — removes counsel from face-to-face exchange entirely. The vizier is not seen to be overruled and the sultan is not seen to be persuaded.

**A procedure.** Hincmar's *De ordine palatii* describes an inner council of the elder counsellors that sets the agenda and takes the decisions, and a general assembly that hears, discusses and is seen to consent, with secrecy of deliberation enjoined on the stated ground that leakage breeds resentment and revolt. Read Hincmar with care: he wrote in 882 amid disaster, excluded from the young king's circle, and the scheme may be advocacy for his own recall rather than a description of practice. The Japanese variant works differently — the council of state established in 1225, operating under the code of 1232, decided by consensus rather than declared majority, which protects the dissenter, who is never recorded as outvoted.

**A ceremony.** The Byzantine compilation on court order rests on the premise that ritual is statement rather than ornament. Envoys were received into a designed sequence: the walk, the silence, the prostration. The mechanical golden lions and the throne that rose toward the ceiling belong to Liudprand's *Antapodosis*, describing his embassy of 949 under Constantine VII, not to the *Relatio* of 968. Persuasion is conducted here with no argument at all: the envoy is not convinced of a proposition, he is placed in a relation.

**A pair.** The royal envoys of the Carolingian West were sent out in twos, typically one lay and one cleric, watching each other — a check on the representative who cannot be observed.

### ▣ The measured cost of interposition

Third-party representation raises the price obtained and raises the rate of impasse (Bazerman, Neale, Valley, Zajac & Kim 1992). Representatives under constituency pressure shift toward contentious tactics and away from exchanging information, producing fewer integrative agreements and more deadlocks; where the constituency is dovish, information exchange recovers and so does agreement quality. Interposition buys protection and distributive advantage at the cost of joint gain and of the deal happening at all.

That gives Commynes' rule a testable form. Two princes who meet convert a negotiable difference into a question of honour; two who do not meet forgo the exchange in which unshared information gets pooled. And it makes the *telhīs* legible as a specific trade rather than a maximum: written counsel removes the possibility of visible defeat and removes with it the discussion that would have found the arrangement suiting both parties better.

The *telhīs* is also a **channel** decision, and this study treats it only as a screening device. Written, asynchronous and face-to-face exchange produce measurably different negotiation outcomes; classing it as a channel choice is what would allow it to be evaluated rather than only described. Not pursued here; logged in VII.2.

## II.3 The evidential furniture

What exists to be used is fixed before the proceeding; what may be inferred is not, and the corpus's ranking of the two requires one substantial correction.

Aristotle divides the **inartificial** proofs — laws, witnesses, contracts, testimony taken under torture, oaths — which are found rather than made and in which skill enters only at selection and framing, from the **artificial**, which the speaker invents. The practical consequence, as the corpus states it, is a strict ranking: where an inartificial proof exists and favours you, everything else is decoration, and arguing probability in addition weakens it, because it signals that you do not think the document sufficient.

**⚠ The modern hierarchy of evidence partly inverts the ancient one.** Reporting the division is unobjectionable; issuing it as procedure is not. Mistaken eyewitness identification is implicated in roughly seventy per cent of United States DNA exonerations, and the National Research Council's assessment (2014) is largely a catalogue of the ways a confidently delivered identification is the least trustworthy item in the room. Testimony extracted under coercion has its own literature and the same shape. **Throughout this study, "inartificial proof" should be read as documents, records and contracts; a confident witness is a probable sign wearing a necessary sign's clothes.**

Within the artificial proofs the distinction that matters is between the necessary sign, which cannot be refuted, and the probable sign, which can. And the probable sign is **symmetric**, as S8 sets out. That symmetry converts into a usable test — could the opponent advance the mirror image of this argument? — and the corpus already ran it, on the speaker's own opening, in the *Ad Herennium*'s fault list.

### Locating the issue

Quintilian recognizes three rational issues and regards the procedural objection as disputed rather than a settled fourth.

```
WHAT IS ACTUALLY IN DISPUTE?
│
├─ Is the tribunal competent to hear this at all?
│   └─ the procedural objection — forum, standing, jurisdiction
│      ▸ Quintilian doubts this is a true issue
│      ▸ cheapest available win, but it signals evasion; the corpus holds
│        that it is spent early or not at all
│
├─ Is the FACT disputed? → CONJECTURE
│   ├─ where documents and records exist, the corpus holds that they are led
│   │   with and the conjecture abandoned
│   └─ otherwise the argument runs from motive, from capacity and opportunity,
│       from signs before during and after, and from consistency of character
│      ▸ all four are reversible; see S8
│
├─ Fact admitted, its NAME disputed? → DEFINITION
│      ▸ theft or sacrilege · error or fraud · gift or bribe
│
├─ Fact and name admitted, its QUALITY disputed? → QUALITY
│      the province of the favourable construction
│      ├─ justification: it was right
│      ├─ excuse: ignorance, necessity, accident
│      ├─ transfer: another is responsible
│      └─ mitigation: it was less than alleged
│
└─ The corpus's rule: this ladder is descended only when forced, because
   every descent is visible and is read as a concession not announced.
```

The **favourable construction** — Quintilian's and Seneca the Elder's technical term for the gloss placed on an admitted fact, which leaves the act intact and changes what kind of thing it was — is what remains when the fact is proven. Seneca's declamation collections record, for each case, the epigrams, the divisions and the constructions proposed by rival declaimers, which makes the third of these the ancient world's training material for reframing what cannot be denied.

### Concealment

The corpus divides sharply on whether deceit is permitted, as D2 sets out, and very nearly agrees on when it fails. The distinction the Renaissance settled on is between feigning what is not and concealing what is, the second widely licensed and the first not.

```
├─ Documents or records available?
│    the corpus holds that they are led with, and probability not argued
│    ▣ this has modern support the corpus does not name — the dilution of
│      diagnostic information by non-diagnostic information, and the
│      depressive effect of appending weak arguments to strong under high
│      elaboration — and a boundary the corpus does not state: under LOW
│      elaboration, number of arguments works as a cue and additions help
│
├─ A necessary sign available?
│    used, and named as necessary so the hearer knows the difference
│
├─ Only probable signs?
│    ├─ against a skilled opponent → reversal expected; the case is not staked
│    └─ against an unskilled one → usable; several stacked rather than one
│
└─ Nothing?
     ├─ Silence or ambiguity — cheap, recoverable, deniable
     ├─ Asserted falsehood — three tests, all of which must pass:
     │    1. can it be cheaply checked?
     │    2. will this party be needed again?
     │    3. does exposure cost more than the concession avoided?
     └─ Otherwise concession, with something bought by it
```

The three tests are not from any single source. They are the common denominator of Cicero's prohibition, Machiavelli's licence, Gentili's restriction of the useful lie, the Confucian insistence on trustworthiness, and Kautilya's ordering of conciliation and gift ahead of dissension and force. **The traditions disagree violently about whether deceit is permitted and agree almost entirely about when it fails.**

### Anticipating the opponent

The corpus answers the question Protagoras raises, and an earlier version of this study reported the question without the answer. *Procatalepsis* — also *prolepsis*, *praemunitio*, *praesumptio* — is the figure of stating and refuting an opponent's argument before he makes it. It is prescribed in the *Rhetoric to Alexander*, discussed by Cicero, and treated systematically by Quintilian, who adds the practitioner's warning that pre-empting an argument the opponent had not thought of hands it to him.

The modern finding is its descendant. Refutational two-sided messages outperform one-sided messages; non-refutational two-sided messages do worse than either. Raising the opposing case and answering it beats ignoring it; raising it and leaving it standing is worse than silence. S3 extends the figure from the opponent's argument to one's own manner.

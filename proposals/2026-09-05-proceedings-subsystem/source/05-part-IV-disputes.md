# PART IV — The disputes

Four places where the traditions conflict rather than vary. Two are reframed by findings in Part I, one is resolved by a finding, and one remains open. Each is headed by the finding that governs it.

**A note on the whole part.** The question of how much the person contributes and how much the situation is not adjacent to the third dispute below; it *is* the third dispute, in other words. Where the corpus theorizes individual difference at all it theorizes fixity — Machiavelli's habituation, Ibn Khaldūn's decaying solidarity, Han Feizi's Mi Zixia, in which merit is not the relevant variable. Zeami is the single voice for malleability. S6 is what dissolves that, and it dissolves the larger question with it.

## D1. Is standing produced or inherited?

*Governed by S1 and S2.*

| Position | Claim | If true, then |
|---|---|---|
| **Aristotle** | Credit is produced by the speech itself; the art does not rest on prior repute | a bad day is a bad day, and recovery is always available |
| **Rome and the Carolingian West** | *Auctoritas* is the sediment of a life; the orator's conduct is evidence in every case he pleads | standing is a stock: spendable, and capable of being exhausted |
| **Ibn Khaldūn** | Prestige is a share in group solidarity, decaying across roughly four generations | most apparent personal standing is borrowed and expires whether or not its holder errs |
| **Han Feizi** | Reception is current favour; identical acts read oppositely as favour moves | there is nothing to build, only a position to hold |

Aristotle's claim is narrower than it first looks — he is describing what belongs to the *art*, not denying that reputation affects outcomes.

**Sorted by mechanism rather than by provenance, the four stop being rivals.** Using the prestige-and-dominance distinction from II.1: Mi Zixia is a **dominance** regime, in which deference tracks the ruler's current disposition and nothing accumulates. Ibn Khaldūn's is **inherited dominance**, decaying as the solidarity that produced it dissolves. Roman *auctoritas* is **prestige with an institutional record attached**. Aristotle is describing what a speech can generate **within a single occasion**, which is a claim about a different span of time rather than a different theory of standing. Four regimes accurately described, and the practical question becomes *which regime is this*, which is answerable, rather than *who is right*, which is not.

Two qualifications carry over. From II.1: low standing suppresses reception now and not reliably later, so in the two positions that treat standing as a stock, a depleted stock is a smaller handicap at delay than at the moment of speaking. From S2: the stock is filled by prior choices, so even the inheritance positions leave the actor a contribution, made earlier.

## D2. Is deceit permitted?

*Open. Touched by S3, which explains why the corpus's own answers are shaped as they are.*

Cicero holds that the useful cannot conflict with the honourable and that concealment which injures is fraud. Machiavelli holds that one must be a great feigner and dissembler, since men are so simple that a deceiver never lacks victims. The Renaissance compromise is the distinction between feigning what is not and concealing what is, the second widely licensed and the first not, made explicit in Accetto's treatise of 1641. Gentili takes up the useful lie in his work on embassies of 1585 by way of an argument descending from Plato, where the lie as medicine is reserved to rulers — which is precisely the contested point where an ambassador is concerned, and **his settled position is disputed in the scholarship and should not be reported either as a permission or as a prohibition.** Confucius makes trustworthiness foundational, which Han Feizi treats as naive. Kautilya's ordering — conciliation, gift, dissension, force — is prudential rather than moral and says nothing about honesty as such.

Diplomatic practice ran ahead of the theory. Barbaro's treatise on the office of the envoy holds that the ambassador's whole duty is the preservation and aggrandizement of his own state; the reception of the text divides between a reading that has him demanding scrupulous truthfulness and Fubini's reading, which finds an open political utilitarianism. Both may be right: honesty commended as instrument rather than as constraint.

**What is not open is when deceit fails**, and the three tests at II.3 are the common denominator of every position above. The general rule underneath them is that silence and ambiguity are cheap and recoverable while asserted falsehood is expensive and, once discovered, is not recoverable — which is why the discovered lie sits in the terminal class at III.4, and why Machiavelli's instruction to his envoy concerns being noticed rather than concealing.

## D3. Is adaptability attainable?

*Resolved by S6, on three legs.*

Machiavelli says matching one's proceeding to the times is decisive, and then says it is nearly impossible for an individual, because past success habituates a man to the method that produced it. Fabius could not become Scipio, and the conclusion he draws is institutional: a republic outlives a prince because it can replace the man rather than change him. Zeami says the faculty is trainable and names the training. Cicero assumes attainability without arguing for it, since his doctrine of fitness would otherwise be an empty prescription.

### First leg: the conditions

Skilled intuition forms under two conditions — an environment of high validity, in which relations between cue and outcome are stable and learnable, and prolonged practice with feedback that is rapid and unequivocal (Kahneman & Klein 2009). Where either fails, what develops is confidence rather than skill. The three environments are set out in S6: Zeami's has both conditions; Machiavelli's has neither; forensic oratory sits between, with fast feedback whose meaning is uninterpretable, and its practitioners — Cicero, Quintilian — think most of the art teachable. **Both men report accurately about their own environments, and the dispute is about feedback structure rather than about human nature.**

The corpus's own distribution confirms it: every tradition here that declares the faculty unteachable by rule is political, forensic or diplomatic, and the one that claims a training is theatrical.

### Second leg: what does train

▣ Technique trains where perception does not. Antonakis, Fenley and Liechti taught a defined set of charismatic tactics — metaphor, story, rhetorical question, contrast, the three-part list, moral conviction, expressed confidence, gesture, voice — in a field experiment and a laboratory experiment; coders confirmed increased frequency, and observers rated the trained speakers higher, with a mean effect near .62, and charisma predicted rated prototypicality and emergence. The authors note that the training took real time and that inexperienced overuse produces self-parody, which is the overshoot fault of S3 arriving in a controlled trial.

▣ Judgment trains partly. Superforecasters are "partly discovered and partly created," with the best model combining dispositional, situational and behavioural variables at a multiple correlation near .64 (Mellers et al. 2015).

▣ And a thin result worth stating as thin: within-person **variability** in personality predicts negotiation gains and satisfaction better than average trait levels — adaptability as a measurable individual difference. Two studies in one paper, dyadic, not replicated at scale. If it holds, it vindicates Machiavelli's *Prince* against his own *Discorsi*.

### Third leg: the environment is a design variable

A forecasting tournament is the deliberate conversion of a low-validity domain into a high-validity one. Attach resolvable questions, deadlines and scores to political judgment, and the trainability appears. **No text in this survey proposes it.** The corpus treats the environment as the condition an actor is placed in; the strongest single answer to what an active actor can do is that he may sometimes build the feedback his domain does not supply.

### What follows

The institutional designs of II.2 are not a crutch for those who failed to train. In a low-validity environment they are the correct response, and a study that catalogues them is describing a rational adaptation rather than an evasion. And Part III's table loses most of its claim to be a trainable procedure and keeps its claim to be a requirement analysis: in a low-validity environment, a stated procedure substitutes for judgment because reliable judgment does not form — unless the validity itself is changed.

**Two markers.** The forensic intermediate case is this study's judgment and appears in no source. The transfer of environment-construction from probabilistic judgment to the interpersonal capacities of Part III is not shown by anything located, and those capacities — reframing under pressure, tempo, allocation of attention — are precisely the ones with no evidence at all.

## D4. Should counsel be public or private?

*Governed by S2. Two axes, and the trade is unresolved on both.*

Four settlements of one problem — how to obtain advice without anyone losing standing for giving or rejecting it — each buying its solution at a different price.

```
                        candour ◄──────────────────────► protection
   ATHENS               ROME              CAROLINGIAN         OTTOMAN
   public, adversarial  public, but       privately decided,  written telhīs;
   the speaker bears    rank-ordered      publicly ratified,  neither party is
   legal risk           by procedure      secrecy enjoined    seen to yield
```

### ▣ The second axis

That axis is right and incomplete. Groups discuss disproportionately what all members already know and systematically fail to surface what only one member knows, which is why a group holding a hidden profile chooses the inferior option (Stasser & Titus 1985). The correction is dissent present before discussion begins, which raises discussion intensity and reduces the bias toward shared information (Schulz-Hardt et al. 2006), and authentic dissent outperforms assigned devil's advocacy, which produces the appearance of challenge without its effects (Nemeth et al. 2001).

**This inverts an earlier reading of the Japanese case.** Consensus without a recorded division was placed near the Carolingian end, on the ground that it protects the dissenter who is never recorded as outvoted. On the second axis it is not near the Carolingian end at all. A procedure that removes the record of dissent removes the means by which dissent does its informational work, and it does so more thoroughly than secrecy does: Hincmar's inner council conceals disagreement from outsiders while permitting it inside the room, whereas a consensus requirement discourages the disagreement from forming at all.

```
                   protection of the dissenter's position
                              ▲
        Japanese consensus  ● │ ● Carolingian secret council
                              │
   ───────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────►
                              │              information pooling
                    Ottoman ● │ ● Rome        ● Athens
                       telhīs │
```

The trade is therefore worse than the single-axis version showed, and saying so strengthens rather than weakens the claim that these are unresolved. None of the four escapes it. The comparative claim — that consensus suppresses dissent more thoroughly than secrecy — is an argument from the mechanism and not a tested comparison; it is graded accordingly.

### Eloquence and consequence

Tacitus argues through Maternus that great eloquence is the child of licence and disorder: it flourishes where the outcome is genuinely in doubt and a body genuinely decides, and it withers when one man decides. The arts described in this study scale with the number of people who must actually be persuaded, and where a single will suffices the technique migrates from public argument to private counsel, taking the literature with it — from rhetoric to mirrors for princes. That migration is what the Byzantine, Carolingian, Persian, Ottoman and imperial Chinese material records.

**Set beside the leader-effects evidence, the observation sharpens into a pair.** The individual's *impact* is greatest precisely where one man decides. Both hold, because they concern different quantities: **eloquence requires many people who must be persuaded; consequence requires few people who can constrain.** The republic maximizes the first and minimizes the second; the sultanate does the reverse. A practitioner choosing between them is choosing between an arena where his art matters and one where his decisions do. The pairing is this study's and appears in neither source.

One limit on the Tacitean half. It is a claim about eloquence migrating, not about the problem migrating. On the second axis above, the information-pooling failure is worst precisely in the private-counsel regimes the literature migrated into — small rooms, shared priors, and a strong incentive not to be the one who disagrees.

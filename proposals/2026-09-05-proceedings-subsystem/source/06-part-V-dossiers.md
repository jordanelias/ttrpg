# PART V — Tradition dossiers

Compact reference, retained because internal coherence within a tradition is information that an argument organized by finding discards: the Ottoman silent sovereign is intelligible only alongside the written summary and the circle of justice, and Byzantine ceremonial only alongside the precedent file and Liudprand. Each dossier names its **fault catalogue**, since 0.4 claims every major text has one, and its **principal contribution** to the findings of Part I.

---

## Greece

Gorgias in the *Encomium of Helen* — speech as a great potentate, persuasion as a causal force classed with drugs and witchcraft. Protagoras — two opposed arguments on every matter, and the notorious promise to make the weaker the stronger, the latter a hostile attribution reported by Aristotle; the anonymous *Dissoi Logoi* of about 400 BC is a **separate text**, not Protagoras's. Corax and Tisias — the probability reversal. Plato's *Phaedrus* — the leading of souls, and the demand that the practitioner recognize the type before him in the moment. Aristotle — the division of proofs; the three grounds of credit; the hearer's task determining the species of speech; the typology of audiences by age and fortune; twenty-eight topics of genuine enthymeme; the dissolution of prejudicial attack; the peroration for feeling. *Topics* VIII — concealment of one's line of attack, how to answer without being trapped, and who is blamed when an argument fails. *Rhetoric to Alexander* — pseudo-Aristotelian, generally assigned to Anaximenes of Lampsacus, and the earliest prescription of anticipating the opponent.

**Institutions.** Juries of 201 to 1,501 with no judge, no appeal and a secret ballot without deliberation. The water clock. Litigants speaking in person, hence the speechwriter's trade and the Lysian art of writing so that the jury believes it hears a man rather than a composition. The assembly's audible interruption as continuous feedback. And the prosecution available against a citizen for having proposed an unlawful decree, which placed personal legal risk on the deliberative speaker.

**Worked example.** Demosthenes on the crown against Aeschines, 330 BC. Demosthenes defends the policy as it appeared at the time rather than the outcome — a counsellor is answerable for judgment, not for fortune. Aeschines failed to secure a fifth of the votes, was fined, and left Athens.

**Fault catalogue.** *Rhetoric* III.3 on frigidity; II.24 on apparent enthymemes; the *Sophistical Refutations* entire; *Topics* VIII on blame for a failed argument.

**Principal contribution.** S8 (the reversal and the check on one's own case); S3 (frigidity as overshoot); S5 by contrast, since Plato's demand is the diagnostic pole against which the *Guiguzi* is generative.

## Rome

Patron and client — the principal does not plead. The sequence of offices as visible accumulated standing. Senators called for their opinion in rank order, so precedence is stated procedurally before debate begins. Competitive assertion of worth among equals as the ambient condition, and the reason Roman rhetoric is preoccupied with insult, wit and the management of hostility.

Cicero — the five canons; instructing, pleasing, moving; credit and feeling as the two conciliating instruments; the fullest ancient treatment of wit, with its restrictions permitting the pompous and the powerful and forbidding the wretched and the widely loved; *De officiis* III on the honourable and the useful.

Quintilian — the doctrine of issues, with three rational and the procedural objection disputed; the favourable construction; *procatalepsis* and *praemunitio*, with the warning against handing an opponent an argument he had not thought of; the limits on invective; the discipline of the rapid exchange; and the orator's life as evidence, where the formula of the good man skilled in speaking is **Cato's**, quoted rather than coined. Seneca the Elder's declamation collections preserve epigrams, divisions and favourable constructions, the last being the training material for reframing what cannot be denied.

Tacitus, *Dialogus* — eloquence as the child of licence and disorder.

**Fault catalogue.** *Ad Herennium* I.11 on the faulty exordium; Quintilian VIII.3 on *vitia* with perverse affectation as the worst of them, VI.3 on the faults of wit, VI.4 on faults in the rapid exchange, XI.3 on faults of delivery.

**Principal contribution.** S3 and S8 both rest on *Ad Herennium* I.11 — the first for its warning against the opening too elaborately worked up, the second for its two symmetry faults. S4 rests on Quintilian VI.4.

## Byzantium

The compilation on court order rests on the premise that ritual is statement rather than ornament. The excerpts on embassies constitute a precedent file, which is a negotiating advantage before either party speaks. Liudprand's *Antapodosis* records the mechanical throne of the 949 embassy; the *Relatio* of 968 records the deliberate humiliation and its aftermath — the leaking and waterless lodging, the undrinkable wine, the seating below a Bulgarian envoy, the friction over Otto's imperial title and a papal letter addressing Nikephoros as emperor of the Greeks. The *Strategikon* attributed to Maurice, whose authorship is disputed, treats the character and handling of various peoples in its eleventh book. Kekaumenos advises standing through inconspicuousness. Payments made to avoid war were recorded as imperial largesse.

**Fault catalogue.** *Strategikon* XI as a catalogue of ways to mishandle each people; the *Relatio* as a first-person failure narrative, which is why it is poor history and excellent evidence of practice.

**Principal contribution.** S2 (ceremonial as a latitude-reducing design); S7 (the tribute fiction as a device that keeps a failure from becoming terminal); S4 (Liudprand's silence in the room, and the relocation afterwards).

## The Carolingian West

Hincmar's *De ordine palatii* of 882, claiming Adalhard of Corbie — the two-tier assembly and the secrecy of counsel, possibly prescriptive rather than descriptive. The consent of the faithful, which must be **seen**, so that identical content carries different force. Alcuin's letters as a working method for admonishing a king: establish affection, praise the specific and recent, locate the fault in the counsellors rather than the king, close on duty owed to God rather than to the writer. Dhuoda's manual of 841–43, rare in instructing a young person directly in survival among the great. Nithard on the oaths sworn at Strasbourg in 842, where each king swore in the other's vernacular so that each army heard its own lord's brother bind himself — audience-fitting as the entire content of the act. Einhard on Charlemagne hearing envoys while dressing. The royal envoys sent in pairs.

**Fault catalogue.** Hincmar cc. XIII–XVI on the fault of leaked counsel, which is stated with its consequence: resentment, and revolt.

**Principal contribution.** S2 (secret counsel as a design that lets participants disagree without any being publicly defeated); D4 on both axes.

## Persia and the Islamic world

Sasanian counsel literature — the letter attributed to Tansar, the testament attributed to Ardashir. Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ's essay on the caliph's entourage, and *Kalīla wa Dimna*, translated from Pahlavi out of the Sanskrit, which is at once an instance of indirect counsel and a treatise about it, and whose trial of Dimna is a complete forensic drama. Nizām al-Mulk's book of government of 1086–91, with its chapter on intelligence agents and its chapter on ambassadors, who come also to observe roads, fortifications, the army and the ruler's character. Kai Kāvus's book of 1082 on measured speech — saying less than one knows, not answering at once, never letting the pleasure of a good remark override its cost. Al-Jāhiz on eloquence as fitness of speech to occasion, subject and the listener's standing. Ibn Khaldūn on solidarity, prestige, the four-generation decay, and the courtier's exposure.

**Fault catalogue.** The *Qābūs-nāma*'s chapters on faults of speech; the fault of speech unfitted to the listener; and Nizām al-Mulk's method, in which the anecdote is usually a failure narrative and the criticism travels inside it.

**Principal contribution.** S1 (Ibn Khaldūn's account of standing as borrowed and decaying); S3 (the anecdote as a device that lets the ruler take or leave the application); S2 (the envoy as observer, feeding row 3 of the requirement table).

## The Ottoman court

The advice-book sequence: Lütfi Pasha's *Āsafnāme* of about 1541, written after dismissal and drawing on al-Māwardī; the anonymous work of about 1574; Mustafa ʿĀlī's counsel for sultans of 1581; Hasan Kāfī's work of 1596; Koçi Bey's treatise of the 1630s. Characteristically addressed to the vizier rather than the sultan, since advising the deputy is safer than advising the sovereign. The circle of justice as the standard argumentative form — no sovereignty without an army, no army without wealth, no wealth without subjects, no subjects without justice, no justice without sovereignty — transmitted through Ṭūsī and the pseudo-Aristotelian *Secret of Secrets* from Sasanian sources and given an Ottoman formulation by Kınalızāde in 1564, **not originated by him.** Its value is that it lets a counsellor say that subjects are being oppressed as a technical observation about revenue. Ceremonial: a brief and largely silent audience, business through the grand vizier, the sultan able to observe the council unseen; envoys held by the arms in the sultan's presence, traditionally explained by the assassination of Murad I at Kosovo in 1389, an explanation widely repeated and **not securely evidenced**. The written summary as counsel removed from face-to-face exchange.

**Fault catalogue.** The genre itself. The advice book exists to name what has gone wrong in the administration, and its authors are frequently men recently dismissed.

**Principal contribution.** S2 (the written summary; the silent sovereign; the fullest case of protection maximized and challenge minimized); S3 (the circle of justice as indirection with a fixed argumentative form).

## China

The *Zuo zhuan* and *Guoyu* on recitation of the *Odes* at diplomatic meetings — ritual obligation and strategic move at once, resting on a shared canon that makes the coded claim legible and deniable together. *Analects* 13.5, where the man who can recite all three hundred but cannot handle an independent embassy is judged useless. The distrust of glibness and the praise of slowness of speech; trustworthiness as the ground of standing. Mencius leading King Xuan from the king's own recorded pity for a sacrificial ox, and his apology at 3B.9 for using an art he distrusts. Xunzi on the rectification of names and on the manner of persuasion — dignity in approach, sincerity, firmness, analogy for clarity, distinction for plainness. Han Feizi on the difficulty of persuasion, the scale that grows backwards, and Mi Zixia. The *Guiguzi*, whose date is contested, with its loop of opening and closing, provoking and observing, estimating and probing, and weighing words. The remonstrance offices, and Wu Jing's compilation of about 720 as the casebook. Sunzi on not pressing a surrounded enemy, and on spies.

**Fault catalogue.** Han Feizi 3, which demonstrates that seven manners of speaking each invite a specific misreading, and Han Feizi 12, which enumerates the ways a persuader dies. *Analects* 1.3 on clever words and an ingratiating expression. And the inaptly chosen ode, whose failure exposes the state's cultivation.

**Principal contribution.** S3 (the register matrix — the single most important fault catalogue in the survey); S5 (the *Guiguzi*, the only text naming elicitation); S1 (Mi Zixia as the limiting case of reception as position).

## Japan

The Seventeen Articles — traditionally dated 604, with authenticity and dating **long contested** and substantial scholarship placing composition nearer the compilation of the *Nihon Shoki* in 720. The first article on harmony, the seventeenth on not deciding weighty matters alone, and the tenth on not resenting another for differing, since we are all merely ordinary men and neither is certainly wise nor certainly foolish. The council of state established in 1225 with its oath of impartial judgment, operating under the code of 1232, deciding by consensus rather than declared majority. House precepts: Hōjō Shigetoki in the thirteenth century, Imagawa Ryōshun in 1412, among the most copied texts in medieval Japan. Zeami — the flower as novelty and the three-part rhythm developed across the treatise of about 1400–18 and its successors, with the sight of detached seeing and the interval of no-action belonging to the treatise of 1424. One meeting and one chance: the substance is traceable to notes of 1588, the fixed four-character phrase to the nineteenth century.

**Fault catalogue.** Zeami on the temporary flower of youth mistaken for the true one, and on performing above one's attained rank; the tenth article's named fault of resenting difference.

**Principal contribution.** S6 (the only tradition claiming a training, and the high-validity environment that explains why); S7 (the tenth article as a device that denies either party's standing is at stake); S4 (the interval of no-action).

## The Latin Renaissance

Castiglione — concealed effort makes what is done appear effortless, and by that concealment produces grace, with visible effort as the cardinal fault. Machiavelli — reputation running on what men see and few touch; hatred as the ruin of standing where fear is not; matching one's proceeding to the times; the impossibility of changing one's nature; and the memorandum to Raffaello Girolami of October 1522 on being thought liberal, honest and wise because the reputation buys access and information. Guicciardini — discretion as the judgment of the particular case that no maxim reaches, and the reserve of the man who does not answer. The diplomatic treatises: du Rosier in 1436, Barbaro about 1490, Gentili in 1585, Hotman in 1603. Commynes — two great princes should never meet in person. The distinction between feigning and concealing; Accetto in 1641; Gracián in 1647; Callières in 1716 as the tradition's terminus.

**Fault catalogue.** Visible effort as the single named fault of the courtier, defined as the negation of the virtue it accompanies. Guicciardini's *Ricordi*, which is largely a catalogue of the ways judgment goes wrong — generalizing from one's own experience, trusting too far, taking half-measures. Machiavelli to Girolami on the envoy's faults: writing home badly, being thought mean and double-faced, being caught concealing.

**Principal contribution.** S3 (visible effort as the overshoot fault, and its Roman ancestry); S6 (the fixity position, and the institutional conclusion drawn from it); S2 (standing as a stock to be filled).

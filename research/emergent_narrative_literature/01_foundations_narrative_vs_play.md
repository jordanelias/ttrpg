### T04_juul2002 — Jesper Juul (2002). *The Open and the Closed: Games of Emergence and Games of Progression*
- **Read:** P (full) · conference paper · Computer Games and Digital Cultures Conference Proceedings (Tampere, 2002) · skipped: reference list, footnotes only
- **Thesis:** Most computer games combine two ways of presenting challenge: emergence (few simple rules that combine into large variation) and progression (challenges delivered serially by the designer). Emergence is the structurally more interesting one and is a third way between "open" and "closed"; storytelling ambition lives in progression, not in emergence.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Emergence is defined as a small rule set that combines to yield many game variations for which players devise strategies; progression as a predefined set of actions the player must perform to complete the game (§Emergence and progression).
  2. Progression gives the designer strong control over event sequence, which is where cinematic or storytelling ambitions are found, at the price of playing "on a rail" and very low replayability (§Emergence and progression; §Progression).
  3. Emergence is anchored in Holland's whole-exceeds-sum idea and Bedau's weak emergence (derivable only by simulation); Juul rejects unpredicted by the designer as a criterion because it is a biographical, intentional-fallacy test (§Introducing emergence; §Emergence in games).
  4. He splits emergence into rule interaction (not really emergence), combination (the variety of sessions the rules allow) and emergent strategies (not deducible from the rules) (§Emergence in games).
  5. An open rule system is still patterned: players respect a game contract and pursue the goal, so outcomes such as Counter-strike fights or Monopoly bankruptcy occur though no rule states them (§The determination of an open world).
  6. EverQuest is an emergence game with embedded progression structures; emergence yields group play, while the social and cultural characterisation of the world is delivered through progression-style quests (§Analysing EverQuest).
- **Argument structure:** Premise: rules are simple yet play is complex, so a framework is needed for how rules shape sessions. Move: import "emergence" from complexity science, tighten it so that exploits easily derivable from rules do not count, then contrast it with a serial-challenge structure. The framework is tested on one case (EverQuest) and a rule of thumb (walkthrough versus strategy guide). Evidence is conceptual analysis plus examples; no data. Weakest link: the emergence/progression line is drawn by a proxy (guide type) and one case, and the claim that storytelling belongs to progression is asserted, not shown.
- **Key terms:**
  - `emergence` — a few simple rules combining into a large, non-obviously derived space of variation that players answer with strategies.
  - `progression` — separate challenges presented serially, with all options and solutions explicit in the rules.
  - `emergent strategies` — the genuinely emergent tier: properties not immediately deducible from the rules.
  - `game contract` — players' tacit agreement to pursue the goal, which makes open systems patterned.
  - `weak emergence` — higher level derivable from lower only by simulation (after Bedau).
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "this is also where we find the games with cinematic or storytelling ambitions" (§Emergence and progression)
  - "Everything that happens in a branching narrative is explicitly pre-determined." (§Progression)
  - "Progression games have walkthroughs: lists of actions to perform to complete the game." (§Conclusion)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T04_juul2002.Q1** *Name the guide a player would write: a walkthrough (ordered list of actions) or a strategy guide (rules of thumb)?* Passes if the guide for the story-bearing content is a strategy guide, or the design states which parts are walkthrough-shaped and which are not. Fails if story beats can only be reached by one ordered action list while the design claims open-ended emergence. Detects: progression disguised as emergence.
  - **T04_juul2002.Q2** *For each claimed "emergent" behaviour, can it be derived directly from a single rule, or does it need simulation or strategy to discover?* Passes if the claimed emergent items are not trivially derivable (not mere rule interaction) and are tagged by tier. Fails if the emergence list consists of rule interactions or exploits. Detects: emergence inflation.
  - **T04_juul2002.Q3** *Which layer carries characterisation and plot: authored sequences or rule-generated variation, and is that split stated?* Passes if the design states where authored sequences end and rule-generated variation begins. Fails if the design promises story from rules but delivers characterisation through hand-placed quests, or the reverse, without saying so. Detects: undeclared hybrid structure.
  - **T04_juul2002.Q4** *Run two sessions with different players and compare the outcome patterns: does play converge on the same shape because of the goal?* Passes if convergence is identified and judged as interesting or dull. Fails if the design assumes full freedom with no analysis of dominant strategies. Detects: unexamined dominant-strategy collapse.
- **Does not license:** Written for 2002 MMO and shooter play; no empirical test; emergence is more interesting is a value judgment. Says nothing about generated narrative text, only about structure of challenge. The three-tier split is thinly argued (a few examples each).
- **Links:** builds on Holland, Bedau, Smith (cited in text). Disputed/extended by T05_jenkins2004 and T07_ryan2001 `[reader's inference]`.
- **Prior-claim check:** none given; definitions and storytelling effects stated above (§Emergence and progression).

### T06_juul2001 — Jesper Juul (2001). *Games Telling Stories? A brief note on games and narratives*
- **Read:** P (full) · journal article · Game Studies 1(1), July 2001 · skipped: author blurb, site navigation, reference list (endnotes read)
- **Thesis:** Games and narratives are related (players retell sessions, games contain cut-scenes and back-stories) but cannot be treated as one thing: games do not translate to stories the way novels translate to films, their time is a synchronous "now" that conflicts with narrative's "prior", and the player's relation to the game world differs from a reader's relation to a story. The aim is to make distinctions, not to protect a word.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. The common arguments for games-as-narrative (everything is narrative; back-stories; shared traits) are a priori or partial: being presentable as narrative does not make something narrative (§Telling stories).
  2. Narrative is defined by transposability across media (existents plus events), so a medium test is whether stories can be retold in games and games in other media; Star Wars and Mortal Kombat examples show the events survive only as simulations with win/fail outcomes, and retelling a game keeps existents and events but loses the dynamic system (§The problem of translation).
  3. Games produce existents endlessly and rule-hierarchies decide which attributes matter ("rules of irrelevance"), unlike narrative existents (§Translating what?).
  4. Narrative holds a gap between story time and discourse time; in a playable game the two converge with playing time, so flashback and flash-forward are almost absent and games are almost always chronological (§Time, game, and narrative).
  5. Games can be abstract (Tetris, Missile Command) because the player is the constant actant, motivated by the game evaluating performance; goals must be ones a player would conceivably work for (§The player and the game).
  6. Experimental narrative does not rescue the equation: stories have an interpretative dominant, games a configurative one, and interpretive instability would make a game unplayable (§The avant-garde fallacy).
- **Argument structure:** He weighs three pro-narrative arguments, concedes a weaker relation than his earlier thesis, then offers three structural differences (translation, time, player relation) and pre-empts the avant-garde counterexample. The evidence is conceptual analysis using narratology (Chatman, Genette) and a handful of canonical game examples. The time argument is the load-bearing step: interactivity needs a not-yet-determined future, narration needs a determined past. Weakest link: the argument leans on classic, linear-media definitions of narrative, which he admits ("fairly traditional definitions"), and so is silent on narratives built after play from a record.
- **Key terms:**
  - `existents` / `events` — Chatman's story parts: actors and settings; actions and happenings.
  - `story time` / `discourse time` — time of events told versus time of the telling; in games these merge with playing time.
  - `ideal story` — the sequence a game's packaging or back-story asks the player to realise; few play sessions follow it.
  - `interpretative` vs `configurative dominant` — what the reader/player chiefly does (Eskelinen, cited).
  - `rules of irrelevance` — Goffman: rules fix which features of an object count (cited).
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "Obviously, only a microscopic fraction of the play sessions actually follow the ideal path" (§Ideal stories / back-stories)
  - "the story time is now." (§Time in the computer game)
  - "there is no such thing as a continuously interactive story" (§Conclusion)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T06_juul2001.Q1** *Take a retelling of one session (log, chronicle, summary): does it keep the existents and events and, if so, what is lost?* Passes if the design says which of the dynamic system, rule hierarchy and player agency are absent from the retelling and does not present it as the experience itself. Fails if the retelling is offered as equivalent to play, or only recounts moves ("and then I ... and then I ..."). Detects: translation loss ignored.
  - **T06_juul2001.Q2** *At the moment the player can act, is any story element presented as already happened or fixed?* Passes if narrated or fixed content sits only at points where the player cannot act (cut-scenes, after-action text), or is not claimed to be interactive. Fails if a design claims continuous interactive narration while the told events are already determined. Detects: interactivity/narration conflation.
  - **T06_juul2001.Q3** *If the design offers an ideal sequence, can it be failed, and how often would a typical session follow it?* Passes if the ideal path is optional and non-ideal sessions yield tellable outcomes. Fails if only the ideal path produces story. Detects: ideal-story monopoly.
  - **T06_juul2001.Q4** *Does the player's goal match something a player would plausibly work toward, with evaluation of performance?* Passes if goals are legible and attainable by player effort. Fails if the narrative requires goals the player has no reason to pursue. Detects: story-goal/player-goal mismatch.
- **Does not license:** Brief polemical note, 2001, built on arcade and shooter examples and traditional narratology; it does not treat narrative generated from simulation state, or multi-player intrigue beyond one endnote. Later work (e.g. T07, T05) contests its narrow definition `[reader's inference]`. Evidence is illustrative, not empirical.
- **Links:** cites Ryan (2001, the T07 talk) in note 8; builds on Chatman, Genette, Aarseth, Eskelinen. Opposed by Murray-style narrativism (cited in text).
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - "games are present-tense, so recount-prose suits only a past-tense chronicle": CORRECTED. Present-tense is CONFIRMED (§Time in the computer game: story time is now, not yet determined; games are almost always chronological). The conclusion about prose is NOT-IN-SOURCE: Juul says retellings keep existents and events but lose the dynamic system and are often dull if detailed (§From game to story), and that players can retell sessions (§Is this it?); he does not discuss chronicle prose or tense choice for it.

### T07_ryan2001 — Marie-Laure Ryan (2001). *Beyond Myth and Metaphor: The Case of Narrative in Digital Media*
- **Read:** P (full) · journal article (keynote text) · Game Studies 1(1), July 2001 · skipped: author blurb, reference list (endnotes read)
- **Thesis:** Claims for narrative in digital media rest on two over-promising myths (hypertext as an Aleph of infinite stories; the Holodeck as live-generated drama). Narrative is a medium-free mental representation, but each medium suits only some story types, and the form of interactivity (internal/external by exploratory/ontological) fixes which narrative possibilities are open; games hold only a "virtual" narrativity realised in retelling.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Narrative is defined semantically and medium-free, as a world situated in time with characters, events and change, built in the audience's mind from a text, a matter of degree, thematically unified and logically coherent (§What is narrative?).
  2. The Aleph myth fails because fragments cannot be freely permuted: they are bound by presupposition, causality and temporal order, so a reader assembles one picture over many sessions (like a jigsaw), not a new story per traversal (§Hypertext, and the myth of the Aleph).
  3. The Holodeck is doubtful on technology, algorithm (real-time plot generation around unpredictable actions) and above all psychology: first-person experience of tragic plots is undesirable, so playable characters tend to be flat problem-solvers (§VR narrative, and the myth of the Holodeck).
  4. Digital media's distinctive resource is responding to changing conditions; four interactivity forms (external-exploratory, internal-exploratory, external-ontological, internal-ontological) afford different plots, and coherence is kept by few branches, narrow action ranges or simulation state (§VR narrative; Groups 1-4).
  5. In simulation games the options available depend only on current world state, so a coherent menu is easy to compute, whereas in classical narrative possible futures depend on the whole past (§Group 3).
  6. Games are not played for the replay; narrative in them is instrumental (lures the player into the world), is told retrospectively while play is prospective, and exists as potential dramatic narrative realised only if retold (§Conclusion).
- **Argument structure:** She starts from the software industry's narrative metaphors, gives a definition, then tests two idealised "myths" against logical coherence and psychology, and finally builds a four-cell typology of interactivity (adapted from Aarseth) with a genre assigned to each cell. Evidence is conceptual analysis with illustrative works (Myst, Choose Your Own Adventure, SimCity, Combat Mission review). The conclusion extends narratology with a third, game-specific "virtual" mode. Weakest link: the final category is argued by analogy (diegetic to dramatic to game) and rests on a single newspaper retelling; the claim that tragedy is unplayable is a personal preference.
- **Key terms:**
  - `narrativity` — medium-free semantic property of a mental representation, a matter of degree.
  - `internal / external interactivity` — user projected as a member of the world versus outside it (god, coach, navigator).
  - `exploratory / ontological` — moves that do not alter the world's history versus decisions that fork it.
  - `virtual history narratives` — outcomes of running a system with changed parameters and contemplating the field of possibilities.
  - `narrative myths` — idealised claims (Aleph, Holodeck) that raise false expectations.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "Narrative representation is constructed by the reader on the basis of the text." (§What is narrative?)
  - "computer games, like sports, are not played for the sake of watching the replay" (§Conclusion)
  - "Games thus embody a virtualized, or potential dramatic narrativity" (§Conclusion)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T07_ryan2001.Q1** *Place the design in the internal/external by exploratory/ontological grid; does the plot structure it promises fit that cell's affordances?* Passes if the stated story types (e.g. emergent chronicle for external-ontological, quest for internal-ontological) are those the cell supports. Fails if it promises a coherent authored plot from a cell that cannot guarantee one (e.g. dense free choice with external position). Detects: interactivity/plot mismatch.
  - **T07_ryan2001.Q2** *When two world-states can follow from one choice, what stops the resulting event sequence from being incoherent (dead character acting, effect before cause)?* Passes if coherence is secured by state-dependent option menus, limited branching or constraint on action range, and this is demonstrable in a log. Fails if narrative output is permutable fragments with no ordering constraints. Detects: Aleph fallacy (permutation without coherence).
  - **T07_ryan2001.Q3** *Is the player's position in the plot one they would want to occupy first-hand?* Passes if the player's involvement is problem-solving, exploration or command rather than forced suffering. Fails if the design's best stories require the player to endure tragic fates as an agent. Detects: Holodeck psychology gap.
  - **T07_ryan2001.Q4** *Is narrative content treated as the point of play or as a prop, and does the design's measure of success match?* Passes if narrative is evaluated by whether it supports immersion and is retellable after play. Fails if session quality is judged by tellability while moment-to-moment play is goal-driven. Detects: narrative as aesthetic end versus instrument confusion.
- **Does not license:** Written when games and hypertext were the cases; no discussion of procedural text generation beyond the Holodeck's algorithmic doubts. Evidence is illustrative. Her tragedy argument is preference-based. The simulation-game passage is only a few paragraphs.
- **Links:** cites Aarseth (typology adapted), Murray (Holodeck), Landow (Aleph). Cited by T06_juul2001 (endnote 8, same conference). Compatible with the weak-narrativity stance of T06 and T04 `[reader's inference]`.
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - "story is a cognitive construct": CONFIRMED. Narrative representation is built by the reader from the text (§What is narrative?), and narrativity is called a cognitive pattern or mental representation independent of medium (§VR narrative, Holodeck). On interactivity: she says it is the response of a medium to user input and distinguishes four forms; she does not treat it as a feature that facilitates narrative meaning (§Group 4).

### T05_jenkins2004 — Henry Jenkins (2004). *Game Design as Narrative Architecture*
- **Read:** P (full) · book chapter / essay (First Person, 2004; text is the web essay) · skipped: endnotes only skimmed, publisher footer
- **Thesis:** The ludology-versus-narratology fight misframes the question: games are less stories than spaces ripe with narrative possibility, so designers are narrative architects whose spatial choices shape story in four ways (evoke, stage, embed, enable emergence). Narrative theory remains useful if the model of narrative is widened beyond classical linear plot.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Five points of agreement concede much to the ludologists: not all games tell stories, play is never reducible to story, and stories do not port unchanged between media (§opening list, points 1-5).
  2. The ludologist case rests on four blind spots: too narrow a model of narrative, too little attention to narrative comprehension, a whole-game rather than local view, and an assumption that narratives must be self-contained (§opening).
  3. Game design is chiefly world and space design (level design, maps, board and dungeon), and games sit in an older tradition of spatial stories (quests, odysseys) in which plot yields to exploration and episode (§Spatial stories and environmental storytelling).
  4. Environmental storytelling works in four ways: evocative spaces (borrow existing associations), enacted stories (staging grounds with broad goals plus local micronarratives in an "accordion" plot), embedded narratives (pre-structured information distributed in mise-en-scene for the player to reconstruct), and emergent narratives (resources for player-constructed stories) (§Evocative Spaces; §Enacting Stories; §Embedded Narratives; §Emergent Narratives).
  5. Story can be treated as a body of information rather than a temporal structure; because players may miss any element, essential information must be redundantly distributed, and past events can be shown by traces in the environment, which also answers Juul's no-flashback claim (§Embedded Narratives).
  6. The Sims illustrates emergence: authored needs, desires, conflicting drives, and a stripped-down, legible object set make narrative likely without pre-scripting it (§Emergent Narratives).
- **Argument structure:** Opens with four hostile quotations, concedes common ground, then reframes with a third term (space) and builds a four-part typology from theme-park design (Carson), film theory and four game examples per mode. Evidence is conceptual argument and illustrative cases (Myst, The Sims, Alice, Majestic); no data. The typology is exhaustive only by assertion: the four categories overlap (his own Group example blends enacted and embedded). Weakest link: the emergent category is argued from one title and a designer's remark plus fan scrapbooks, with satisfaction asserted ("most players").
- **Key terms:**
  - `narrative architect` — a designer who shapes narrative possibility through spatial and world design rather than telling a plot.
  - `environmental storytelling` — story information carried by the physical space (after Carson).
  - `micronarrative` — a short, localised narrative unit, like a Battleship Potemkin "attraction", that shapes emotional experience without a master plot.
  - `embedded narrative` — pre-structured story distributed through the environment for the player to reassemble.
  - `emergent narrative` — story that takes shape through play, neither pre-programmed nor unstructured.
  - `accordion structure` — fixed plot points with expandable or contractible moments between them.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "examining games less as stories than as spaces ripe with narrative possibility" (§opening)
  - "a story is less a temporal structure than a body of information" (§Embedded Narratives)
  - "Games are no more locked into an eternal present than films are always linear." (§Embedded Narratives)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T05_jenkins2004.Q1** *For each story the design aims to deliver, which of evoke, enact, embed, emerge carries it, and what in the design (space, objects, authored events, rules) does the carrying?* Passes if every intended story beat is mapped to a mode and a named carrier in the design. Fails if story is said to arise with no carrier, or if every beat is authored text with no spatial or systemic carrier. Detects: unattributed narrative.
  - **T05_jenkins2004.Q2** *If a player misses or reorders any one element, is the essential information still delivered elsewhere?* Passes if each essential fact appears in at least two independent places or channels, and the design tolerates reorder of episodes. Fails if a single missed object breaks comprehension of the plot. Detects: single-point information loss in non-linear play.
  - **T05_jenkins2004.Q3** *In an emergent mode, list the authored ingredients that make narrative likely (conflicting drives, legible objects, consequence traces). Which are present?* Passes if the design names conflicting needs or goals for agents, consequences that persist, and a limited legible vocabulary of objects or events. Fails if the design relies on players will make their own stories without those ingredients (blank page). Detects: sandbox-without-hooks.
  - **T05_jenkins2004.Q4** *Do past events leave readable traces in the world that later play can encounter?* Passes if an earlier event changes what the player later sees or meets. Fails if the past exists only in a log or menu. Detects: invisible history.
- **Does not license:** A 2004 position essay, partly a polemic aimed at ludologists; typology is illustrative and the modes overlap. Spatial emphasis fits 3D and exploration games; applies to strategy or systemic games only by analogy. He accepts the four modes as his own scheme, not a test. Treats player satisfaction as self-evident.
- **Links:** disputes T06_juul2001 (flashback and Star Wars adaptation points, explicitly) and the Juul/Adams/Costikyan/Eskelinen quotations; draws on Murray (procedural authorship), Carson, Lynch; T04 not cited `[reader's inference]`.
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - "four modes: evocative / enacted / embedded / emergent narratives; designers build spaces that afford story": CONFIRMED with wording CORRECTED. The introductory list gives four functions (evoke pre-existing associations; staging ground where events are enacted; embed information in mise-en-scene; resources for emergent narratives), and the section headings are "Evocative Spaces", "Enacting Stories", "Embedded Narratives", "Emergent Narratives"; the closing summary says evoked, enacted, embedded, emergent. "Narrative architects" and "spaces ripe with narrative possibility" are his phrasing (§opening; closing paragraph).

### T01_aylett1999 — Ruth Aylett (1999). *Narrative in Virtual Environments — Towards Emergent Narrative*
- **Read:** P (full) · conference paper (symposium position paper, 4 pages, "preliminary thoughts") · AAAI Fall Symposium on Narrative Intelligence, Technical Report FS-99-01 · skipped: reference list. Extraction lost word spaces and the last page is out of order (a conclusion fragment sits mid-text); quotes below avoid garbled spans.
- **Thesis:** Pre-scripted narrative clashes with the freedom a virtual environment gives the user. Instead of scripting the top of the narrative hierarchy, build characters bottom-up from drives and behaviours so that narrative emerges from their interaction, and use "social presence" (social pressure as an extension of physical presence) to steer the user toward behaviour that helps a narrative emerge.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Moving a fully scripted TV narrative into a virtual environment removes what makes the environment different (individual interaction, choice of viewpoint), and giving users authoring tools does not remove the need to exploit the environment's own characteristics (§A Narrative Problem).
  2. Narrative can be modelled as a hierarchy (overall plot, character-level abstract action sequences, physical behaviour), with scripting ranging from fully fixed to improvised; improvisation in virtual environments exists mainly at the lowest level (§Narrative approaches).
  3. Behavioural architectures create complexity bottom up, so the hierarchy can be read bottom-up and narrative at the plot level may arise from interaction of simple components (§Narrative approaches; §Emergent Narrative).
  4. Football and free dramatic improvisation show narrative arising where characters, relationships, conflicting aims and constraints are specified but no plot is; this needs enough dynamic events outside the characters' control and a sufficiently rich set of behaviours, not defined here (§Emergent Narrative).
  5. Emergent narrative is fragile and small-scale: it may not emerge, it runs in continuous time with no way to skip boring parts, and scene-breaks would require computing off-stage activity for the agents (§Emergent Narrative).
  6. Games do not solve the user's role (adventure games disconnect story from player action; arcade games lack plot; undo destroys coherence), so social presence is proposed to communicate appropriate behaviour (§The role of the user).
- **Argument structure:** A design problem from one project (Virtual Teletubbies) is generalised through a narrative-level hierarchy; the move is to reverse the hierarchy's direction of control, justify it with analogies (football, improvisation, the Noo-noo and cloud episode), then list the problems that arise (fragility, scale, user role) and propose social presence. There is no evaluation: the paper says it reports thoughts, not completed work. Weakest link: "sufficiently rich" behaviour is left undefined, and the claim that narrative emerges is supported by a thought example, not a run.
- **Key terms:**
  - `emergent narrative` — narrative arising bottom-up from interaction of characters, with no pre-given plot; the paper uses and discusses it but does not claim to have named it.
  - `narrative hierarchy` — plot / character-level action sequences / physical behaviour (cognitive or reactive).
  - `social presence` — illusion of social location, by which social convention or pressure conveys the behaviour expected of the user.
  - `virtual actor` — a character that carries out narrative actions in the environment.
  - `internal drives` — hunger, fatigue, curiosity: the state that activates behaviour groups.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "narrative is emergent, since it has emerged from human life experience" (§Emergent Narrative)
  - "one of the risks of emergent narrative is that it may not emerge" (§Emergent Narrative)
  - "Social presence is therefore an extension of physical presence" (§The role of the user)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T01_aylett1999.Q1** *At which level of the plot / action-sequence / behaviour hierarchy is the design's variation generated, and which levels are fixed?* Passes if the design states the level of authoring and the level of generation and shows that the generating level produces observable variation in logs. Fails if all variation is at physical-behaviour level (cosmetic) while the plot is fixed, yet claimed as emergent. Detects: emergence at the wrong level.
  - **T01_aylett1999.Q2** *Which characters have conflicting drives, specified relationships and events outside their control, and in a play log do these produce a recognisable arc (precipitating event, conflict, resolution) without authored plot?* Passes if at least one traced episode meets all three ingredients, and the log reports how often arcs fail to appear. Fails if the log shows long runs with no narrative structure ("boring football matches") and no measure of that rate. Detects: unmeasured emergence rate.
  - **T01_aylett1999.Q3** *Can the user undo or skip consequences, and does narrative coherence survive?* Passes if consequences persist across the player's actions, or the design says what coherence it forgoes. Fails if undo or reload makes the recorded narrative path meaningless to the player. Detects: undo undermining coherence.
  - **T01_aylett1999.Q4** *What tells the player which behaviour would help a story emerge (briefing, social pressure, role cues)?* Passes if a named mechanism communicates expected conduct. Fails if the player must guess which actions fit. Detects: user-role gap.
- **Does not license:** Four-page speculative paper in 1999 with a children's-TV test case; no evaluation; rich enough never defined. Its football and improvisation examples are intuition. Emergence here is a systems-architecture claim, not a storytelling theory. `[reader's inference]` later work in this area gives it fuller form.
- **Links:** builds on behavioural robot architecture (Barnes 1996), Hayes-Roth's Virtual Theater, IMPROV (cited in text); no corpus ID cited.
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - "coined the term 'emergent narrative' in 1999": NOT-IN-SOURCE as a coinage claim. The paper uses and briefly defines the term (§Emergent Narrative; abstract) and calls the combination with social presence "little explored" (§Conclusion), but never claims to have introduced the term; it reads the existing idea of emergence from behaviour architectures into narrative.
  - "introduced the 'narrative paradox' (player freedom vs. narrative coherence)": CORRECTED. The phrase "narrative paradox" does not appear. The abstract states a "clash" between pre-scripted narrative and VE freedom, and the text notes undo undermines narrative coherence (§The role of the user). The word "paradoxical" is used only about emergent narrative itself seeming to lack a needed plot (§Emergent Narrative).

### T02_mateas2001 — Michael Mateas (2001). *A Preliminary Poetics for Interactive Drama and Games*
- **Read:** P (full) · journal article · Digital Creativity 12(3), pp. 140–152 · skipped: reference list. The abstract is lost in extraction and a few paragraphs in §4.4–5.1 are interleaved by the two-column layout; the argument was reconstructed from the readable text.
- **Thesis:** Player agency, the feeling of acting with effects that match one's intention, arises when the material resources available for action (what the world, characters, objects and interface afford) are balanced against the formal constraints from the plot (what the story makes dramatically probable). That balance gives a prescriptive model for interactive drama and a diagnostic for any interactive experience.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Interactive drama is defined as first-person enactment of a unified, intensified plot; it is a deliberate niche among interactive stories, most of which have been episodic, hypertextual or socially constructed (§2).
  2. Murray's three categories (immersion, agency, transformation) are phenomenological; agency is primary because the other two already exist in Aristotle's model, and agency is the new experience interactivity provides (§3, §4.1).
  3. Aristotle's six parts of drama (plot, character, thought, language, pattern, spectacle) are linked by two causal chains: the formal chain runs from the author's plot downward; the material chain is what the audience experiences and climbs upward to recover the plot (§4.2).
  4. Adding a player adds two causal chains at the level of character: the player's intentions become a new source of formal causation, constrained from below by material resources (affordances) and from above by plot-level dramatic probability (§4.3).
  5. Agency requires balance: many options with no plot-given reason to choose (Zork Grand Inquisitor) or rich plot with poor action means (menu-only dialogue) both reduce agency, while Quake's proto-plot and affordances match; adding more of either side can leave the experience out of its "sweet spot" (§4.4, §6.1).
  6. Agency (first-person, moment-by-moment) conflicts with transformation-as-variety (third-person, across replays); the proposed resolution is a plot that is smoothly mutable in response to global state, so each run has a unitary plot and different runs differ, with no visible branch points (§5.2).
- **Argument structure:** Premise: a theory is missing that guides what to build and how. Moves: take Murray's categories and Laurel's Aristotelian structure; define material and formal cause; insert the player as a character; derive agency as a balance condition; test it by examples (Zork, Quake) and by the conflict with replay variety. The conclusion feeds a technical agenda (believable agents, natural-language dialogue, coordinated plot/character) and a project (Façade) built on it. Evidence is conceptual analysis with illustrative examples, no study of players. Weakest link: "balance" is not measured or operationalised, and the replay-variety solution is asserted, not demonstrated.
- **Key terms:**
  - `formal cause` — the abstract plan the work is heading toward; in drama, the author's view: plot, down to characters, thought, language, pattern and spectacle.
  - `material cause` — what the thing is made of; the audience view, from spectacle up to plot, and in interaction the resources that supply actions.
  - `agency` — the feeling of empowerment from taking actions whose effects relate to the player's intention; not mere interface activity.
  - `dramatic probability` — the plot-derived sense of which actions are worth considering; it supplies motivation.
  - `affordance` — a material resource that makes an action obvious as well as possible (positive constraint).
  - `proto-plot` — top-down expectations set by design alone, in a game with no plot or characters in the strict sense.

- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T02_mateas2001.Q1** *For a given decision point, list the actions the world makes available and the reasons the story/goal structure gives to prefer one over another.* Passes if each available action is tied to a stated goal, and the goals rank them. Fails if there are many actions and no basis for choosing between them (material surplus), or a strong goal and no action that serves it (formal surplus). Detects: material/formal imbalance.
  - **T02_mateas2001.Q2** *Does a player action change the thing the player intended to change, in a way the player could predict?* Passes if the effect of an action corresponds to its stated intent in a trace. Fails if the player can twiddle controls with little effect on the experience, or effects are not the ones intended. Detects: false agency.
  - **T02_mateas2001.Q3** *Run the same design with different early player choices: do plots differ in a way not traceable to visible branch points, and does each run still end with a coherent shape?* Passes if different early choices lead to different coherent endings and later choices matter less as the possibilities narrow. Fails if runs converge to the same plot (inconsequential play) or diverge into incoherence. Detects: agency/variety conflict unresolved.
  - **T02_mateas2001.Q4** *Can the player see why computer-controlled characters act as they do and how it relates to the plot?* Passes if characters communicate motive (action expression), so the player can reason about them. Fails if autonomy is internal only and observed behaviour cannot be read. Detects: unreadable agents.

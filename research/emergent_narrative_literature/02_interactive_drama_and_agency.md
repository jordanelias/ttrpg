## Interactive drama, agency, and the narrative paradox

### T11_aylett2000 — Aylett (2000). *Emergent Narrative, Social Immersion and "Storification"*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference/discussion paper (the text itself names no venue; it carries a stray header AAAI Symp 99 Narrative) · venue per brief: NILE00, not confirmable from the text · skipped: reference list · domain: NARR
- **ID check:** The text is not the 1999 symposium paper. It cites Cavazza et al 2000 (to appear) and Umaschi Bers & Cassell 2000, and it mentions the film *Gladiator* (2000). So it was written in 2000 on a reused 1999 symposium template. The venue (NILE00) cannot be confirmed from the text alone; no MISMATCH on author, title or period.
- **Thesis:** Pre-scripted plot clashes with the freedom a virtual environment gives its user (the narrative paradox). Aylett proposes building narrative bottom-up from interacting characters (emergent narrative) and treating the user as one participant whose experience is subjectively storified. Keeping the user's behaviour usable for narrative needs social presence: persistent, remembering agents and no restart.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Narrative can be viewed as levels (plot, abstract action sequences, physical behaviour). Improvisation is well explored at the bottom level and almost absent at plot level, and the top level is hard for any agent because it binds several characters (Narrative approaches).
  2. Reading the hierarchy bottom-up gives a character-based emergent narrative that parallels free actor improvisation, where actors get roles and a conflict but no plot (Narrative approaches; Emergent Narrative).
  3. Football shows narrative emerging from specified characters, relationships, conflicting goals and constraints, but also shows the failure rate: many matches are boring and the narrative can fail to appear (Emergent Narrative).
  4. Emergent narrative is small-scale and place-and-time contingent, and the narrative depends on viewpoint. A storification process turns emergent events into a story for each individual, so the user is not optional (Emergent Narrative).
  5. Conventional games (adventure, arcade) give the user only a sequencing puzzle or physical action, and their undo/restart facility destroys narrative coherence. A coherent emergent narrative therefore needs social presence: a defined user role that other agents reinforce, penalise and remember. That implies a persistent world, no restart, and autobiographical agents (The role of the user).
  6. The proposed Holodeck separates on-stage from off-stage areas, as a soap does. It needs in-character entry and exit for the user and a way of reporting off-stage events back, such as gossip or a recap voice-over (The Holodeck).
- **Argument structure:** The argument runs from a Teletubbies behavioural-architecture project, through analogies (football, improvisation, soap opera), to design requirements. No experiment is reported and there is no user study. The Teletubbies examples (the cloud and the Noo-noo, the wellington boots) are described, not measured. The paper calls itself a discussion paper, and its only evidence is analogy and the author's own viewing impressions. The weakest link is the claim that social pressure and persistence will steer a user toward narratively useful behaviour. That is asserted, and the text itself says the user may not know what the right behaviour is. The paper also concedes that emergent narrative may simply not emerge.
- **Key terms:**
  - `emergent narrative` — story arising bottom-up from interaction between simple characters and events, with no authored plot.
  - `storification` — the subjective, individual process of turning emergent events into a story; the paper credits the idea to Dautenhahn 99.
  - `social presence` — the illusion of social location: the user accepts the agents as social beings who reward and penalise role behaviour.
  - `narrative paradox` — reconciling the participant's freedom with the coherence a story needs.
  - `on-stage / off-stage` — only the on-stage area is viewed or entered by the user; the rest runs concurrently.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "the creation of complexity bottom up via interaction between essentially simple components" (Narrative approaches)
  - "the user cannot be considered an optional addition to an emergent narrative" (Emergent Narrative)
  - "Thus a restart facility cannot exist" (The role of the user)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T11_aylett2000.Q1** *If the design has save/restore, undo or reload, what in the story state survives it?* Passes if consequences and other agents' memories of the user's character persist, or the design says openly that coherence is traded away. Fails if a reload silently erases narratively significant events. Detects: undo-induced loss of narrative coherence.
  - **T11_aylett2000.Q2** *Can a log reader name, for each generated story, whose viewpoint it is told from, and does the system tell different stories to different observers?* Passes if events are surfaced per viewer or per participant. Fails if there is one global event list with no point of view. Detects: missing storification, meaning events with no one positioned to experience them.
  - **T11_aylett2000.Q3** *Which events occur off-stage, and how do they reach the user (gossip, news, recap) when the user returns?* Passes if skipped time is both simulated and reported. Fails if elapsed time is skipped without simulation, or simulated without any channel back to the user. Detects: off-stage activity that is invisible or uncomputed.
  - **T11_aylett2000.Q4** *Do non-user characters reward, penalise and remember user behaviour in role?* Passes if a logged user action changes later agent behaviour toward the user. Fails if agents react only in the moment. Detects: absent social presence.
- **Does not license:** Do not read it as evidence that emergent narrative works. The paper is a position paper, the football and soap analogies are illustrative, and the paper itself says narrative may fail to emerge. It does not validate social presence empirically. Its claim that a restart facility must not exist is a design stance for role-coherent worlds, not a general result for games.
- **Links:** builds on Dautenhahn 98/99 (storification, autobiographical agents), Barnes 96 (behavioural architecture), Hayes-Roth and IMPROV (user-as-director); cites the Holodeck project of Cavazza et al. It states the narrative paradox, which T12_louchart2003 then takes up (the Louchart paper's own text is not read in this entry).
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - "extends emergent narrative with 'storification' (the user's subjective construction of narrative)": CORRECTED: storification is credited to Dautenhahn 99 and is the subjective story-making of any individual, whether user or spectator; Aylett uses it to argue the user is not optional (Emergent Narrative).
  - "social presence": CONFIRMED (The role of the user): social presence is the extension of physical presence to social location, enforced by role reinforcement and memory.
  - "an off-stage / on-stage 'Holodeck' proposal": CONFIRMED (The Holodeck): the world is divided into on-stage and off-stage areas, the user sees only on-stage action, and off-stage activity runs concurrently. The Holodeck is only initial ideas; nothing is built.

### T12_louchart2003 — Louchart & Aylett (2003). *Solving the narrative paradox in VEs – lessons from RPGs*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper (short) · IVA 2003 per brief (the text itself names no venue) · skipped: reference list · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** The tabletop RPG, with a Game-Master (GM) authoring encounters to steer a provisional plot while players control characters, is a workable compromise between narrative control and user freedom. The authors judge only part of the GM role computable: the signs of narrative impasse can be categorised and character-level play looks feasible, while sensing and acting on players' satisfaction is described as much more difficult and the GM's interventions lean on out-of-character interaction.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. The RPG was studied because it is an interactive medium that fits neither Aristotelian nor structuralist narrative models, and the literature on it is thin, being mostly campaign setting books (§1, §2).
  2. Method: knowledge elicitation from one expert GM (Dr Ian McBriar, a 2001 world champion of a team-championship event), using the KAT technique and Builder software, which formalises Popper's falsificationism. No other GMs, no observation of live games, and no number of sessions is given (§2).
  3. The GM places encounters of five types (descriptive, social, information-gathering, problem-solving, combat) in time and space to trigger player reactions, so that an anticipated but hypothetical plot unfolds and its pace can be shaped (§3, Table 1).
  4. Control is split by level: the GM controls plot, pace and structure; players produce content from abstract action downward (§3).
  5. The GM intervenes by adding actions when players take too long, branch unexpectedly, misjudge their role or skip essential encounters; he also monitors players in and out of character and corrects if some are not enjoying the game (§3).
  6. Conclusion on computability: simulating character-level control seems feasible and so does some high-level control; interventions draw on human judgement and creativity. Signs of impasse are easy to categorise, but assessing and acting on user satisfaction is much harder. The GM's mechanisms also rely too much on out-of-character interaction (§4).
- **Argument structure:** The premise is that the narrative paradox is unsolved and that RPGs, already solving it for humans, might offer a mechanism. The authors elicit that mechanism from a single GM, map it onto their level hierarchy, and judge each part against what AI can currently do. The evidence is one expert's account run through a knowledge-engineering protocol, with no triangulation. The weakest link is the generalisation from one GM's self-report to RPGs. The results (Table 1, the intervention triggers) are also reported without any procedure for how they were validated. The feasible verdicts are the authors' judgement; nothing is implemented or tested.
- **Key terms:**
  - `narrative paradox` — conflict between pre-authored plot and user freedom of movement and interaction in a virtual environment.
  - `encounter` — a GM-placed scene or NPC situation that triggers player decisions and carries the plot; five types in Table 1.
  - `intervention` — a GM's dynamic addition when the story is threatened by delay or unexpected branching.
  - `hypothetical plot` — the a priori campaign line, which is only provisional and rewritten from session to session.
  - `real-time director` — the authors' label for the GM role, between a staged-plot director and an improv artist.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "knowledge acquisition through elicitation from an expert" (§2)
  - "Although the signs of narrative impasse can be relatively easily categorised" (§4)
  - "a real-time director" (§4)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T12_louchart2003.Q1** *Does the design give its story-steering agent a defined set of intervention types (encounters or equivalents) with stated triggers?* Passes if each trigger (stall, unexpected branch, role misread, skipped essential content) maps to a named response. Fails if steering is ad hoc or hidden in unrelated code. Detects: undefined narrative control.
  - **T12_louchart2003.Q2** *Is the plot treated as provisional, with a logged rule for how it is rewritten when players diverge?* Passes if divergence produces a recorded re-plan. Fails if divergence is just ignored or blocked. Detects: a brittle fixed plot.
  - **T12_louchart2003.Q3** *Does the design monitor player enjoyment or engagement, or state that it cannot?* Passes if it either measures a satisfaction proxy or explicitly leaves the gap open. Fails if it claims GM-equivalent adaptation with no such measure. Detects: unfounded claims of GM equivalence.
  - **T12_louchart2003.Q4** *Which parts of its steering are actually human out-of-character negotiation, and are those parts replaced or silently dropped?* Passes if each dependency on out-of-character talk is replaced by an in-world mechanism or acknowledged as lost. Fails if the GM's out-of-character role disappears without replacement. Detects: lost GM functions.
- **Does not license:** Do not cite it as evidence that a GM's techniques generalise: it is one interviewee, one session set of unstated size, and no procedure check. It is also not a computational result, because nothing was built. The feasibility judgments are the authors' own opinions, dated 2003. It does not show a GM-less system can match human interventions; its own conclusion says the opposite for the hard parts. It also does not describe encounters as a drama-management algorithm, only as a GM's practice.
- **Links:** builds on Aylett 1999 and 2000 (the cited NILE00 title in reference [1] matches T11_aylett2000, supporting that entry's ID check), on Louchart & Aylett 2002 and on Aylett & Louchart 2003 (theory of VR narrative). It compares itself to Façade's beats (cites Mateas & Stern 2003), calling beats a far lower level of abstraction than the GM-player interface (§4), which bears on T03a_facade_gdc03.
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - "an RPG Game Master's mechanisms (encounter-driven structure, interventions on impasse) are a model for computational storytelling": CORRECTED: the paper says GM mechanisms offer a good compromise and are partly simulable, but sensing and acting on satisfaction is described as much more difficult than categorising impasse signs; method is elicitation from ONE GM (Dr Ian McBriar), not interviews with several (§2, §4).

### T03a_facade_gdc03 — Mateas & Stern (2003). *Façade: An Experiment in Building a Fully-Realized Interactive Drama*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper (mid-project report) · GDC 2003 per brief (the text itself names no venue) · skipped: reference list, the ABL feature list and API message list (read, not analysed) · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** A middle ground between hand-authored story graphs and open simulation is feasible: a moment-to-moment simulation of two characters provides local agency, while a drama manager sequencing author-annotated beats toward a target tension arc provides unity, pacing and global agency. The authors state plainly that this is an architecture, not a story generator, and that the quality still depends on a great deal of hand-authored content.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Structured narrative gives a well-formed experience (unity, efficiency, pacing, perhaps an Aristotelian arc) and simulation gives agency and freedom, and each lacks the other's strength (Approach and motivation).
  2. Façade runs as a simulation moment-to-moment, and an invisible drama manager adds and retracts behaviours and discourse contexts in units called beats (the smallest unit of dramatic action), each annotated with preconditions and effects on story state (Approach and motivation).
  3. The beat sequencer filters beats by precondition, keeps the highest priority tier, scores each candidate by how closely its tension effects match the next segment of a target arc (score is 1 over e to the power of the slope mismatch), multiplies by weight and draws at random (Beat Sequencing Language).
  4. Agency is split into local agency (immediate, visible reaction inside a beat) and global agency (the whole interaction history shaping which beats follow); the drama manager supplies the second (Player interaction; Drama management).
  5. Failure is detectable by the manager itself: cumulative error between actual and desired tension arc, or an empty candidate set, signals that the beat collection is not rich enough (Beat Sequencing Language).
  6. The authors list their own failures and limits: language understanding errors, authorial labour, authorial complexity, doubtful sufficiency of global agency, the player not being protagonist, and unproven scaling beyond intimate settings (Failures of the system).
- **Argument structure:** The paper moves from a gap in games (no relationship themes, scripted NPCs), via a two-pole taxonomy (graph versus simulation), to a hybrid architecture, then to a self-assessment. The evidence is a system description plus the authors' own counts; there is no player study. The conclusion says explicitly that future papers will try to evaluate the claim. The weakest link is the equation of agency with the number of possible orderings; their own footnote concedes that this does not address how meaningfully different the orderings are. The 25% seen and thousands of orderings figures are estimates by the authors during authoring, not measured outcomes.
- **Key terms:**
  - `beat` — the smallest unit of dramatic action; here a bundle of behaviours with preconditions and effects on story state.
  - `drama manager` — an invisible agent that sequences beats using author-written annotations and a target tension arc.
  - `local agency / global agency` — immediate reaction within a beat / effect of interaction history on later beats.
  - `story tension arc` — the Aristotelian curve of desired tension values against which candidate beats are scored.
  - `discourse act` — a coarse category (about 40 types) into which every typed utterance or gesture is mapped.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "a beat is the smallest unit of dramatic action that moves a story forward" (Approach and motivation)
  - "match an Aristotelian dramatic arc" (Approach and motivation)
  - "only seeing about 25% of the total content in any one session" (Early Observations: Player agency)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T03a_facade_gdc03.Q1** *Is there an explicit pacing target (a curve over a named story variable), and does a log show the pacing component choosing its next unit by distance to that target?* Passes if the target, the variable and the selection rule can be read from the design or log. Fails if pacing is a claim with no target variable or no selection rule. Detects: pacing asserted but not mechanised.
  - **T03a_facade_gdc03.Q2** *Does the design have an alarm for the pacing mechanism running out of suitable content (empty candidate set, or error growing over time)?* Passes if such a state is detected and reported. Fails if it silently picks the least bad candidate forever. Detects: content starvation hidden by a selector that always picks something.
  - **T03a_facade_gdc03.Q3** *Can local reaction and global consequence be told apart in the design, and are both present?* Passes if some player actions change the immediate response and some change what happens later, and a reviewer can point to each. Fails if only one level exists. Detects: agency present only locally (hollow) or only globally (unreadable).
  - **T03a_facade_gdc03.Q4** *What fraction of authored content does a single run reach, and is that reported?* Passes if the design states the coverage per run. Fails if content volume is cited without per-run coverage. Detects: content volume mistaken for variety.
- **Does not license:** Do not take it as evidence that beat sequencing works; the paper itself calls it mid-project and offers no player data. It does not license the belief that agency is proportional to orderings, which the authors state as a stipulation and then qualify. The authors say the system assembles rather than generates and that the story genre was chosen because it resequences well; they say plot-heavy drama does not. Its two-pole taxonomy (graph versus simulation) is the authors' framing. The counts (about 200 beats, 18 beats per story) are the authors' estimates during authoring.
- **Links:** builds on Bates 1992 and Weyhrauch 1997 (drama management), McKee (beats), Hap (ABL) and Laurel 1986; it positions itself against Tale-Spin, plot graphs and Mimesis. Louchart & Aylett 2003 (T12_louchart2003) cites a sibling 2003 Mateas & Stern paper (TIDSE 2003, not this one) and says beats work at a far lower level than the GM-player interface. Its 2005 successor is T03b_facade_aiide05.
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - "a drama manager sequencing beats to an Aristotelian arc (a pacing-director precedent)": CONFIRMED (Beat Sequencing Language; Approach and motivation). The mechanism is a weighted random draw over precondition-satisfied, highest-priority beats scored against a tension arc; the arc is a scoring target, not a script. This paper's addition about agency is the local/global split and its proxy measures (see Early Observations); it states no player evidence.

### T03b_facade_aiide05 — Mateas & Stern (2005). *Structuring Content in the Façade Interactive Drama Architecture*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper · AIIDE-05 (copyright line AAAI 2005) · skipped: reference list · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** High-agency interactive drama can be built by recasting player interaction as abstract social games scored by hidden numeric state and expressed through performance, and by structuring content as small causally independent units that several parallel sequencers intermix. The authors then grade their own result: local agency was bought at the expense of global agency, and only 27 beats were authored.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Agency is easy in games because moves change simple numeric state; for story, interaction is recast as social games (affinity, hot-button, therapy) played with discourse acts and scored by hidden values that the characters act out, so the player never sees a number (Resolving Game Versus Story).
  2. Content is divided into multiple, mostly causally independent fronts of progression, run by parallel sequencers that can intermix, instead of one branching tree (Richness Through Coherent Intermixing).
  3. The content hierarchy is joint dialog behaviours (about 2500), organised in beat goals and mix-ins inside 27 beats, plus global mix-in progressions; the drama manager sequences beats by preconditions, weights and priority against an Aristotelian tension arc (Beats; Drama Management).
  4. Coherence between independent pieces is kept by interruption and re-establish mechanics and by matching tone across 3 to 5 alternates per line, so players read unrelated content as causally linked (Strategies for Coherent Intermixing).
  5. Local agency (immediate, context-specific reaction) and global agency (the shape of the whole, including the ending, determined smoothly by player action) are evaluated separately; the authors say the global kind fell short (Evaluating Agency; Failures and Successes).
  6. The designed genre mattered: a loose, sparse plot helped local agency and cost global agency, and the large domain raised player expectations the system could not meet (Failures and Successes).
- **Argument structure:** The paper reasons from the premise that agency is the hardest interactive pleasure, to a design choice (sparse-plot theatrical drama, social games, intermixed sequencers), to a self-evaluation. Evidence is the authors' production experience, plus estimates that they label as their own estimations. The self-evaluation percentages are given as estimates and the natural-language failure rate as coming from informal evaluations; no sample size, participants or protocol appear. The weakest link is that the headline high agency claim rests on content counts and the authors' judgement, and the paper's own Failures section concedes the global-agency target was missed.
- **Key terms:**
  - `local agency / global agency` — immediate, context-specific, meaningful reaction to an action / the global shape of the experience determined by player action, with the player able to see how.
  - `joint dialog behavior (jdb)` — atomic unit: a 1 to 5 line coordinated exchange between the two characters.
  - `beat goal / beat mix-in / global mix-in` — canonical sequence of goals in a beat, a beat-specific reaction, and a cross-beat topic progression with three tiers.
  - `social game` — a numeric score about relationships (affinity, hot-button topic tiers, self-realisation) driven by discourse acts and shown only through performance.
  - `coherent intermixing` — keeping causally independent pieces consistent through discourse mechanics and tonal matching.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "recast interactions within a story world in terms of abstract social games" (Resolving Game Versus Story)
  - "the number of meaningfully different beat sequences are few" (Global Agency)
  - "far lower global agency than we initially hoped for" (Failures and Successes)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T03b_facade_aiide05.Q1** *Does the design report nominal and meaningfully different outcome counts separately?* Passes if both are given, or the design says how many orderings change the story's meaning. Fails if it counts orderings or permutations as variety without checking that they differ in effect. Detects: combinatorial variety that is cosmetic.
  - **T03b_facade_aiide05.Q2** *For a sample of player actions, what share gets a specific immediate response, a generic one, or none?* Passes if such a distribution is measured or at least estimated from logs. Fails if responsiveness is asserted without any per-action classification. Detects: unmeasured local agency.
  - **T03b_facade_aiide05.Q3** *Can a player tell, from what is shown, what hidden state their actions changed?* Passes if state changes are legible through some channel (performance, text, record). Fails if hidden scores drive outcomes with no player-readable trace. Detects: opaque state, agency that cannot be perceived.
  - **T03b_facade_aiide05.Q4** *Do content volume and per-run coverage match the agency being claimed?* Passes if the design states total content, content reached per run, and the number of selectable top-level units. Fails if a large global-agency claim rests on a handful of top-level units. Detects: content-starved sequencing.
- **Does not license:** Do not cite its percentages (25/40/25/10 local-agency split, 30% understanding failure) as measurements; they are the authors' estimates from informal evaluation. It is one game, one domestic-drama genre, and one 20-minute experience; the authors tie their success to a sparse-plot genre and do not claim the approach carries to plot-driven or large-scale stories. It does not show that the tension arc improves experience, because no comparison condition exists. Its numbers disagree with the 2003 paper on several counts (see below), so neither should be quoted alone.
- **Links:** builds on and updates T03a_facade_gdc03 (cites it as Mateas & Stern 2003b); uses Murray's immersion/agency/transformation, McKee's beats and Berne's head games; cites Crawford on combinatorial explosion. Agency here is later refined in T08_agency2009 [reader's inference: the 2009 paper shares Mateas as an author].
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - "a drama manager sequencing beats to an Aristotelian arc (a pacing-director precedent)": CONFIRMED (Drama Management) — the beat whose story-tension effects best match the near-term target arc is chosen, with weights and priorities also applying. It operates only at the coarse level (27 beats, about 15 per run), with finer sequencing delegated to within-beat sequencers.
  - Quantitative and evaluative claims: about 2500 jdbs in about 20 minutes; 27 beats, about 15 met per run; at most 25% of content per run; about 3 person years of authoring; thousands of nominal sequences but few meaningful ones; self-estimated local-agency split; about 30% natural-language failure in informal evaluation; global agency admitted far lower than hoped; therapy-game state communicated poorly. Compared to the GDC 2003 paper (T03a_facade_gdc03): 2003 said about 200 beats, about 18 per story, about 40 discourse acts, two person years, thousands of orderings as the global-agency measure. 2005 uses beat for a larger unit (27) and treats the jdb as the closer match to the canonical beat, so the two beat counts are not comparable; it says ordering counts overstate agency.

### T08_agency2009 — Wardrip-Fruin, Mateas, Dow & Sali (2009). *Agency Reconsidered*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper · DiGRA 2009 · skipped: reference list · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** Agency is a phenomenon between player and game, occurring when the actions a player desires are among the actions the underlying computational model supports (and vice versa). It is neither free will nor a mere experience or structural property. So supporting it takes a software model of the play domain, an interface that sets accurate expectations, and tolerance for improvised play.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Murray's agency (the power to take meaningful action and see results) is distinct from mere participation and activity, and Church's intention plus perceivable consequence name a near-identical idea from game design; both leave open where the player's desires come from (Agency is introduced, twice; Agency and drama).
  2. Mateas's structural account: agency is experienced when formal affordances (the actions motivated by the plot's dramatic probability) balance material affordances (the actions the presented world makes available); an imbalance, as in adventure games with many things to do and no reason to choose, lowers it (Agency and drama).
  3. Leaning on the Eliza effect (an illusion of intelligence) gives only a short-lived illusion of agency, because breakdown damages dramatic probability and makes players doubt the materials for action; the SimCity effect works differently because the surface has analogues in the internal model, so players can build an accurate model by experiment (Agency and software models).
  4. More presence and realism can reduce agency: in Dow's study of three versions of Façade (desktop, voice, augmented reality), AR raised expectations and made players feel less free to play, and the voice version looked unconstrained while hiding real constraints (Agency and interfaces).
  5. Once agency has been established early (the affinity game), players kept trying to act in the later therapy game even when their actions had no effect on the model, so agency becomes part of expectation (Agency and interfaces).
  6. Plans are only one resource for improvised, situated play; designs like Far Cry 2's, with failure that keeps the fiction moving (shortened composition and execution cycles), support agency better than reset and reload (Agency and improvisation).
- **Argument structure:** The paper begins with the intellectual history of the term (Murray, Church, Mateas), then proposes the phenomenon-based definition and tests it against four design issues. Its evidence is mixed: conceptual argument, two software examples (Eliza, SimCity) whose analysis is taken from Wardrip-Fruin's own book, a summary of Dow's user study (three conditions, interview quotations; sample size and method not reported in this paper), and a designer's conference talk. The authors state that their aim is not to argue for the definition on theoretical grounds but to show that it focuses attention on design issues. The weakest link is the study evidence: summarised second-hand with quotations and no counts, and the AR conclusion rests on one study of one game.
- **Key terms:**
  - `agency` — the phenomenon occurring when the actions players desire are among those they can take, as supported by the underlying computational model.
  - `dramatic probability` — what the fiction makes expected next; it motivates (formal) actions.
  - `material / formal affordances` — actions made available by the presented world / actions motivated by the plot's shape; agency needs a balance.
  - `Eliza effect` — the illusion that a system is more intelligent than it is, which breaks as initial expectations are violated.
  - `SimCity effect` — initial expectations differ from the model, but the surface elements map onto internal processes, so players learn the model by experiment.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "Players will experience agency when there is a balance between the material and formal constraints." (Agency and drama)
  - "agency requires the construction of a playable software model" (Agency and software models)
  - "an increased sense of presence and realism can actually act to decrease agency" (Agency and interfaces)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T08_agency2009.Q1** *For a given player aim the design suggests, is there a supported action (and for each supported action, a reason in the fiction to take it)?* Passes if a reviewer can pair actions and motives in both directions. Fails if there are many available actions with no reason to prefer one, or motives the game never lets the player act on. Detects: formal/material affordance imbalance.
  - **T08_agency2009.Q2** *When a player tries something outside the model, does the response keep the fiction going, and does the surface correspond to something in the model?* Passes if the failed attempt yields a coherent continuation, and the displayed elements have counterparts in the internal state. Fails if the system produces replies unrelated to its state (Eliza-style) or terminates or resets. Detects: surface divorced from model; breakdown of dramatic probability.
  - **T08_agency2009.Q3** *Can a player learn the model by experiment, i.e. does the same action produce consistent, discoverable consequences?* Passes if a play log shows players refining their expectations. Fails if expectations raised by the interface (natural-language, realism) exceed the supported actions. Detects: expectation gap.

### T10_choicepoetics — Mawhorter, Mateas, Wardrip-Fruin & Jhala (2014). *Towards a Theory of Choice Poetics*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper (theory outline) · FDG 2014 per brief (the text names no venue) · skipped: reference list · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** The narrative effect of a player's choice can be analysed by splitting each choice into framing, options and outcomes, and then asking three further questions: how the player is engaging (mode of engagement), which known pattern the choice follows (choice idiom), and which experience dimension it moves (dimensions of player experience). The authors call this an outline for future theory and empirical work, not a tested theory.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Traditional narrative theory is silent on how players are affected by choice structures, so a poetics of choice is needed that unifies narrative theory, link poetics, procedural rhetoric and craft advice (Abstract; Related theories).
  2. A choice structure consists of framing (content before the choice that shapes interpretation), options (the discrete interface elements) and outcomes (content shown after); framing plus options set the player's expectations (Choice structures).
  3. Modes of engagement (avatar play, role play, power play, exploratory, analytical and critical play) depend on motive and approach, and the same choice affects players differently by mode; for example, an illusion of choice may survive linear play and break under exploratory or analytical play (Modes of engagement).
  4. Choice idioms are reusable patterns with typical effects: dead-end option, false choice, blind choice, dilemma, flavor choice, delayed effect, puzzle choice, unchoice (Choice idioms).
  5. Dimensions of player experience influenced by choice structure: agency, influence, autonomy, identification, transportation, absorption, responsibility, regret; for example, influence (ability to affect outcomes) is necessary for agency, but agency also needs predictability from framing and options (Dimensions of player experience).
  6. The theory can advise design: for role-play, make choices internal, persona-based, with outcomes depending on the constructed persona (Conclusions).
- **Argument structure:** The paper builds from a definition, to a survey of related theory and craft writing (a game review, a publisher's blog), to three partial catalogues, with examples taken from published commercial games. The evidence is conceptual synthesis plus illustrative examples; it reports no experiment, and the player-type evidence is borrowed from others (Knickmeyer & Mateas on Façade, Thue et al.). It declares that it collects existing insights rather than presenting new ones. The weakest link is that the lists are explicitly partial and unvalidated: nothing shows that the modes, idioms or dimensions are exhaustive, distinct from one another, or measurable. The authors state that the theories need refinement before use in studies or design.
- **Key terms:**
  - `choice structure` — framing, options and outcomes of a single explicit, discrete choice.
  - `mode of engagement` — the player's perspective, motive and practice in choosing (avatar, role, power, exploratory, analytical, critical).
  - `choice idiom` — a generic structure or pattern of a choice that generally achieves a specific effect, such as false choice or dilemma.
  - `influence` — raw ability to affect story outcomes, whether or not effects can be predicted; separate from agency.
  - `internal choice` — a choice of protagonist action as in an adventure game, in contrast to choices of perspective or authorial choices.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "A choice structure consists of the framing, options, and outcomes associated with a choice." (Choice structures)
  - "this list is only partial" (Choice idioms)
  - "We intend to build on this work ourselves to implement computational models of choice poetics" (Conclusions)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T10_choicepoetics.Q1** *For a named decision point, can the design state its framing, its options and its outcomes, and what the player will expect from each option?* Passes if each of the three parts is identifiable and the expectation raised by framing plus options can be written down. Fails if outcomes cannot be traced to options, or the framing gives no basis for expectation. Detects: blind choices and untraceable consequences.
  - **T10_choicepoetics.Q2** *Which modes of engagement is the design built for, and does a choice survive a second, exploratory or analytical, playthrough?* Passes if the design names its target mode(s) and a replay does not expose false choices or dead ends. Fails if it relies on an illusion that only works on a first linear pass. Detects: false choice fragile under replay.
  - **T10_choicepoetics.Q3** *Sort the design's choices by idiom. How many are false, flavor, or delayed, and is that proportion intended?* Passes if each choice has a recognised idiom and its weight in the design is known. Fails if the design cannot say how many of its choices change anything beyond text. Detects: choice inflation (many options, few consequences).
  - **T10_choicepoetics.Q4** *Does the design separate influence from agency, that is, can players affect outcomes and also predict how?* Passes if both are addressed. Fails if outcomes are influenced but unpredictable (delayed effects with no signal), or predictable but not influential. Detects: influence without agency, and the reverse.
- **Does not license:** It does not license quantitative conclusions about what works: no experiment is reported. Its scope is explicit, discrete, internal choices in narrative games with fixed branching layouts; implicit and continuous choices (including most simulation play) are explicitly left out. The idiom and dimension lists are partial. The avoid false choices style advice it contextualises comes from craft blogs and reviews, not from controlled study. It is also a theory of presented choices, so it is not a model of how a simulation generates events without presenting a choice.
- **Links:** builds on and cites Wardrip-Fruin et al. 2009 (T08_agency2009) for agency as informed control; also Thue et al. (player modelling), Knickmeyer & Mateas (Façade), procedural rhetoric (Bogost), link poetics (Bernstein, Tosca), Mason (levels of agency).

### E16_riedl2013 — Riedl & Bulitko (2013). *Interactive Narrative: An Intelligent Systems Approach*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · survey article (author preprint) · AI Magazine 34(1) per brief (the text says only to appear in AI Magazine) · skipped: reference list and author bios; Figure 3 (the landscape map) is garbled in the extraction as a block of reference numbers, so the placement of individual systems in the map could not be read · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** The central problem of interactive narrative is balancing coherent story progression against user agency, and the standard answer is an experience manager that projects future trajectories and intervenes through non-player characters. The authors claim the research landscape can be organised along three dimensions (authorial intent, virtual character autonomy, player modelling), and that AI widens a system's expressive range without reducing agency; this last is asserted, not tested.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. The core challenge is making the player feel agency over the direction or outcome while keeping events building on prior events; user actions can introduce inconsistencies the system cannot build on, whether by ignorance or by testing the system (Experience Management).
  2. An experience manager (a generalisation of Bates's drama manager) looks ahead through possible futures and picks interventions that steer toward the best narrative by author-given criteria (structure, tension, pedagogy), not the most likely one; the user should not notice it (Experience Management).
  3. Authorial intent runs from branching story graphs (every trajectory authored; content grows exponentially with choice points), through plot graphs with precedence constraints searched by an experience manager, to generative managers with an embedded story generator (Authorial Intent).
  4. A generative manager must solve the boundary problem (the user deviating from the intended narrative) by intervention (prevent, e.g. NPC direction, a jammed gun) or accommodation (let it happen and regenerate to repair causal inconsistency) (Authorial Intent).
  5. Virtual character autonomy runs from strong story (NPCs act only with the manager's permission) to strong autonomy (NPCs unaware of the plot; this extreme is emergent narrative); the tension is global structure versus local believability, and Façade and the Automated Story Director are described as middle positions (Virtual Character Autonomy).
  6. Player modelling (learning the user's preferences and play style) is early-stage and mostly used under strong authorial intent; the paper also links it to education (Player Modeling).
- **Argument structure:** The paper builds a framing problem (Hamlet on the Holodeck: what happens when the user slays Claudius in act one), defines the experience-manager solution, then sorts about 20 years of systems into a three-axis taxonomy and discusses each axis. The evidence is a literature survey with systems placed on a figure and a few pictured examples; there is no comparative data or evaluation. The weakest link is that the taxonomy is the authors' own organising device; the claim that AI increases expressive power without diminishing player agency is not backed by any measurement in the text. The survey's own corpus is research-lab systems, with Façade the only publicly released exception.
- **Key terms:**
  - `experience manager` — a disembodied agent that monitors the world and intervenes (usually through NPCs) to drive the experience according to a quality model; generalises drama manager beyond drama.
  - `boundary problem` — recognising and responding to user actions that deviate from the desired narrative.
  - `intervention vs accommodation` — preventing a deviation versus allowing it and regenerating the story.
  - `strong story / strong autonomy` — NPCs fully directed by the manager / NPCs unaware of the plot; the emergent-narrative end of the axis.
  - `plot graph` — nodes are events and arcs are precedence constraints; some nodes are manager actions.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "how to balance the need for a coherent story progression with user agency" (Experience Management)
  - "A drama manager is an intelligent, omniscient, and disembodied agent" (Experience Management)

### T13_wardripfruin2008 — Wardrip-Fruin (2008). *Reading Digital Literature: Surface, Data, Interaction, and Expressive Processing*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · book chapter (HTML) · *A Companion to Digital Literary Studies*, ch. 8 per brief (the text itself carries only the chapter number) · skipped: reference list; figure images (8.1 to 8.6 appear only as captions) · domain: MIX
- **Thesis:** Critics who read digital works only from the audience's surface miss what the work does; a work should be read as the interplay of surface, data, process and interaction. Using Tale-Spin as the case, the chapter argues that its most interesting operations (planning, inference, lying) are almost never visible in its output, and that a simulation of human behaviour is always an authored fiction.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Most critical work uses an implicit audience model (surface plus possible interactions), while Aarseth's main alternative is a traversal-function model (scriptons, textons, traversal functions); each foregrounds some aspects and hides others (Models for Reading Digital Literature).
  2. Tale-Spin's most famous output was a hand-translated early malfunction, and its errors (Meehan's mis-spun tales) make readers ask how such a story could come to be, which turns attention to the system's processes (Reading Tale-Spin's Outputs).
  3. Aarseth's model cannot be applied cleanly: there are no clear textons (the world is conceptual-dependency expressions and Mumble assembles sentences on the fly), and most of the action never becomes a scripton (Locating Tale-Spin's Traversal Function; Tale-Spin's Traversal Function).
  4. Tracing the simulation shows rich psychological action (relationship weighing, speculative worlds, lies) while the output contains travel itineraries and one-line mentions of the psychology; one lie is a breakdown because the answer-choosing component does not know it was chosen out of kindness (Tale-Spin's Simulation; Observations on the Simulation).
  5. The proposed model: surface (site of presentation and possible interaction), data, processes that employ data, and interaction, defined as a change to the state of the work, for which it was designed, that comes from outside the work. Authorship of any element is left open. Groupings of these are operational logics, and Tale-Spin's central one is planning (A New Model; Employing the Model).
  6. Eliza effect versus Tale-Spin effect: the first is a surface impression of far more complexity than the process has, the second is complex process hidden from the surface. A hidden process offers less to an audience than the visible errors of a broken one (Models for Reading Digital Literature; Resurfacing).
- **Argument structure:** The chapter works by a staged close reading of one system: first through the audience model (the famous output), then through Aarseth's model (which fails), then through a trace of the simulation, and finally through the new model, reading Murray's and Aarseth's treatments of Tale-Spin as consequences of the surface-only view. The evidence is one case (Tale-Spin and its companion Mumble, with Meehan's dissertation as primary source), contrasted briefly with two of the author's own works. The Eliza effect is taken as known and the Tale-Spin effect is defined by argument, with no empirical test of audience perception. The weakest link is generalising from a single 1976 system whose published output was partly hand-edited; the new model is offered as preliminary and open to refinement, which the author states himself.
- **Key terms:**
  - `Eliza effect` — systems that give the audience the impression of a much more complex process than is actually present.
  - `Tale-Spin effect` — the obscuring of a complex process so that it cannot be perceived by the audience; works with complex internal processes hidden at the surface.
  - `surface / data / process / interaction` — the model's four elements: where the work is presented, what the processes employ, the operations, and outside-in changes to the work's state.
  - `operational logic` — a grouping of data, process, surface and interaction that can be compared across works (here, planning-based simulation).
  - `expressive processing` — the idea that the processes of a work, not just its output, carry meaning and can be read.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "the obscuring of a complex process so that it cannot be perceived by the audience" (Models for Reading Digital Literature)
  - "always an encoding of the beliefs and biases of its authors" (closing argument, after Tale-Spin's Traversal Function)
  - "an interesting, successful, hidden process will offer less to an audience" (Resurfacing)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **T13_wardripfruin2008.Q1** *List what the simulation computes per turn (decisions, plans, deceptions) and what reaches the player; what fraction of significant internal events surface in any form?* Passes if consequential internal events are reflected on the surface or discoverable. Fails if the most interesting processing leaves no trace, as in a decision that looks like a random number. Detects: Tale-Spin effect (hidden process).
  - **T13_wardripfruin2008.Q2** *Does the surface imply more intelligence than the process has? Take the three most impressive-looking outputs and trace each to its source.* Passes if the traced process is as complex as the output suggests.   - **T13_wardripfruin2008.Q3** *When a component fails, does the visible error reveal a systems cause (a character ignoring a drowning friend), and can each such failure be traced to a missing inference?* Passes if failures are diagnosable and the missing rule can be named.   - **T13_wardripfruin2008.Q4** *Does every character trait the audience is shown correspond to an input to the decision process, and does anything outside that process affect behaviour?* Passes if shown traits and decision inputs coincide. Fails if characters have stated feelings or traits that no process consumes. Detects: simulated persons whose depicted inner life is inert.

The Tale-Spin effect is a term coined by one author on one case; no study measures it. The chapter's claim that Tale-Spin's planning model fails as a simulation rests on the Suchman and Agre critique, cited rather than tested here. It gives no method for deciding how much process should be shown.

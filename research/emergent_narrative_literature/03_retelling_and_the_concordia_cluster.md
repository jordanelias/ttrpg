### E01_eladhari2018 — Eladhari, M. P. (2018). *Re-Tellings: The Fourth Layer of Narrative as an Instrument for Critique*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper · ICIDS 2018 (LNCS 11318, pp. 65–78) · skipped: reference list · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** Players' re-tellings of what happened to them in an interactive narrative system (INS) form a fourth layer of narrative, sitting above the architectural, narrative-design and discourse layers. Because people only retell experiences that were memorable, the occurrence of re-tellings is proposed as a blunt indicator of design success and, once collected and analysed, as an instrument for critiquing an INS.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Interactive narrative is conventionally divided into three layers: (1) a base code/architectural layer giving the conditions for a world, (2) a narrative-design (story) layer of actants, driving forces and back-story, and (3) the narrative discourse, i.e. the unique, session-specific played sequence of events (§1.2, abstract).
  2. Re-telling (talk, blogs, fan fiction, streams, avatar diaries, official chronicles, even automated event-log diaries) is a distinct fourth layer, added because the three-layer division is a general consensus (§1.2, §7).
  3. If re-tellings emerge in the wake of a system, that indicates the experience was memorable and important enough to pass on, hence a successful or even artistically original design (§1, §7.2).
  4. More procedural and more varied experiences make re-telling more valuable, since the more unique traversals a system affords, the more there is to compare (§1).
  5. Re-tellings can be mined or collected (data-mining; a web-form collection in Klastrup's Death Stories Project) and analysed for which aspects are well received; a typology of fourth-layer narratives is left as future work (§7.2, §8).
  6. Authoring narrative potential is story construction, not story telling; designers are narrative architects, and layers separate who may author what (§2, §3).
- **Argument structure:** Premises: layered models of INS are near-consensus; people retell what matters to them. Moves: add a fourth layer for retelling; supply her Story Construction (SC) model (code / story / discourse / narrative text layers, with actant-theory and practical-syllogism machinery for the story layer) as the underpinning; then argue from one worked example. Evidence is a position paper plus one case (the *Alice and Kev* Sims 3 blog, 178 reader comments at one chapter, as of 2018-08-01) and a cited prior project; no systematic collection or measurement is done. Weakest link: retelling frequency is inferred to track design quality with no control for fandom size, streaming culture, platform reach or the fact that tellability may come from the player's own authorial effort rather than from the system.
- **Key terms:**
  - `INS` — interactive narrative system: games and other systems where each traversal yields a different story.
  - `fourth layer` — players' re-tellings of their play experiences, outside the system's authored text.
  - `narrative potential` — the accumulation of meaningful experience arising from agency, which lets participants build their own stories.
  - `discourse layer` — the sequence of experiences an agent actually goes through; where agent state evolves.
  - `expressive agent` — any human or computational entity that performs actions adding to the fiction, including NPCs and abstract forces such as weather.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "The act of creating narrative potential is an act of story construction, not story telling." (§2)
  - "an - admittedly somewhat blunt - indicator of a successful design." (§7.2)
  - "Future work includes a typology of fourth layer narratives." (§7.2, before Fig. 2)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **E01.Q1** *Does the design leave evidence that players retell their sessions, and do those retellings describe events the system itself produced?* Take a sample of external retellings (forums, streams, posts, diaries) or the playtest debrief transcripts. Passes if a stated share of the retold events can be traced to system-generated occurrences rather than to scripted set pieces. Fails if no retelling channel exists or retellings only restate authored content. Detects: tellability claimed without any observed retelling.
  - **E01.Q2** *Can each retold event be located in the code, story or discourse layer?* For ten retold events, name the layer that produced the memorable part (a rule interaction, an authored goal/driving force, or the player's own sequence of choices). Passes if the mapping is stable and not all land in one layer. Fails if the memorable part is always the player's own interpretation. Detects: credit misallocation between system and player.
  - **E01.Q3** *Do two sessions from the same starting state give players something to compare?* Run or review at least two plays and list events present in one and absent in the other. Passes if the tellable events differ. Fails if retellings would be interchangeable. Detects: low variance that kills comparison value.
  - **E01.Q4** *Does the system itself produce a retelling (diary, chronicle, log-to-text) and is it a faithful product of recorded events?* Passes if every sentence in the generated retelling maps to a logged event. Fails if it embellishes beyond the log. Detects: system-side retelling that fabricates.
- **Does not license:** Treating retelling volume as a measured success metric (the author calls it blunt and offers no measurement). Concluding that The Sims 3 is highly successful from one blog. Reading it as an empirical result: it is a position paper with one case. Reading fourth layer as an established term of art: it is her proposal.
- **Links:** Builds on Koenitz's SPP model (protostory/process/product), Klastrup's Death Stories Project, Mateas & Stern (local/global agency), Koster (impositional vs expressive), Jenkins (narrative architecture), Genette, Chatman, Greimas (all cited in the text). Relation to E02 and E11: later work applies or tests the idea `[reader's inference]`.
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - retelling is a 'fourth narrative layer' on top of three others: CONFIRMED (abstract, §1.2, §8). The three are (1) architectural/code, (2) narrative design/story, (3) narrative discourse (played sequence). Note the body's SC model (Fig. 1) names its four text layers code / story / discourse / narrative, which is a slightly different decomposition from the abstract's.
  - the occurrence of re-tellings is an indicator of a well-designed interactive narrative system: CONFIRMED (abstract, §7.2) with the author's own hedge admittedly somewhat blunt. Use as critique: collect re-tellings (data-mining), analyse them for what is well received (Klastrup precedent), build a typology (future work).

### E02_sych2020 — Sych, S. (2020). *When the Fourth Layer Meets the Fourth Wall: The Case for Critical Game Retellings*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper (short, about 9 pp.) · ICIDS 2020 (the text file carries no proceedings header, so venue and year come from the label plus the reference dates last accessed 25-Jun-2020) · skipped: reference list · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** A significant subset of player retellings are themselves critical of the game they retell. They ironically display and mock the system's bizarre or incoherent outputs, so retelling cannot simply be read as a marker of a well-made system. Scholars using retellings as an instrument of critique should attend to the critique players have already done inside them (abstract, §1).
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Eladhari (and, with a similar stance, Ryan) ties retellings to system quality, and Eladhari calls both for a blunt count (more retellings = better system) and for closer analysis (§1).
  2. Two Reddit anecdotes from RimWorld (a prisoner-recruitment story and a polar-colony visitors story) are retellings that reference specific mechanics, wink at a knowing audience, and get their humour from the system's emotional incoherence (§2).
  3. This makes them immanent critique: simultaneously a compelling retelling and a public analysis of the narrative system; in the extreme cases they do not track system quality, and are compelling despite, or because of, weak story material (§2).
  4. The rival reading is Ryan's computational art brut (crude, uncanny output is part of the form's appeal); Sych rejects it for these cases because art brut is defined by naivety and these retellers are highly self-reflexive, while allowing that raw brut outputs may be taken up critically by retellers (§3).
  5. A retelling is neither purely the reteller's nor purely the system's product; conflict between them is a friction-filled partnership, and critical retellings are one manifestation of that friction (§4).
  6. Critical retellings are defined by four features: shorter and more anecdotal than narrative; they reference the system's mechanics; they address a knowing audience; they retell with an ironic or satirical edge (§4).
- **Argument structure:** Premise: retellings give a window onto audience experience in the wild. Moves: define a subtype by example, anticipate the strongest alternative reading (art brut, success-by-oddness), then reject it by the reteller's reflexivity plus a designer-patch test (one could imagine a patch note fixing these outcomes, §3). Evidence is exactly two Reddit anecdotes, two Dwarf Fortress retellings in a footnote and one developer-interview quotation; the author flags the limits himself (anecdotes only; readers assumed to know RimWorld). Weakest link: no sample, count or coding, so significant subset is asserted not measured; and which outputs are less than desirable is the author's judgement, though §3 concedes that good cannot be presupposed.
- **Key terms:**
  - `critical retelling` — a short, anecdotal, mechanic-referencing, knowing-audience retelling with a critical, ironic edge toward its own game's system.
  - `immanent critique` — critique carried inside the retelling itself rather than applied by an outside analyst.
  - `retelling` — a player's narrative or anecdote about their play, from a passing remark to news-style reports.
  - `computational art brut` — Ryan's term for the crude, uncanny, eccentric quality of emergent-narrative output; here the rival reading.
  - `narrative system` — borrowed from Eladhari's INS; the author's evidence is games only.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "These critical retellings focus on bizarre and uncanny outcomes of a narrative system" (§1)
  - "the very seams of the simulation itself are laid bare for all to see" (§3)
  - "It should be clear that this question poses a false dichotomy." (§4)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **E02.Q1** *When retellings of the design are examined, are they classified by stance (celebratory, neutral, ironic/critical) rather than only counted?* Take any retelling set and code stance for each. Passes if the evaluation separates retellings that praise outcomes from those that mock them and reports the split. Fails if volume alone is used as a quality signal. Detects: conflating retelling volume with success.
  - **E02.Q2** *For each retold outcome that looks incoherent, is there a recorded disposition: intended eccentricity or defect?* Apply the patch-note test: could a one-line fix (X will no longer do Y) be written without losing what players liked? Passes if each such outcome is dispositioned with a reason. Fails if oddities stay charming by default. Detects: incoherence excused as emergent charm.
  - **E02.Q3** *Do independent event-generating subsystems read each other's recorded history (relationships, prior deaths, grievances) before acting?* Find or construct a play log with a prior grievance and check whether later events contradict it, as in the RimWorld cases. Passes if event selection consults the history; fails if the log shows contradictory behaviour. Detects: visible seams between subsystems, the failure Sych's retellers exploit.
  - **E02.Q4** *Are mechanism-referencing in-joke retellings and narrative-length retellings reported separately?* Passes if both types are sampled and reported. Fails if only one type is sampled. Detects: sampling bias from anecdote-shaped retellings.
- **Does not license:** Concluding that exposed seams or breakdowns are a design asset: Sych says only that retellers can turn weak output into pleasurable retellings, and he frames the underlying outputs as less than desirable. Concluding that the prevalence of irony has been measured (two anecdotes plus footnoted examples). Concluding that critical retellings refute Eladhari's indicator for full-length, character-focused retellings (he leaves those open as a limit). Importing any Heideggerian reading (none exists in the text).
- **Links:** Disputes and extends E01 (explicit, §1–§2); cites Kreminski et al. 2019 (E11) for the in the wild argument and Generative games as storytelling partners (ref. 15) for storytelling partnership; engages Ryan's curatorial emergent narrative and art brut (*Curating Simulated Storyworlds*, ref. 8). Concordia in the affiliation line is Concordia University, Montréal, not a narrative system; a corpus strand labelled Concordia should not be read as implying a system of that name `[reader's inference]`.
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - retelling/tellability is a success criterion: CORRECTED. Sych reports Eladhari's and Ryan's claim that retellings indicate quality, but his thesis is a wedge between the two: critical retellings do not track narrative system quality (§2). The word tellability does not occur; the nearest is tellable retelling (§2).
  - critical retellings surface irony or breakdown as immanent critique: CONFIRMED for irony and immanent critique (§2, abstract). Breakdown appears only in the abstract in the ordinary sense (failures and breakdowns), not as a theory term.
  - Heidegger's breakdown (ready-to-hand → present-at-hand) works as a 'criticality dial': NOT-IN-SOURCE (searched for Heidegger, ready-to-hand, present-at-hand, dial: no hits). Irony via Hutcheon (fn. 3) is the only theoretical lens on criticality.
  - deliberately exposed seams/uncanny specificity are a tellability asset: CORRECTED. Seams are exposed by the player's retelling, not deliberately by design, and the text places the flaws in the system (seams of the simulation ... laid bare, §3). Retellers amplify flaws to make a tellable anecdote (§2); the designer-side asset reading is Ryan's art brut, which Sych accepts only for raw output and rejects for these retellings (§3).
  - Later-thesis additions: storytelling partnership is IN this paper but credited to Kreminski et al. (§4); friction is IN the paper (§4 heading A Friction-filled Partnership; friction is part of what lets systems support creativity); the exact phrase generative friction and any NEW GAME+ / menu content are NOT-IN-SOURCE.

### E11_retellings2019 — Kreminski, M., Samuel, B., Melcer, E. & Wardrip-Fruin, N. (2019). *Evaluating AI-Based Games through Retellings*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper · AIIDE-19 (Fifteenth AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Interactive Digital Entertainment), pp. 45–51 · skipped: reference list · domain: MIX (NARR evaluation of AI/PCG games)
- **Thesis:** Player retellings can serve as a human-centred evaluation method for AI-based and PCG-based games, surfacing what typical story shapes players construct, how varied those stories are, and how well a game supports narrativization, which playtraces and direct questioning miss. Players' best retellings go beyond the logged events through extrapolative narrativization, and games that give them underdetermined, evocative hooks and modelled characters support that more.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Retellings are a subjectively-grounded playtrace: they capture what players made of events, including cosmetic details, which an exhaustive log cannot (Intro).
  2. A grounded-theory (constructivist) analysis of wild retellings yields a 10-code codebook plus a four-way story-type classification (chronicle, demarcated period, character biography, day-in-the-life); Stellaris retellings are more varied and have larger casts than Civilization VI ones (Collecting and Analyzing Wild Retellings, Table 1).
  3. Anecdotally, retellings with no reversals read as a steady rise, a few (up to three) feel compelling, and more begin to feel incoherent; reversal density also separated the two Prom Week versions (same section; Evaluating Prom Week).
  4. An ablation of Prom Week's social reasoning (random action choice instead) made storytelling harder and less coherent in the ablated version in this sample, while surprise did not differ between versions (Evaluating Prom Week).
  5. Resonant retellings are driven by extrapolative narrativization, and the authors suggest designers offer interpretable hooks, leave details underdetermined, model individual characters and their intentionality (Discussion).
  6. The existence of a sizable corpus of naturally occurring retellings may indicate a game is in some sense a success, echoing Eladhari (Conclusions).
- **Argument structure:** Premise: standard evaluation does not reach the variety-and-narrativization dimension that AI/PCG claims to enable. Moves: (a) mine wild retellings to build a codebook; (b) recruit players to author retellings so they can be interviewed; (c) run an ablation on a research game with no wild retellings. Corpus and sample sizes: 50 retellings gathered per subreddit (/r/Civ, /r/Stellaris) by searching stories and AAR; 20 chosen at random from each and coded until code saturation; then two undergraduate design students produced four retellings (Civ VI, Stellaris) and four more in two Prom Week versions, with interviews. Weakest link: the human-subject part is n=2 novice players with an acknowledged learning-curve confound, codes were chosen from the authors' intuitive understanding of narrative interest, and the reversal and compellingness claim is explicitly anecdotal.
- **Key terms:**
  - `retelling` — a story a player tells about their play experience.
  - `wild retellings` — naturally occurring retellings posted in community forums (e.g. after-action reports tagged AAR).
  - `extrapolative narrativization` — a player inventing story elements not modelled in the game from in-game details.
  - `PW-A / PW-B` — Prom Week with, and with social reasoning disabled (random action selection).
  - `reversal` — a coded event where an established trend (power relation, goal) turns over.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "We propose to study retellings as a kind of subjectively-grounded playtrace." (Intro)
  - "a process that we term extrapolative narrativization" (Discussion)
  - "we did not allow players enough time to get acclimated prior to retelling construction." (Potential Drawbacks)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **E11.Q1** *Does the design offer evocative, underdetermined hooks (a trait, a coincidence of juxtaposed facts) that a player could extrapolate a motive from without the engine having modelled one?* Take 10 generated entities or events and write down the story a player could build from each. Passes if the design intends and permits that gap (hook without an engine-stated reason). Fails if every fact is either fully explained or without narrative surface. Detects: output that is either over-determined or inert for narrativization.
  - **E11.Q2** *Can the design's output be sorted into the authors' four story types (chronicle, period, biography, day-in-the-life), and what is the distribution?* Read a sample of 20 outputs or retellings and tally. Passes if more than the default chronicle appears and the missing types are explained. Fails if every retelling is an unstructured chronicle. Detects: output that only supports log-reading.
  - **E11.Q3** *Does a variant with one system disabled produce measurably less coherent or harder-to-tell stories?* Run the ablation: disable one narrative-relevant system (replace its choices by random), have the same testers tell stories in both orders, compare reversal density and interview responses. Passes if the system's absence is visible in retellings. Fails if the retellings are indistinguishable, meaning the system is not contributing to narrativization. Detects: a narrative mechanism that does nothing the player can use.
  - **E11.Q4** *Does retelling frequency control for community size and for novice acclimation?* Passes if the count is normalised (they used search results relative to members) and testers had time to learn the game. Fails if raw counts or first-session novices are used. Detects: confounded retelling metrics.
- **Does not license:** Treating the codebook as validated (it grew from the authors' intuition and 40 wild retellings; it has no reliability testing reported). Generalising the Prom Week ablation (two players, one design). Concluding that Stellaris is better than Civ VI at supporting storytelling on this evidence alone: the Reddit counts are hedged (social contagion, and the split between the two students), and the authors say the combined /r/Civ community compounds the gap in Stellaris's favour. Treating the reversal-compellingness link as a measured effect: it is stated as anecdotal. The authors themselves note experienced creators might yield different insights and that surprise questions may have been poorly framed.
- **Links:** Builds on E01 (explicit: retellings as an instrument of critique; Alice and Kev); cited by E02 for the in-the-wild retelling method (E02 credits 'storytelling partnership' to a different Kreminski paper, FDG 2019, not to this one); uses Ryan's curationist narrative as motivation and Comme il Faut (Prom Week) from the text's references.
- **Prior-claim check:**
  - An earlier survey dated this paper 2022: CORRECTED. The text shows AIIDE-19 (2019), copyright 2019, pp. 45–51; authors Kreminski (UCSC), Samuel (Univ. of New Orleans), Melcer (UCSC), Wardrip-Fruin (UCSC).
  - Method / sample sizes / stated limits: reported above (50 gathered and 20 coded per game, 10 codes + 4 story types, 2 students, 8 student retellings, drawbacks section on time cost and acclimation).

### E04_lessard2022 — Lessard, J. & Paré-Chouinard, S. (2022). *Dramatic Situations for Emergent Narrative System Authorship*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper · ICIDS 2022 (venue per label; the text has no proceedings header; it cites works last accessed 2022/06/06) · skipped: reference list, acknowledgements · domain: NARR (with PCG/authoring)
- **Thesis:** Polti's thirty-six dramatic situations are a useful mid-level authoring heuristic for emergent narrative systems: abstract enough not to over-determine output, formal enough to guide low-level mechanics. They are generative because they are independent of, yet productive of, both event sequences and character psychology, and they are likely to be tellable because they are rare, high-stakes moments.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Emergent narrative authoring happens at the level of world-modelling, so designers lack rules of thumb for making good stories more likely; existing precedents (Adams's story-first deduction in Dwarf Fortress, The Sims' embedded story trees, Ryan's literary themes) are either too specific or too vague (§1).
  2. A dramatic situation is a state, not a sequence, so it can be reached by many different causal histories and lead to many outcomes (sequence-independent / sequence-productive) (§2.1).
  3. A situation does not require a prior character psychology and can be what characterises its participants by what they do when facing it (character-independent / character-productive); this also lets a player-character stumble into it without scripted restoration of behaviour (§2.2).
  4. Quality is assessed with the narratological notions of tellability and eventfulness (Hühn's type II events, Schmid's five properties: unpredictability, effect, irreversibility, non-iterativity, relevance); the authors judge it reasonable that situations qualify and note that they are prevalent in successful works (§2.3).
  5. In their Chroniqueur project (world generator, social simulation, encyclopedia-style chronicle interface), situations are not modelled or tracked; mechanics were merely designed so that roughly half of the 36 become reachable (§3).
  6. Polti's list is a convenience, not a limit: designers should pick situations suited to genre and world, and real player retellings are a source of medium-specific situations (§4).
- **Argument structure:** Premises: emergent narrative needs a bridging design concept; Polti's situations are such a concept; the argument is first conceptual (re-theorising Polti with Sartre, Szilas, Hühn). The empirical step is a retelling test: one team member, familiar with the system, ran a new world for about 100 simulated years (2–3 minutes of real time), then had 10 minutes to find a cluster of events containing a supplication and wrote a narrative using the in-game chronicles, three times in the same world and period. Each retelling is then analysed against the three criteria (sequence, character, tellability). Facts come from the simulation while psychological readings are admitted to be projected by the writer. Weakest link: n=3 stories by an insider, a pre-selected situation, and no comparison condition (a system built without situation heuristics), so the claim that the heuristic produces tellable content is untested against any control.
- **Key terms:**
  - `dramatic situation` — a state of the fiction defining abstract roles and conflicting aspirations (e.g. supplication needs a persecutor, a supplicant and an indecisive power); not a plot.
  - `sequence-independent / sequence-productive` — a situation does not depend on specific upstream or downstream events, but its actualisation yields recognisable event sequences.
  - `character-independent / character-productive` — a situation does not require prior characterisation; participants acquire it through what they do.
  - `tellability` — the noteworthiness of a story (narratology), here made operational by Schmid's five eventfulness properties.
  - `retelling test` — writing a story from the system's records, constrained to simulated facts, used as evidence of the system's story potential.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "dramatic situations are not sequences but states" (§2.1)
  - "they solely informed the design of its mechanics" (§3)
  - "Further research will be needed to test this hypothesis." (§4)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **E04.Q1** *Can the design name the target situations (states of roles plus conflicting aims, not plots) its mechanics are meant to make reachable, and for each is there a trace of it arising from mechanics alone?* Passes if every named situation has at least one unscripted play-log instance and the list includes those not reachable. Fails if situations are asserted from rules with no observed occurrence, or are produced by a script or drama manager. Detects: reachability claimed without evidence.
  - **E04.Q2** *Across seeds, does the same situation arise via different causal histories and different casts?* Collect 3+ instances of one situation and compare upstream causes and character attributes. Passes if causes and casts differ in more than names. Fails if instances are the same chain with re-labelled actors. Detects: a hidden script behind a situation.
  - **E04.Q3** *How often does each target situation occur over a long run, and does recurrence erode eventfulness?* Count occurrences per simulated century and check the five eventfulness properties (unpredictability, effect, irreversibility, non-iterativity, relevance) for a sample. Passes if frequency is reported with a judgement that the situation is not routine. Fails if the commonest situation is the same one each run (the authors admit their feast-murder opener is probably too common). Detects: tellable events degenerating into a repeated pattern.
  - **E04.Q4** *Given a fixed time budget (here 10 minutes) and the system's own records, can a reader who did not build it find and write a tellable account containing a target situation, using only logged facts?* Passes if three of three can be produced by a non-author. Fails if only the author can find them or the story needs invented facts. Detects: story potential that exists only in the author's head.
- **Does not license:** Concluding that real players will find these stories (the authors say the deliberate search doesn't allow us to conclude this and that sustained-engagement behaviour is untested). Treating Polti's thirty-six as a validated or exhaustive taxonomy (authors call it convenient and inspirational, and non-limitative). Reading the close to 50% coverage as a quality measure: it is a count of reachable situations by the authors' judgement. Reading the three tellability analyses as evaluation: they were written by the same team that built the heuristic.
- **Links:** Uses E01 (cited as the source for retellings as evidence of emergent story potential, though the text attributes it to Eladhari et al.; the paper has a sole author) and E11 (retellings as a research protocol); engages Ryan (*Curating Simulated Storyworlds*), Aylett, Louchart et al., Szilas et al. Same institution as E02 (Concordia University) `[reader's inference: same research milieu]`.
- **Prior-claim check:** none listed for this ID.

### E05_tension2020 — Kybartas, B., Verbrugge, C. & Lessard, J. (2020). *Tension Space Analysis for Emergent Narrative*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · journal article (arXiv v1 preprint of an IEEE Transactions on Games paper, 22 Apr 2020; the text carries the IEEE copyright line) · skipped: references, acknowledgements, algorithm pseudocode read only for what it computes · domain: NARR (with PCG-style analysis)
- **Thesis:** The narrative potential of a character-driven emergent narrative can be analysed at design time, before any story is generated, by modelling characters as sets of ideal worldviews over a shared fact-space and plotting the distance (tension) between worldviews across all possible world states. The same plot supports authoring by sketch. The method measures structural conflict potential, not story quality.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Emergent narrative authoring is hampered because systems and content interact combinatorially and the impact of a piece of content on the range of possible stories is not defined (§I).
  2. Using Ryan's possible-worlds model, a character is a perceived actual world plus one ideal world per theme; actions are pre/postcondition pairs; all state is truth values over propositions (§IV-A).
  3. Tension is the potential for conflict and can be computed before a narrative exists (Manhattan distance between worlds, ignoring don't care values); conflict is what happens during running (§IV-B).
  4. The tension space is a heatmap over every possible actual-world state of the goal-distance of an x-axis worldview against a y-axis worldview; each action is a movement in it classed per axis as harmony, conflict or no change; strong and weak tension spaces describe how likely conflict is (§V-A).
  5. In a single worked case, the authors' own puzzle game Subject and Subjectivity, the analysis shows the design goals (distinct characters, one ideal suitor, a red herring) as visible patterns in the spaces and movements (§V-B).
  6. The same structures can be run backward: a sketch of movements on the grid is decomposed and fitted to worldviews and actions (A* search for actions), recreating one character in about a minute with five sketches (§VI).
- **Argument structure:** Premise: complexity of simulation defeats intuition; therefore a formal, content-level metric is needed. Moves: formalise the model, borrow expressive range analysis (a heatmap of metric values to see what a generator can reach) but treat the system as a conflict generator because good narrative is unclear; show that the heatmaps reveal known design goals; invert the analysis into an authoring tool. Evidence is a system description plus one retrospective case, designed by the authors before the analysis existed; there is no user study, no other work analysed, and the sketch tool reproduces one of three characters and not all actions. Weakest link: the validated claim is the analysis can see goals the authors already knew they had; nothing shows it can find unknown problems or predict stories players would find interesting, which the paper explicitly places outside scope.
- **Key terms:**
  - `worldview` — an ideal world: a character's desired truth values for propositions under one theme (e.g. personal, family, society).
  - `tension` — the potential for conflict, computed as distance between worlds before play; `conflict` is its realisation in a running simulation.
  - `tension space (TS)` — a heatmap of all possible world states plotted by the goal distance of two worldviews; a rough expressive range for a pair of worldviews.
  - `movement` — the effect of an action on position in a tension space, classed for each axis as conflict, harmony or no change.
  - `strong / weak tension space` — one where most movements harmonise one worldview while conflicting with the other, versus one where an action tends to help or harm both together.

### E07_iens2021 — Kreminski, M. & Mateas, M. (2021). *A Coauthorship-Centric History of Interactive Emergent Narrative*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper · ICIDS 2021 (the text shows authors and a Springer-style layout but no proceedings header; venue and year per label; it cites 2020 works as the latest) · skipped: reference list · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** The history of emergent narrative shows two contrasting purposes bundled under one label: letting the player participate as a character in an authored storyworld, and letting the player author new stories with the system as partner. Discussion and evaluation of interactive emergent narrative (IEN) systems should separate the two so authorship-oriented systems can be designed and judged on their own terms.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. This is not a bibliometric study. Coauthorship means the player co-authoring stories with the system, not co-authorship among researchers; the method is a close reading of key texts, with Table 1 tracking each publication's definition of EN against Louchart and Aylett's three user roles (spectator, participant, author) (§1, Table 1).
  2. Computational EN predates the term (Tale-Spin and others, per J. Ryan), and a rival tradition, author simulation (Dehn, planner-based generation), is not EN (§2).
  3. The term's academic origin (Galyean 1995) and its popularisation (Aylett 1999; Louchart & Aylett 2004) cast the user as participant, with EN presented as a solution to the narrative paradox of structured story versus user autonomy (§3).
  4. Almost in parallel a player-authorship view appears (Mateas 2002, Jenkins 2004, Walsh 2011), which locates narrative construction in the player and stops just short of treating systems as authorship tools (§4).
  5. M.-L. Ryan's and later J. Ryan's expansive definitions subsume both, with J. Ryan's curationist model splitting emergent narrative into raw material, curation and narrativization performed by a human or by a story-sifting system (§5).
  6. The two aims are sometimes compatible but often at odds, e.g. limiting the viewpoint to one character strengthens identification but impedes telling stories about events the character did not witness; a parallel clarifying split already occurred in tabletop RPGs (GM-less versus traditional) (§1, §6).
- **Argument structure:** Premise: the field's term has drifted. Moves: (a) fix a working definition of authorship (Louchart & Aylett: seeking control over direction to give a satisfying structure), (b) read each source's definition and sort it by user role, (c) show the participation strand dominates the Aylett research programme while an authorship strand runs alongside it, (d) argue the confusion mirrors the pre-GM-less tabletop situation. Evidence is a selective reading of about a dozen texts, building on prior histories (Walsh, J. Ryan, Larsen et al.), not a systematic or quantitative survey; the authors also build authorship-oriented games, so the argument is an advocacy position. Weakest link: the sorting of sources into roles is interpretive (they note that Aarseth sits ambiguously between participant and author), and the S/P/A marks of Table 1 are not recoverable from the text extraction.

  - `interactive emergent narrative (IEN)` — emergent narrative as an answer to combining interactivity with narrativity, via simulation and user interaction.
  - `participation play` — the player takes part as a character in an authored storyworld.
  - `authorship play` — the player builds a story out of what the system produces; the computer acts as a storytelling partner.

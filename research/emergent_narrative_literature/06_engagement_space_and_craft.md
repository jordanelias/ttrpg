### G01_sdt2006 — Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski (2006). *The Motivational Pull of Video Games: A Self-Determination Theory Approach*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · journal article (four empirical studies) · Motivation and Emotion 30 · skipped: reference list; most numeric table cells are scrambled in the extraction, so coefficients were taken from the running text · domain: ENG
- **Thesis:** Games are motivating to the extent that play satisfies the basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence and (in multiplayer) relatedness. The authors also claim that the sense of presence or immersion follows from need satisfaction plus intuitive controls.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Perceived in-game autonomy and competence predict enjoyment and preference for future play across games (Studies 1–3, Results).
  2. Only competence predicted a behavioural free-choice measure of continued play in the single-game study (Study 1, Primary analyses).
  3. Intuitive controls are a "price of admission": they relate to motivation only through autonomy and competence, and only in the preferred game (Study 2, Results/Brief discussion).
  4. Presence is associated with need satisfaction and with intuitive controls, so immersion is a downstream effect of need-supporting play (General discussion).
  5. In an online sample, relatedness adds independent prediction of enjoyment and intended future play beyond autonomy and competence, and beats Yee's motive scores on incremental validity (Study 4).
  6. Short-term well-being effects of play are mixed on average (vitality fell) and are moderated by need satisfaction (Studies 1–3, Brief discussions).
- **Argument structure:** The premise is that earlier player typologies (Bartle, Yee) describe the content of current games rather than underlying motives. The authors derive a needs account from self-determination theory, build a questionnaire (PENS), and test it in three lab studies of commercial N64 games and one survey. Study 1: n=89 undergraduates (66 female), 20 minutes of Super Mario 64, with regressions and Baron-Kenny mediation. Study 2: n=50, a crossover of Zelda against A Bug's Life, 40 minutes each, chosen for a rating gap (97.8% vs 56.6%). Study 3: n=58, four games, multilevel models. Study 4: n=730 from one online community (51 female), a cross-sectional survey. All predictors and most outcomes are same-session self-report from new scales (some alphas as low as .63–.66). The weakest link is the causal wording: need satisfaction and enjoyment are measured together, so "predicts" is correlation. Presence is measured by a nine-item scale that mixes physical, emotional and narrative items and is then averaged, so the study cannot say which kind of immersion tracks which need.
- **Key terms:**
  - `PENS` — Player Experience of Need Satisfaction; the authors' new questionnaire (in-game autonomy, competence, relatedness, presence, intuitive controls).
  - `competence` — feeling capable under challenge that is demanding but not overwhelming.
  - `autonomy` — feeling free to choose tasks and means, and not controlled.
  - `presence` — sense of being in the game world as against operating controls; the authors' nine-item scale.
  - `intuitive controls (IC)` — ease of mapping intent to input; a mediated predictor, not an end in itself.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "Competence and autonomy perceptions are also related to the intuitive nature of game controls" (Abstract)
  - "it may be premature to conclude that computer gaming is negatively related to well being" (General discussion)
  - "presence is experienced when games achieve two goals" (General discussion)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **G01.Q1** *Does the design give the player a stated, observable route to feeling effective: graded challenge, legible feedback on results, and controls or inputs the player can learn?* Passes if a play log or doc can point to where a player sees that their action changed an outcome and could predict it. Fails if outcomes are generated without a visible link to player input. Detects: competence starvation (the player is a spectator).
  - **G01.Q2** *Where is player choice over means and goals, and is it informational rather than controlling?* Passes if the player can pick among goals or methods and the design records those choices. Fails if progression is a fixed sequence, or rewards act as compulsion. Detects: autonomy thwarting.
  - **G01.Q3** *If the design claims immersion or presence, which play-level conditions (competent, controllable action) is the claim tied to, and how is it measured?* Passes if the claim is tied to such conditions and measured. Fails if immersion is asserted from narrative or visual richness alone. Detects: immersion asserted without a play-level basis. [reader's inference: the paper ties presence to need satisfaction and controls; it did not vary fiction or graphics.]
  - **G01.Q4** *For any multi-agent or social feature, is relatedness with actual or simulated others made available and measured, or assumed?* Passes if the design names how a player can feel connected. Fails if relatedness is asserted without any such interaction. Detects: unsupported relatedness claim.
- **Does not license:** Do not read this as showing that story or graphics do not create immersion; those were not varied. Do not cite autonomy and competence cause immersion: the data are same-session self-report correlations, and the one non-self-report outcome (free-choice continued play) was 31 vs 54 players in Study 1. Studies 1–3 used mostly female undergraduates, N64 titles from 1996–1999, 20–40 minute sessions; Study 4 is a self-selected, 93% male forum sample. Explained variance for hours played and intended future play was about 10–11% in Study 4. The paper says vitality dropped on average and that well-being effects are short-term only, so it does not show games improve well-being. The authors themselves state the PENS and presence measures were new and under-validated, and that genres like The Sims were untested. Nothing here covers procedural content or emergent story.
- **Links:** Builds on and compares itself with Bartle's player types and Yee's motive model (Study 4 directly). Cites Koster's *A Theory of Fun* for the appeal to psychology (Introduction). [reader's inference] Often invoked alongside MDA's fellowship/challenge aesthetics (G03).
- **Prior-claim check:** (no claim was listed for this ID beyond the brief to report needs, designs, N and the immersion finding) Immersion finding: CONFIRMED as above (General discussion; Study 3 within-person: both autonomy and competence predicted presence; Study 1 only competence).

### G03_mda — Hunicke, LeBlanc & Zubek (2004). *MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · workshop paper (framework / position paper) · Game Developers Conference Game Design and Tuning Workshop materials (AAAI workshop version) · skipped: reference list · domain: ENG
- **Thesis:** A game can be analysed and designed through three linked lenses (mechanics, dynamics, aesthetics), and designer and player read that chain in opposite directions. Design and tuning should therefore start from the intended player experience and work down to rules, not up from features.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Games differ from books, film and music because consumption is unpredictable: the event string and its outcome are unknown when the product is finished (MDA, opening).
  2. Mechanics are components at the level of data and algorithms; dynamics are their run-time behaviour under player input and each other's output; aesthetics are the emotional responses the designer wants evoked (MDA).
  3. Designer's direction is mechanics, then dynamics, then aesthetics; the player's direction is aesthetics first, then observable dynamics, then operable mechanics (MDA in Detail, MDA as Lens).
  4. "Fun" should be replaced by a vocabulary of eight aesthetics: sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, submission (Aesthetics).
  5. Dynamics can be modelled (dice distributions, feedback loops), and a runaway positive loop (Monopoly) destroys tension and agency; the fix is to alter mechanics and playtest (Dynamic Models, Mechanics, Tuning).
  6. There are no "AI mechanics" as such; intelligence comes from the interaction of AI logic with gameplay logic, so an AI component cannot be judged apart from the experience it serves (MDA at Work, Wrapping Up).
- **Argument structure:** The paper asserts a three-layer decomposition and justifies it by illustration, not by test. Premises: games are behaviour-producing artifacts, and small changes cascade between layers. Moves: define the layers, give a taxonomy of aesthetics, then work through Monopoly (a tuning example) and a three-version babysitting AI game that changes audience and so changes mechanics. Conclusion: reason from aesthetic goals to dynamics to mechanics, iterating with playtests. The evidence is conceptual analysis plus worked examples, from the authors' workshop teaching experience; there are no player data. The weakest link is that "aesthetics" is a list of desired experiences with no measurement procedure, so this mechanic produces that aesthetic is a design hypothesis, not a result. The Monopoly fixes are proposed, and only playtesting is said to settle them.
- **Key terms:**
  - `mechanics` — the rules and components at the level of data representation and algorithms (plus the actions afforded).
  - `dynamics` — behaviour of the mechanics running over time on player input.
  - `aesthetics` — the desirable emotional responses; the paper's meaning of "fun", not art style.
  - `lens` — each layer is a view of the game; separate but causally linked.
  - `tuning` — iterative adjustment of values and thresholds against aesthetic goals.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "games are more like artifacts than media" (MDA)
  - "the mechanics give rise to dynamic system behavior" (MDA in Detail)
  - "Understanding games as dynamic systems helps us develop techniques for iterative design and improvement" (Conclusions)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **G03.Q1** *For each mechanic, can the design name the run-time dynamic it is expected to produce and the aesthetic that dynamic serves?* Passes if the chain is stated for the headline mechanics and each link can be observed in a run or log. Fails if mechanics are listed without any named play behaviour, or aesthetics are claimed with no dynamic beneath them. Detects: feature-driven design with no experiential target.
  - **G03.Q2** *Does the design state its target aesthetics from the eight (or another declared closed list), with relative emphasis?* Passes if aesthetics are named and ranked. Fails if the only goal is "fun" or immersion. Detects: undefined experiential goal.
  - **G03.Q3** *Does any feedback loop in the system widen gaps between leaders and trailers, and what restores tension?* Passes if loops are identified and either damped or intended. Fails if a runaway lead makes the later play inert. Detects: positive-feedback collapse (the Monopoly case).
  - **G03.Q4** *Is there a recorded tuning step: a playtest or simulation result that moved a value because of an aesthetic aim?* Passes if a change is tied to an observed dynamic. Fails if the numbers are set once and never revisited. Detects: untuned design asserted as balanced.
- **Does not license:** MDA is a descriptive and pedagogical framework from workshop teaching; it has no empirical validation, and it does not prove that the eight aesthetics are exhaustive or distinct (the paper itself lists "Competition" in examples while it is absent from the list). It gives no way to measure whether a stated aesthetic occurred. The designer/player asymmetry is asserted, not shown. It should not be cited as showing that narrative is an outcome of mechanics; "narrative" here is one aesthetic (game as drama), and the paper says dramatic tension comes from dynamics with rising tension, release, and denouement without showing how.
- **Links:** Cites LeBlanc's own lecture and the GDC workshop materials, Church (formal abstract design tools), Barwood & Falstein (design rules). [reader's inference] Its fellowship/challenge/expression vocabulary overlaps with SDT's relatedness/competence/autonomy (G01); the source does not say this.
- **Prior-claim check:** (no specific claims listed beyond the brief) Chain, eight aesthetics and designer/player direction: CONFIRMED (MDA; MDA in Detail; Aesthetics).

### G04_fernandez2011 — Fernández-Vara (2011). *Game Spaces Speak Volumes: Indexical Storytelling*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference paper (author manuscript) · DiGRA 2011 · skipped: bibliography and ludography · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** Environmental storytelling is too broad; the useful core is "indexical storytelling", in which a story is built from traces (signs physically connected to past events) left by designers or players in the game space. It matters for design because reading and leaving traces is tied to how the player acts in the game, not just to world flavour.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Environmental storytelling, borrowed from theme parks, covers many strategies and fits games awkwardly because park designers treat the visitor as a viewer, not an active agent (Environmental Storytelling).
  2. Following Peirce, an index is a sign connected to its referent, often as a consequence of it (smoke, footprints, bloodstains); indexical storytelling is story told mainly through such traces, so the player reconstructs events instead of being told them (Definition; In Practice).
  3. It is "story-building" by both designer and player, not storytelling in the traditional sense (In Practice; Conclusion).
  4. It serves two histories, of the game world and of the player; the world's history includes detective reconstruction, interpretation of remains (including "ghosts"), and signage/tutorials (History of the Game World).
  5. Remains alone are often not tied to gameplay, whereas indices that cue what to do (or not do) restore behaviour; tutorials phrased in controller terms break the fiction (Interpretation of Remains; Signage and Tutorials).
  6. Player-made traces require a persistent world (state not reset) and, to become story, other agents or players must be able to read and react to them (Player's Traces).
- **Argument structure:** The paper surveys existing environmental-storytelling theory (Carson, Jenkins, Nitsche, and the 2010 GDC talks by Rouse and by Smith and Worch). It then brings in Peirce's icon/index/symbol distinction to carve out a narrower concept, and illustrates it with a series of game examples (Myst, Deadline, Portal, Bioshock, Demon's Souls, Metal Gear Solid 2, Colossal Cave, Castlevania). It differs from background or backstory narrative in one respect: the trace has to matter to play (it cues, warns, or can be acted on), whereas remains whose meaning is purely for interpretation are classed as environmental storytelling that is "not directly relevant to gameplay". It also separates itself from Smith and Worch's systemic example (Half-Life graffiti and bullet marks), which it calls involuntary and an afterthought. The evidence is conceptual analysis plus selected examples; there is no player study, so claims about what players infer or enjoy are assertions. The weakest link is the claim that the category is new and "productive": the examples are chosen to fit, and the paper says the practice already existed.
- **Key terms:**
  - `indexical storytelling` — narrative constructed mainly from indices: traces left by past events or actions, which the player must interpret.
  - `index` — a sign physically tied to what it points to (smoke for fire, a bloodstain for a wound); contrasted with icon and symbol.
  - `restore behavior` — the player re-enacting a script that the game space affords (borrowed from performance theory).
  - `remains` — traces of earlier agents' actions in the space, including "ghosts".
  - `extradiegetic index` — a cue (tutorial, GTA arrow) that points outside the fictional world to controller or rules.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "The novel concept of indexical storytelling refers specifically to generating stories through traces" (Indexical Storytelling: Definition)
  - "Persistence is not as common as one would think" (History of the Player: Player's Traces)
  - "the story can be told through objects, the remains of previous events" (Conclusion)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **G04.Q1** *Which in-world traces (state left by earlier events or by the player) can the player observe, and does each point to a specific cause that can be reconstructed?* Passes if a named trace is generated from a real prior event and an observer can infer it. Fails if the "evidence" is placed decoration with no event behind it. Detects: set-dressing standing in for history.
  - **G04.Q2** *Do the traces change what the player (or an agent) does next?* Passes if a trace cues, warns, or alters another agent's behaviour in an observable way. Fails if traces are read only for flavour. Detects: story detached from play.
  - **G04.Q3** *Is the world persistent enough that player actions leave lasting traces, or are areas reset?* Passes if state changes are kept and later visible to others. Fails if changes vanish on leaving. Detects: non-persistent world defeating trace-based story.
  - **G04.Q4** *Can non-player agents read player-made traces and respond?* Passes if a player-made trace causes a logged reaction by another agent. Fails if only the player interprets them. Detects: one-way trace system.
- **Does not license:** This is a single-author position paper with selected examples and no empirical testing, so it does not show indexical storytelling works better than other approaches, or that players actually reconstruct stories. The paper says interpretations will vary by player and that some remains are not tied to play. Its examples are mostly authored, handcrafted spaces (Bioshock, Portal); the paper does not address generating traces by simulation. The claim that tutorials are inherently a problem is tied to controller-referencing text of 2011-era games. It does not claim that story-building is narrative, and ends by leaving that open.
- **Links:** Builds on and refines Carson (G05) via Jenkins, Nitsche, Rouse, and Smith & Worch; cites Carson for the theme-park origin and critiques that origin (Environmental Storytelling). Uses Bioshock as an example (G06's subject) for remains.
- **Prior-claim check:** "differs from background or embedded narrative": CORRECTED/PARTLY: the text contrasts indexical storytelling with world-building and backstory exposition (Abstract) and with environmental storytelling as a broad concept, and with Smith and Worch's "systemic" example; the phrase "embedded narrative" does not appear in the source.

### G05_carson2000 — Carson (2000). *Environmental Storytelling: Creating Immersive 3D Worlds Using Lessons Learned from the Theme Park Industry*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · practitioner essay (trade-press feature) · Gamasutra, 1 March 2000 · skipped: site navigation, author biography, latest news and podcast listings at the end · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** Games can borrow theme-park practice: put the story into the physical space, so that the environment itself tells the player where they are, what has happened and what to expect. The designer's job is to draw the audience in and make them stay, using setting, props and lighting as the narrator.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. "Story" means a coherent set of world rules (a "big picture", not a linear plot) that unifies the team and must not be broken, or players feel cheated (The Importance of Story).
  2. A player must be able to answer "Where am I?" in about 15 seconds and "What is my relationship to this place?" soon after, ideally by clues in the environment (Where Am I?).
  3. "Cause and effect" vignettes (broken doors, wreckage, a returned-to place found altered, trails to follow) let players deduce events and danger, and may be triggered by the player's own actions (Storytelling Through Cause and Effect).
  4. Anchor alien spaces with something familiar, and use contrast, asymmetry, restraint in textures and lighting, and guided attention, as in theatre (Familiar; Remember, This is a Theatre; Contrasting Elements).
  5. Environment must serve playability; a story chosen early lets a team argue over details rather than redo the look late (Paradox of Designing for "Gamers").
  6. Wished-for future: exploration that builds relationships and tests characters, not frag counts or timed repeat puzzles (Missed Opportunities).
- **Argument structure:** The author is a former Disney Imagineer and moves from experience: theme parks and 3D games share the challenge of immersing visitors; theme-park tricks work; therefore games should use them. Evidence is the author's career anecdotes (the Pirates of the Caribbean canvas buildings, a programmer's flickering torches) and a few game examples (Half-Life, Duke Nukem 3D, Myst); there are no player data, no comparisons and no measure of immersion. The practical rules are specific and checkable in a build (15-second orientation, no rule-breaking, props that mean something). The weakest link is the repeated assertion that these techniques make players "immersed" or pulled in, which is never tested, and that the park audience is the same as the game audience.
- **Key terms:**
  - `environmental storytelling` — story infused into the physical space a person moves through, so space conveys the narrative.
  - `story` — in this essay, the world's governing rules and "big picture", explicitly not a linear plot.
  - `cause and effect vignette` — a staged scene that implies a prior event or a danger ahead.
  - `Following Saknussemm` — Carson's name for leaving a trail of traces by an earlier character for the player to follow.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "the story element is infused into the physical space a guest walks or rides through" (Environmental Storytelling)
  - "give them asymmetry whenever possible" (Using Contrasting Elements)
  - "Use your environments to draw us in deep" (Missed Opportunities, closing)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **G05.Q1** *Within the first moments of play, what tells the player where they are and what their role is, without a text dump?* Passes if the opening state yields both answers from observed cues (log of the player's first minute, or a screenshot-level description). Fails if both require reading external lore. Detects: orientation failure.
  - **G05.Q2** *Does the design state its world rules and is there any instance where content breaks them?* Passes if a rule list exists and no shipped element contradicts it. Fails if elements are added for being "cool". Detects: world-rule inconsistency.
  - **G05.Q3** *For each environmental or world-state cue, which prior event does it depict, and can that event be traced to the simulation?* Passes if cue and event are both recorded. Fails if the cue is only decoration. Detects: unsourced scenery.
  - **G05.Q4** *Do places change over time or in response to the player, and is the change visible when the player returns?* Passes if a return visit shows altered state tied to events. Fails if places are static or reset. Detects: static world.
- **Does not license:** This is an opinion essay by one practitioner; no claim about immersion, retention, or players prefer self-discovery is tested, and the author concedes a gap between beautiful and playable. The concept is about authored, spatial, mostly visual 3D levels of 1999–2000 and does not address text, systems, strategy-scale worlds or procedurally generated events. The "15 seconds" figure is asserted without a source. It should not be cited as showing that environmental storytelling is effective, only as the origin and a checklist of craft techniques. Fernández-Vara (G04) treats the theme-park framing as problematic because park visitors are not active agents.
- **Links:** The origin text cited by G04 (Fernández-Vara) for environmental storytelling. Mentions Half-Life, Duke Nukem 3D, Myst. No connection to Bioshock (G06) in the text.
- **Prior-claim check:** (no claims listed beyond the brief) Principles extracted as above; assertions without evidence flagged in Argument structure and Does not license.

### G06_hocking2007 — Hocking (2007). *Ludonarrative Dissonance in Bioshock*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · blog post (single-game critique) · Click Nothing (TypePad blog), 7 October 2007 · skipped: site sidebars; the comment thread was only glanced at and is not used as evidence · domain: NARR
- **Thesis:** Bioshock's mechanics and its plot send opposite messages about what the player should do, and this contradiction undercuts both. The post names the ludic and the narrative as two "contracts" with the player, and argues that when they conflict, the player is left unable to feel connected to either.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. The post is a critique for developers, not a review; the author likes the game and sets out a limited criticism of "the fabric of the game" (opening, and "First, this is not a review").
  2. The ludic contract is "seek power and you will progress", which aligns with Randian self-interest, and the Little Sisters choice successfully makes the player feel that theme through play (The game begins by offering two contracts).
  3. The narrative contract is "help Atlas and you will progress"; it conflicts with the ludic one because it frames helping others as right, because Atlas opposes the philosophy the mechanics teach, and because the player has no choice to refuse (three problems).
  4. The mechanics offer freedom to harvest or rescue; the fiction offers none about Atlas, so a player who adopts the mechanical philosophy has no story path except to stop playing (That's the dissonance).
  5. The later reveal that the player's obedience was dictated by the narrative, not by a ludic constraint the player accepted, turns a disturbing contradiction into an insult after a long commitment (the "mocked" passage).
  6. The remedy implied is games that "marry their ludic and narrative themes into a consistent and fully realized whole" (closing).
- **Argument structure:** Premises: a game communicates through both systems (ludic) and story (narrative), and a good game's two should agree. Moves: identify the theme, show that the mechanics carry it, then show that the plot's required action runs against it, and then argue the reveal makes the contradiction hostile. The evidence is one critic-designer's own playthrough and felt response; the only outside data is a link to analysis of whether harvesting is in fact the best choice (the author notes rescue is not worse in net terms). The weakest link is that "dissonance" is demonstrated through the author's reaction and a reading of the Little Sisters as a mechanics-expressed philosophy, which he admits he only "half" understands. Note that the compound term ludonarrative dissonance appears in the title only; the body argues the same thing with dissonance between ... game ... story, so the term is coined by headline and not formally defined.
- **Key terms:**
  - `ludonarrative dissonance` — conflict between what a game is about as play (its mechanics' incentives) and what it is about as story.
  - `ludic contract` — the implicit offer made by the rules: what the mechanics reward.
  - `narrative contract` — the offer made by the plot: what the story asks the player to do.
  - `critique (vs review)` — analysis for practitioners of what a game means, as against purchase advice.
- **Verbatim anchors:**
  - "a powerful dissonance between what it is about as a game" (opening)
  - "the game cannot be enjoyed as both a game and a story" (opening)
  - "The game openly mocks us for having willingly suspended our disbelief" (That's the dissonance)
- **Apparatus — tests for a design:**
  - **G06.Q1** *State what the rules reward and what the story praises; do they agree?* Passes if the incentive of the most-played action matches the value the fiction attaches to it. Fails if the system pays for what the fiction condemns (or the reverse). Detects: ludic/narrative contradiction.
  - **G06.Q2** *Does the narrative require any action that the mechanics let the player decline, or the reverse?* Passes if every required story beat is reachable by the choices the mechanics offered. Fails if the plot overrides an established mechanical choice. Detects: forced-choice override.
  - **G06.Q3** *If the fiction comments on the player's behaviour, is the comment consistent with what the player was actually shown and allowed?* Passes if the narrative's judgments match recorded player options. Fails if the story blames or mocks the player for a constraint of the design. Detects: story that mocks the player's compliance.
  - **G06.Q4** *Where a moral choice is offered, does the mechanical payoff depend on the choice or equalise it?* Passes if the cost of the choice is felt in play. Fails if both branches pay the same, making the choice cosmetic. Detects: choice without consequence. [reader's inference: this test comes from the post's side note on whether harvesting is net best, not from its main argument.]
- **Does not license:** One designer's critique of one game, from his own play and reaction; there is no player data, and the post itself says it is partial, half-understood and not a review. It does not establish that the effect is general or that players are bothered by it, nor does it define a measurable threshold of "dissonance". The coined phrase is often used to mean any story-gameplay mismatch; this post argues a narrower case: a conflict between two incentives the game itself sets up, plus the reveal that the story overrode the player's compliance. The comments dispute whether Bioshock's mechanics really encode self-interest; those were not analysed here. Games that are purely systemic (no authored plot) fall outside what the post addresses.
- **Links:** Does not cite other corpus items. Mentions Bogost's critique of Bully and his own GDC 2004 talk on simulation boundaries. Bioshock is also an example in G04. [reader's inference] The "contracts" framing is a relative of MDA's player/designer split (G03), but the post never says so.
- **Prior-claim check:** (definition requested) The term is used in the title and defined by the body's argument, not by a stated definition: CORRECTED/QUALIFIED: the words "ludonarrative dissonance" occur only in the title; the body defines the problem as a dissonance between what the game is about as a game and as a story (Hocking §opening).

### G10_booth2009 — Booth (2009). *The AI Systems of Left 4 Dead*
- **Read:** `P (full)` · conference talk slides (practitioner system description) · Valve; slides only, no speaker notes in the extraction · skipped: repeated animation-step slides with no text (the many "Reactive Path Following" frames) · domain: PCG
- **Thesis:** In Left 4 Dead, four goals (robust behaviour, competent bot stand-ins for humans, replayability, dramatic pacing) are served by procedural population and an "AI Director" that varies enemy pressure from an estimate of each player's emotional intensity. Booth claims simple algorithms are enough to produce compelling pacing.
- **Core arguments:**
  1. Static placement of enemies and loot lets players memorise an optimal route, kills suspense and cooperation, and even several hand-placed script variants get learned (Promote Replayability).
  2. Structured unpredictability populates the level by layering population functions (wanderers, mobs, specials, bosses, caches, items) with designer-set randomness in space and time, relative to the team's position along the flow distance and an "Active Area Set" (Populating via Structured Unpredictability).
  3. Pacing is borrowed from Counter-Strike's "spiky" rhythm: constant combat is fatiguing and long inactivity is boring, so the Director makes peaks and valleys (Generate dramatic game pacing).
  4. The algorithm: estimate each Survivor's emotional intensity, track the maximum of the four, and remove major threats for a while if it is too high; intensity rises with damage taken, incapacitation, being pushed off ledges, and nearby infected deaths, and decays toward zero unless infected are engaging the survivor (Adaptive Dramatic Pacing).
  5. The pacing state machine is Build Up, Sustain Peak (3-5 s after the peak threshold), Peak Fade (wait for a natural break), Relax (minimal threats for 30-45 s or until the team has travelled far enough), then Build Up again; bosses are unaffected (Adaptive Dramatic Pacing).
  6. It adjusts frequency of peaks and not their amplitude: "pacing, not difficulty" (Adaptive Dramatic Pacing reacts to Survivor team).
- **Argument structure:** The talk is a system description, not an evaluation. Goals are listed, then each is met by a mechanism, with rationale from the developers' earlier experience (Counter-Strike, Team Fortress). The pacing claim rests on one premise (varied intensity is more compelling than constant intensity), a crude intensity estimator, and a threshold/timer state machine; the conclusion is that it "works".

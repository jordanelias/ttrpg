# Venice: how cases, debates and heresy charges actually moved, stage by stage

## Status: FILED (reference, tier 3; informs design, settles nothing)

**Scope.** The repo already holds Venice's offices and the 1268 doge election (`research/governance/`). This file adds PROCEDURE in three parts: (1) the criminal and political courts (Signori di Notte, Avogaria di Comun, Quarantia Criminal, Council of Ten and its Zonta, State Inquisitors), (2) how the Senate and Great Council debated, balloted and elected, (3) Venice's dealings with Rome in heresy cases (the Venetian Holy Office with its three lay assessors, Bruno, the 1606 Interdict, Sarpi, the Foscarini affair) as the model of crown-over-church modulation. Every claim carries a source tag (key at the end). `[FROM MEMORY, UNVERIFIED]` marks what I could not source this session; `I don't know` marks a hole. **Reliability warning:** the best procedural narrative I could read in full is Contarini (written in the 1520s-30s, printed 1543, English 1599), a patrician's idealised description of the rules as they were meant to work, not a record of practice. Where a modern secondary summary adds or contradicts him, both are given. Most modern secondary sources here were reached through web summaries, not page-checked; they are flagged `[SUMMARY]`.

## Part 1. The courts

### 1.0 Routing: who takes which matter

| Matter | First seat | Who tries | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Petty disorder, vagrancy, theft, some homicide, sodomy (early) | the six night officers (Signori di Notte), one per sestiere | night officers themselves for light penalties; otherwise they pass the file to a college of judges | CONT p.96-97; TRECC-PG |
| Grave crime, or crime by a person of rank | the three Avogadori di Comun (they alone accuse; no private accuser) | the Forty (Quarantia Criminal); the Senate "seldom", the Great Council "most seldom" | CONT p.85-89, 93 |
| Offences against civil concord, state secrets, coinage, sodomy, blasphemy, later patricians' private lives | the Council of Ten (17 voters: ten plus Doge plus six councillors), with a Zonta | Ten (+ Zonta) | CONT p.77-81; ENW-TEN ("from 1624", patricians' private lives) |
| Crimes against the state, leaking of secrets | three State Inquisitors drawn from the Ten and the ducal councillors | the Inquisitors, unanimously; heavier penalties go to the Ten | ITW-INQ; SANTIN; LESSICO |
| Heresy | the Venetian Holy Office (Part 3) | tribunal of 3 clerics and 3 lay assessors | ENW-HI |

Contarini's own summary of the allocation: "all other great and weightie crimes" beyond the Ten's are reported by the Avogadori and "well pondered and debated" by the Forty (CONT p.84-85).

### 1.1 The two rites

Scholars describe Venetian criminal law as mixing an accusatory process with the medieval inquisitorial one (Cozzi, *Authority and the Law in Renaissance Venice*, pp.293-345; I could not read the scanned PDF, so this is from a search-result gloss of it: WARWICK-COZZI `[SUMMARY]`). A second gloss says judges were bound to open a trial on mere suspicion (ARIEL, not opened; `[SUMMARY]`). In practice the two rites differ on every key a contest needs:

| | Accusatory-looking: Avogadori before the Forty (CONT) | Inquisitorial: the Ten and Inquisitors (CONT, SANTIN, ITW-DIECI) |
|---|---|---|
| Who opens | an Avogador names the accused; no private person may accuse | the Ten, on a vote after a denunciation, or on its own agents' report |
| Is the accused heard | yes, with advocates, in open pleading | no: excluded from the debate; deliberation on the dossier only |
| Does the accused see the evidence | yes, a copy of the written record | no copy, no names of accusers or witnesses |
| Counsel | yes, two at public cost if poor | not admitted in the hall (CONT); in practice the defence is written and "often drafted by outsiders" (SANTIN, citing Cozzi) |
| Verdict | secret ballot, majority of balls | secret ballot; thresholds by stage |
| Review | none; the Forty's capital sentence is final (CONT p.93) | none in law; revision "after many years", rarely granted (SANTIN) |

### 1.2 Stages A: the night officers (Signori di Notte), minor and early-stage matters

1. A watch: each of the six night officers patrols his sestiere every other night with officers and sergeants (CONT p.97-98).
2. Arrest and inquest: they "throughly examine the cause", write down witness depositions and "whatsoever else the party hath confessed, eyther of his owne accord, or els by the constraint of torture" (CONT p.96).
3. Light offences (whipping, imprisonment): the captains of the night "dispatch the matter" themselves, including imprisoning or torturing without asking any college. Contarini sets this against the Avogadori, who lack that authority and must first inform the college (CONT p.96-97).
4. Capital cause: the file goes to a college of judges for sentence. Contarini names them "iudges of the Properties" (Giudici del Proprio); an Italian summary agrees that the night lords "istruivano" theft, some homicide and sodomy cases and then "li portavano ai giudici del proprio" (TRECC-PG, 14th c.). The two sources agree on the shape; whether that was still so by 1500 I don't know.
5. Handover upward: in sodomy the Ten later took over (a commentary on Ruggiero's *Boundaries of Eros* says so `[SUMMARY]`; date and mechanism: I don't know).

### 1.3 Stages B: Avogaria to Quarantia Criminal (CONT p.85-93, read in full unless marked)

1. **Intake and charge.** Crime brought to the Avogadori. If they think it worth their censure, one of them (the charge "belonging chiefly to three") is entered as accuser, "declared and argued to be guiltie". The Avogaria itself is a screen: it makes "a preliminary evaluation of evidence and procedural correctness" and decides which cases go to full trial (EMV-AVOGARIA `[SUMMARY]`). Under the 14th-c. rule the denunciation had to be signed if private, or could come from a security council (TRECC-PG).
2. **Choosing the bench.** The Avogador reports to whichever council he prefers; usually the Forty; "greater" matters to the Senate; "greatest" to the Great Council (CONT p.85, 90). Contarini calls the Avogadori "Tribunes of the lawes" but notes the Ten's rise had diminished them.
3. **Coercive step decided by the bench.** The council debates whether the accused is to be imprisoned, tortured, or left at liberty to defend; he is summoned by decree or "secretly apprehended" by the Avogadori's officers (p.86). Decision on torture sits with the council, not the accuser.
4. **Examination and written record.** The accused answers; witnesses and proofs are produced "on each side"; each item is "registred in writing" for both sides; a copy goes to him; a recess is set so he can instruct his advocates (p.86-87).
5. **Counsel.** If he cannot pay, "two advocates are appointed at the charge of the commonwealth", because "no one doe receiue punishment without being first admitted to say what hee can in iustification of himselfe" (p.87). (A modern gloss says some statute parts gave legal help to the poor: TRECC-PG.)
6. **Trial day.** The accusation belongs to the Avogadori, to be "sharpe" yet without railing; the advocate answers; either side may speak again until one says it has no more (p.87-89). Only the Avogadori may accuse, to spare private citizens feud and hatred (p.88).
7. **Retirement.** The accused and his advocates are sent out. The Avogadori are shut in with the judges and secretaries (p.89).
8. **First question: guilt alone.** The Avogadori move that the accused be punished without naming a penalty. Three pots: white (condemn), green (absolve), red (doubtful). Each judge drops a linen ball secretly. The presidents count (p.89-90).
9. **Counting.** More than half of all balls in white: condemned. More than half in green: acquitted and released, the request rejected. Neither: if the red leads, adjourn to a new day; at a second balloting, red may again lead and adjourn; at the third, "no farther regard" is held to the doubtful and the case must resolve into acquittal or condemnation (p.90-91).
10. **Penalty as a second vote.** The Avogadori propose the harshest penalty for the offence ("their office being more to incline to seueritie than to mercie"); the heads of the Forty and the councillors propose lighter ones, "vnlesse the fault be so hainous". The penalty is then chosen by the same method used for Senate bills: drop the opinion with fewest balls and re-ballot until one has more than half (p.91-92).
11. **Finality and secrecy.** "No citizen nor magistrate" may be absolute judge; judges vote by secret suffrage, never aloud, so as to judge "much more freely" (p.92-93). The Forty's sentence in capital cases is "ratified and firme without admitting any appeale" (p.93).
**What differs in practice (disputed or modern).** (a) The penalty the Avogadori proposed "rarely won a majority"; the penalty backed by the most powerful judges prevailed (TRECC-PG, a 14th-c. gloss; Cozzi per WARWICK-COZZI `[SUMMARY]`). Contarini says only that heavier proposals come from the Avogadori and lighter from the others, which is compatible. (b) A capital sentence needed confirmation by the Signoria to be executable (a search gloss of Treccani; not opened `[SUMMARY]`). Contarini does not mention it. (c) For homicide where guilt was not manifest, "la coscienza e la discrezione del giudice" decided (TRECC-PG), i.e. no fixed arithmetic of proof. Whether the Quarantia ever applied a two-witness or half-proof rule: I don't know.

### 1.4 Stages C: the Council of Ten (+ Zonta)

*Composition (CONT p.78-81; DAMOSTO ?p=7235):* ten councillors elected yearly by the Great Council, no two of one family, none re-elected at once; the Doge and six ducal councillors, making 17; three Capi, monthly, who convene the council and make the report (a Capo cannot report alone: two Capi or four councillors must). A Zonta (adjunct): Contarini counts fifteen senators, making 32 voters; Da Mosto says it began in the Faliero trial (1355), twenty members, reduced to fifteen in 1529, lapsing in 1582 when none of the fifteen was confirmed by the Great Council; one Avogador attends and cannot vote but can propose, accuse members and suspend. English Wikipedia says the Zonta was "about 15 to 20" and sat "until 1582" (ENW-TEN).

1. **Intake.** Written denunciation in a Bocca di Leone ("Boche de Leon") set round the city and in the Palace. The box legend: *Denontie secrete per via d'inquisizione contra cada una persona con l'impunita secreteza et benefitii giusto alle leggi* (DLF). Other feeds: capi di sestiere and the Ten's own informers (ITW-DIECI). Rules on admission, as reported:
   - a 1387 law: unsigned accusations in the Ten's box burned unconsidered (search-result gloss `[SUMMARY]`);
   - 1542: anonymous blasphemy denunciations admitted only if three eyewitnesses named (same gloss `[SUMMARY]`);
   - a 1635 law: four-fifths to proceed; a 1647 law: first, unanimity of the councillors and Capi plus at least five-sixths of the Ten to declare the matter one of state, then four-fifths to accept it (SANTIN, `[SUMMARY]`);
   - weapons, masks and boats: two testimonies before the Avogadori first (SANTIN);
   - Italian Wikipedia gives the thresholds differently (four-fifths for signed, five-sixths for anonymous; ITW-DIECI). The sources conflict; I cannot reconcile them.
2. **Screening and tailing.** The Capi guide the investigation (ITW-DIECI). Two Inquisitors are elected monthly to investigate (DAMOSTO). Before examining witnesses, at least two or three spies verify the denunciation (SANTIN). The Avogador reviews the file with the Ten and helps decide whether to dismiss or open the special inquiry (SANTIN).
3. **Coercive acts.** Arrest, search and torture are decided by a majority of the Ten; the Inquisitors dei Dieci cannot act alone (SANTIN). Torture is used "only after the general inquiry leaves guilt certain"; a doctor is consulted; the confession is void unless repeated the next day; in Venice it precedes the defence, in the Terraferma it follows (SANTIN `[SUMMARY]`).
4. **Examination.** Witnesses swear two oaths, *de veritate* and *de silentio*; no confrontation of witness with witness or accused with witness (only among co-accused); a witness who denies his deposition may be held and examined in the torture room; two notaries record (SANTIN). Contarini: the Heads examine the accused, "his confession written", then the matter is reported to the college (p.80).
5. **Defence.** The accused "is never suffered to come into the college", nor friend nor lawyer (CONT p.80). He may show public acts, not private writings; he cannot learn the accusers; he gets no copy (SANTIN). In practice defence is written. Contarini adds that the Heads and judges "plead as well in the behalf of the accuser as of the offender" (p.80).
6. **Debate and vote.** The order of votes: Avogador (proposes only), Inquisitori, Capi, Consiglieri, Doge. If no majority emerges after five votes, the accused can be freed, the case sent elsewhere, or the process "reformed"; a conviction takes four further confirming votes (SANTIN; the exact meaning of "four further votes" is unclear to me). To undo a settled act the Ten needs two-thirds of its college (CONT p.79-80).
7. **Sentence and sanctions.** Death by beheading, hanging, or night drowning in the lagoon; but exile (*bando*) and confinement (*relegazione*) are more common (ITW-DIECI). Preventive detention is long and added to the sentence, not deducted (SANTIN). Public pronouncement: Doge Faliero was beheaded publicly and his painted portrait erased (CONT p.81-82); Foscarini's body was displayed head-down between the Piazzetta pillars (ENW-FOSC).
8. **Appeal and review.** None from the Ten (ITW-DIECI; SANTIN; DLF "inappellabile"). Revision after years, seldom granted. The Avogador's presence is "the main safeguard" and he "could suspend or challenge" its trials (ITW-DIECI). After the 1582 reform each Avogador could contest the Ten's measures (DAMOSTO). The Capi decide last-instance appeals against censors' sentences (DAMOSTO) and, for rectors' judgments under the Ten's rite, subjects appealed to the Capi dei Dieci twice, then to the Council (SANTIN).
9. **Secrecy.** Oath of secrecy for all members (ENW-TEN); witness interrogations secret and leaks punished; denouncers' identities kept in a separate archive; the last phase excluded relatives of the accused and any council members who had denounced him (SANTIN). Contrast: ordinary hearings before the Forty are open to the parties; only the ballot is secret.

### 1.5 Stages D: the State Inquisitors

1. **Founding and seats.** Three: two "black" from the Ten and one "red" ducal councillor (ITW-INQ). The standing magistracy dates from 20 Sept 1539 per ITW-INQ; the 1851 lexicon says decrees of 20 Sept 1539 and 19 Apr 1583 made them permanent (LESSICO). I follow neither as the single date.
2. **Acting.** Acts must be unanimous; a single inquisitor's urgent arrest is void if the others disagree (SANTIN). They send spies first, interrogate witnesses and conduct the process "rapidly" (LESSICO). Two further "Inquisitori dei Dieci" may vote but not propose guilt or penalty (SANTIN).
3. **Sentence.** The three concurring votes are the sentence; it is "published in the Great Council" (LESSICO) or at the next session of the sovereign council after notice to the Ten (SANTIN), unless secrecy requires otherwise (ITW-INQ). Lighter penalties are theirs; a harsher one goes to the Ten (SANTIN).
4. **Appeal.** None (SANTIN, ITW-INQ). Review of other magistrates' files needs all three and a unanimous, written, signed decision (SANTIN).
5. **Standard of proof.** English Wikipedia says only that they "applied a low standard of proof" (ENW-TEN); no primary text found.

### 1.6 Avogadori: review, suspension and intromissione

The word has more than one sense in my sources, which agree on function but not on a single definition:
- **Intake-investigation stage** of the 14th-c. criminal process: after denunciation comes the *intromissione*, collection of testimony and proofs by the Avogadori or the Signori di Notte; then the *placitare* (the case set out before the court, defence allowed); then the vote; then the proposal of penalty; then its approval (TRECC-PG, six stages).
- **Right of review of an appeal's admissibility:** after the Auditori's creation (1343) the Avogaria "kept only the right of intromissione", i.e. judging whether an appeal to the Forty could be admitted, limited after 1343 to criminal matters (DAMOSTO ?p=6824, `[SUMMARY]`).
- **Suspension of unlawful acts:** at least one Avogador must attend the Great Council and the Pregadi and can suspend deliberations that violate the laws; in the Ten he is without vote but may propose and challenge (DAMOSTO). Elsewhere: "protection of constitutional legality" and "power to suspend the unconstitutional measures" (ENW-AVOGARIA). Since 1400 the Doge could be taken to court (same). Avogadori could themselves be prosecuted for negligence before the Forty (same).
- **Prosecution of councillors** by complaint (DAMOSTO). The Avogadori keep the registers of those admitted to the Great Council (ENW-GC).
- **Number and term:** regularly three, plus two extraordinary ones on occasion; term one year, 16 months from 1314; a cooling-off period of equal length from 1551 (DAMOSTO).

I did not find who then decides after a suspension (the Council itself again, a higher body, a time limit) in any source I could read. Setti 2014 (*Acta Histriae* 22/1, pp.127-144) would answer it; the PDF was not retrievable. **I don't know** the post-suspension mechanics.

## Part 2. Senate and Great Council

### 2.1 The Senate (Pregadi), CONT p.68-76 unless marked

1. **Business arrives at the College.** The Collegio is the Doge, the councillors and sixteen Savi (six of the first order for state and war; five of the second for the army; five of the third for the sea). Mornings until three o'clock go to petitioners, then letters are read; the Savi withdraw to consult (p.70-72).
2. **Deliberation among the Savi.** The week's president (a seven-day rotation) states the question and asks each in turn, first order first, last-week's president first, then by age, the proposer last; sea matters only from the sea order (p.72-73). They then report to the Doge and councillors, who may lean to one opinion or add a reason; the matter is debated again and each opinion is "put in writing" by the secretaries (p.73).
3. **The proposal is a menu of written opinions**, not a single motion: the Senate hears all Savi's opinions "whether all of one mind or of sundry" (p.73-74).
4. **Speeches.** The week's first-order president rises, or the eldest; speakers address from a raised platform "modestly, and with grauity", in turn, each answering the last. **Floor rule:** no ordinary senator may speak "till the Sages that will take the matter vpon them, haue made an end"; after that any senator may speak (p.74-75). **Initiative rule:** a senator who devises a new opinion may not report it; "one of the Colledge, or a counsellor, or the President of the forty" does (p.75).
5. **Ballot.** The secretaries bring as many pots as there are opinions, plus a green pot (reject all) and a red pot (doubtful). Each senator drops one linen ball in secret. The councillors count (p.75).
6. **Counting.** An opinion with "halfe the senators" is enacted. If none has half, the opinion with fewest balls is dropped and the vote is repeated until two remain; one of them must exceed half (p.75-76). The same procedure picks a penalty among rival proposals (p.91-92).
7. **Next item.** Unless late, the next matter follows the same way. Foreign envoys are heard by the Doge, councillors and the College; the Senate decides the answer and a secretary reads it to them (p.76).

*Quorum and size.* Contarini: 120 senators (60 elected by lot-and-ballot, 60 "adjuncts" by judgment), "above 220" with ex officio members (Ten, Forty, procurators, etc.) (p.66-68). English Wikipedia: roughly 230 voting, quorum 70, typical attendance about 180; some senators could vote only ("por ballotta"), some could only propose ("por parte") (ENW-SEN; unsourced figures in the article). A Zonta of adjuncts was added from 1363 and fixed from 1506 (ENW-SEN). The Avogadori sit in the Senate from 1293 (ENW-SEN).

### 2.2 The Great Council: election by lot-and-ballot (CONT p.22-39)

1. **Setting.** Held on holidays, about noon, in a hall with ten long benches; doors shut, keys laid at the Doge's feet. The Chancellor reads the vacancies. Avogadori and the Ten's Heads sit apart as watchers; the presidents of the session swear to see the laws kept and violators punished (p.22-23).
2. **Selecting nominators (lot).** Three pots hold balls, in the middle one 36 gold and 24 silver; the benches are called by lot in pairs; each citizen draws from a side pot, a gold ball is shown to a councillor, then he draws from the middle pot; gold again makes him an elector, silver loses his chance. Result: 36 electors in four groups of nine; kin of an elector may not stand; no more than two of a family (p.23-27).
3. **Nominate and confirm.** Each nine withdraws to a room where a secretary reads the laws against bribery. Each elector draws a numbered ball and names a citizen for the office bearing that number; the nine ballot, six of nine confirm, else he names another. The nominator is surety for his nominee's use of public money (p.27-29).
4. **Four competitors** per office (two for minor ones); electors are dismissed and may not vote further that day (p.28).
5. **The vote.** The secretary announces each competitor; the competitor and his kin leave the hall; the secretary reminds the voters of their oath. Boys carry double pots (white inside = favour, green outside = against, concealed so none sees the choice) along the benches with linen balls; the councillors on the Doge's right count the favourable, those on his left the others; secretaries write the totals (p.37-39).
6. **Outcome.** The competitor with the most votes **who also exceeds half** is declared; if none does, "the whole proceeding" is void and the office goes to the next session (p.39).

*Legislation.* Reports to the Great Council can be made only by the Doge and councillors, with the Forty's presidents added (CONT p.62-63). By the 15th-16th centuries the Senate had become the de facto legislature while the Great Council kept election and judicial power (ENW-GC). The Great Council's 1797 quorum was 600; I found no quorum for earlier times (ENW-GC). **The Arengo:** nothing found; I don't know. The 1268 doge election is already in the repo.

## Part 3. Venice and Rome: heresy as a contest between two authorities

### 3.1 The standing arrangement (ENW-HI unless marked)

- **Origin.** The Great Council voted on 12 Aug 1289 to admit the papal inquisition; a bull of 28 Aug 1289 established the Holy Office there, the doge keeping control over proceedings and the inquisitor swearing loyalty to the Republic. After Paul III created the Roman Inquisition (1542), Venice resisted direct allegiance; the nuncio Giovanni della Casa (from 1544) negotiated a compromise. On **22 April 1547** the Minor Council created the **three savi all'eresia** (ENW-HI; ERET; Minor Council = Doge plus six councillors).
- **Bench.** Six members: three clerics (nuncio, inquisitor, patriarch or his vicar) and three lay. Inquisitorial power was split: nuncio and inquisitors "represented Roman interests"; patriarch and Savi "Venetian interests" (ERET). Savi: senators, usually ex-Ten or ex-ambassadors to Rome, two-year terms, papalist families deliberately excluded (ENW-HI; ERET). A university handout says they had no jurisdiction of their own and that their presence breached two features of the Roman model, secrecy and purely ecclesiastical competence (search gloss of SCARAMELLA `[SUMMARY]`).
- **Gates.** At least one Savio must be present to convene; proceedings without them are void *ipso iure*; they approve arrest warrants and their authorisation is needed to carry out a sentence (ENW-HI, one source; a second gloss, from a Cardiff paper I could not open, agrees `[SUMMARY]`). They do not judge; they hold two gates, the arrest and the execution. A caution: the "void *ipso iure*" and "needed to carry out sentences" claims rest on that one encyclopedia article; I did not see the 1547 text.
- **Control of personnel.** The government could demand an inquisitor's replacement: in 1560 it removed Felice Peretti (later Sixtus V); after that the inquisitor came from the Dominicans (ENW-HI). The Ten refused in 1560 to let Peretti stay a few days more despite pressure from the legate and Rome (JEH).

### 3.2 Stages of a Venetian heresy case (ENW-HI; ERET)

1. **Denunciation.** Most cases start from a denunciation; anonymous ones were "rarely pursued unless the matter was serious".
2. **Witnesses** are examined; an arrest warrant issues once charges are confirmed (the Savi sign off); trial in absentia is possible.
3. **Interrogation** (*costituti*) until a full confession is obtained; torture, mainly strappado, in about three percent of documented cases.
4. **Defence.** The defendant may have a canon-law advocate; lying witnesses are punished.
5. **Sentence and abjuration**; the Savi authorise execution.
6. **Execution.** 18 executions in 1,560 sixteenth-century trials. Venice allowed only secret drowning, the condemned rowed out at dawn with a priest and sunk with a stone; Rome wanted public burning. Example: Spinola drowned silently on the night of 31 Jan 1567 "despite the insistence of the papal nuncio... that he be burned publicly" (ERET). Reasons offered: protecting the city's reputation for tolerance toward Protestant merchants; also fear (Martin, in ENW-HI).
7. **Patricians** were dealt with lightly: penances or exile (ERET).

### 3.3 Extradition: when does the crown hand a heretic to the church? (ERET; JEH)

Venice handed over foreign heretics of lesser standing, refused to surrender its own subjects (Aurelio Vergerio under Paul IV; Soranzo), and refused the nuncio sight of Vergerio's papers (1546). The **Council of Ten**, not the Holy Office, decided extradition (Lorenzo Tizzano, 13 Mar 1554) (ERET). One scholar holds that Venice extradited only people directly under papal authority; the JEH author finds the government's stated reasons more convincing but concedes the evidence is inconclusive (JEH). Sarpi, writing later, argues Rome's Inquisition governed only Rome and the Pope should respect each local one's arrangements (JEH, as glossed).

### 3.4 Giordano Bruno in Venice (DBI-BRUNO, read)

1. 22 May 1592: Mocenigo has him seized by his own men. 23 May: denunciation to the Venetian inquisitor Gabriele da Saluzzo, with three printed books, an autograph and two named witnesses (the booksellers Ciotti and Britano). Prison of San Domenico di Castello. Further denunciations 25 and 29 May (a long list of doctrinal charges).
2. 26 May: witnesses heard and first *costituto*. 30 May: second. 2-4 June: interrogation on the charges; Bruno argues he spoke "filosoficamente e secondo li principii e lume naturale". 23 June: favourable witnesses (including Andrea Morosini). 30 July: last Venetian *costituto*, Bruno admits "marginal doubts" and submits.
3. The case goes to Rome's Cardinal Santa Severina, who had already been interfering. 12 Sept 1592: formal Roman request to transfer. 17 Sept: the Venetian tribunal consents. 28 Sept: the request goes to the Collegio under the Doge. 3 Oct: the Collegio gives an unfavourable opinion, sent to the Senate; Rome told no. 22 Dec: Rome renews, citing precedent. 9 Jan 1593: Rome is told the Senate has approved. 19 Feb: Bruno leaves by sea to Ancona; 27 Feb: Roman prison.
4. **Not in the text:** Senate vote tally; who sat as lay assessor (DBI-BRUNO and ERET-BRUNO name only the inquisitor). A web chronology gives 20 Feb 1593 for departure and a second source gives 17 Jan for the surrender (search glosses; conflicting; `[SUMMARY]`). The path is thus Holy Office -> Collegio -> Senate, i.e. the crown's executive and legislature decide whether a church case leaves the territory, not the church bench.

Sarpi's argument: Venice was faithful to Catholicism, but secular authority could legislate on church property and punish criminal clerics; the "ecclesiastical liberty" charge was pretext (DBI-SARPI). A Sarpi summary says a compromise left Venice "the right to try clergy in civil courts" (ENW-SARPI), but I could not verify the terms of settlement beyond handing over the prisoners. Whether the Ten acted in the Interdict: not in any text I read.

### 3.6 The Foscarini affair (ENW-FOSC; ENW-PAF)

Two Foscarinis. **Antonio Foscarini** (Venetian patrician, ex-ambassador) is the Ten's case:
1. 20 Feb 1615: his secretary Giulio Muscorno denounces him to the Ten for selling state secrets to Spain. He is held about three years and absolved 30 July 1618.
2. 8 Apr 1622: arrested leaving the Senate; charged with meeting foreign ministers and passing secrets to the Emperor's secretary and to **the Pope's nuncio** at the Arundel residence. Witnesses are State Inquisition agents Domenico and Girolamo Vano, relaying a servant of the Spanish ambassador.
3. Tried by the Ten; "presumption of guilt" is held established; convicted 22 Apr 1622, strangled in prison, body hung head-down between the Piazzetta pillars. Torture is not mentioned.
4. 16 Jan 1623: the Ten exonerates him after the Vanos and the servant retract; the Ten publicly acknowledges error, a state funeral follows. A request for further interrogation to find co-conspirators was declined. Dates differ between sources (execution 20, 21 or 22 April; exoneration 16 or 28 Jan 1623; I give the Wikipedia page's).
5. Church link: the page places the case in the post-Interdict rivalry of "Giovani" and "Papalisti"; Sarpi declined Foscarini's legacy; "motives remain unknown". Here the contact with a papal envoy is a state-secrets charge, tried by the Ten, not a church matter.

**Paolo Antonio Foscarini** is a different man: a Calabrian Carmelite whose 1615 Copernican letter was condemned in 1616 by the Roman Inquisition, with no Venetian connection (ENW-PAF). If the brief meant this Foscarini, it is a Roman case, not a Venetian one.

### 3.7 What the Venetian pattern gives the engine as "crown over church"

1. **Church tribunal, crown gate.** The church bench exists on the crown's territory, but the crown holds the arrest gate and the execution gate through lay assessors (ENW-HI).
2. **Control of the personnel** (inquisitor removal 1560).
3. **Subject-status filter on the forum:** own subjects stay; foreigners of lesser standing may go; decision made by the crown's executive/legislature (Collegio, Senate or Ten), not the church bench (ERET; DBI-BRUNO).
4. **Mode of punishment** differs: secret drowning versus public burning (ERET).
5. **Jurisdiction over clergy** is asserted by statute, and defended by ordering clergy to carry on; non-compliant orders are expelled (JESUIT). The church's censure then bites only where local clergy follow Rome: "most clergy complied with Venice" (JESUIT; ENW-SARPI), which is the repo's item 13a (weight scales with local legitimacy), seen from the crown's side.

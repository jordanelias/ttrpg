# The Inquisition and the Heresy Trial: Procedure, Crown Entanglement and Local Legitimacy

## Status: FILED (reference, tier 3; informs design, settles nothing)

## 1. The medieval process, stage by stage

### 1.0 The form it departed from

Lateran IV (canon 8, for proceedings against clerics) allows three routes: **accusation** (a formal written charge first, *legitima inscriptio*), **denunciation** (after charitable admonition), **inquiry** (after public notice or "clamorous insinuation"). The accused is present, learns "the charges, the witnesses' names and testimony", may offer exceptions; the superior may not be "both accuser and judge" [Lat4, c. 8]. A notary records every citation, testimony and appeal and each party gets a copy [Lat4, c. 38]. Canon 18 bars clerics from blessing ordeals, so proof moves to confession and witnesses; Bueno ties inquisitors' reliance on witnesses to the "disappearance of archaic proofs" [Bueno, 53]. Innocent III's *processus per inquisitionem* let a court act without an accuser [W:Inquisitorial system]. The repo's *libellus* then *litis contestatio* skeleton I could not source to a primary text `[FROM MEMORY, UNVERIFIED]`. **What the heresy inquisition took out of the canon 8 inquiry:** the accuser's liability, the accused's sight of names, counsel, and the separation of judge from prosecutor.

### 1.1 Stages in order

| # | stage | what happens | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Who starts it** | Bishops must visit and find heretics (*Ad abolendam*, 1184); canon 3 has three or more reputable men, or the neighbourhood, swear to report heretics, refusal marking the refuser a heretic. Papal inquisitors (mostly Dominicans, Franciscans) from 1231 under Gregory IX; an inquiry opens on arrival in a district. | [W:Medieval Inquisition]; [Lat4, c. 3]; [W:Bernard Gui] |
| 2 | **Term of grace** | A month; confessors get light penance (pilgrimage), no prison, no handover, and their confessions yield names. Spanish tribunals gave 30-40 days. Toulouse, Easter 1235: crowds came forward so thick that Dominicans borrowed Franciscans and parish priests. | [CE]; [W:Spanish Inquisition]; [Lea, 2.16] |
| 3 | **Citation** | Usually by letter to the parish priest; sometimes secular authorities. Of d'Ablis's 17 cases, 4 were spontaneous ("*veniens non citatus*"), the rest began from a denunciation. | [CE]; [Bueno, 51-52] |
| 4 | **Oath; bail** | Sworn on the four Gospels to appear and accept sentence; usually not jailed during inquiry; bail possible; breaking the oath counts against him. A panel of lieutenants, clerics, officials and a notary sits as witnesses to each session. | [CE]; [Bueno, 53] |
| 5 | **Interrogation** | By the inquisitor or lieutenants (d'Ablis mostly delegated; Fournier questioned personally). The notary records. Each deponent is asked to list others present, so one trial seeds many ("cluster model"; ~60 names from one confession). Fournier opens with a list of errors drawn from a prior denunciation. | [Bueno, 52-53] |
| 6 | **Proof** | Two witnesses minimum; persons barred in ordinary canon law (heretics, excommunicates, the infamous) accepted in heresy. Full proof is full confession or eyewitnesses. d'Ablis's 17 files use no outside witness (depositions are compared); Pamiers used witnesses often. | [CE]; [Bueno, 53] |
| 7 | **Accuser secrecy** | Names withheld, legalised under Gregory IX, Innocent IV, Alexander IV per [CE]. [CE] adds that Boniface VIII ordered disclosure; **I cannot confirm that**, and my unverified memory is of *Statuta quaedam* (Sext) and *Multorum querela* (Clementines) moving names toward disclosure to the bishop, not the accused `[FROM MEMORY, UNVERIFIED]`. Practice is clear: Fournier refused the bailiff of Montaillou the accuser's name because it "*non erat de consuetudine vel stilo officii Inquisitionis*". No confrontation or cross-examination; defence witnesses rare. | [CE]; [W:Medieval Inquisition]; [Bueno, 51] |
| 8 | **Safeguard on names** | The accused may name persons of "mortal hatred"; if the accuser is among them the charge fails and a false accuser faces life prison (Gui: a father who falsely accused his son). | [W:Medieval Inquisition]; [CE] |
| 9 | **Counsel; appeal** | *Si adversus vos* (1205) barred lawyers from aiding heretics; later an adviser "beyond suspicion". The accused may challenge a prejudiced judge and appeal to Rome at any stage; Eymerich advised against going. At Pamiers no counsel (a secondary account citing Gui's *Practica*). | [CE]; [W:Medieval Inquisition]; https://scindeks-clanci.ceon.rs/data/pdf/0003-2565/2017/0003-25651704124R.pdf (snippet only) |
| 10 | **Pressure; torture** | Prison and short rations first (David of Augsburg's four means: fear of death, confinement, visits from the tried, torture). *Ad extirpanda* (15 May 1252, after the murder of Peter of Verona) allows coercion "like thieves", short of danger to life or limb, once only, when evidence is nearly proven; confirmed 1259, 1265. Evasions: a later session as "continuation"; Eymerich allows a separate torture per charge yet writes *quaestiones sunt fallaces et inefficaces*. A confession under torture must be freely repeated next day. Clement V required the bishop's consent. In practice: no torture in d'Ablis's register, one case in Fournier's. | [W:Ad extirpanda]; [CE]; [W:Directorium Inquisitorum]; [W:Bernard Gui]; [Bueno, 54] |
| 11 | **Consilium of *boni viri*** | Innocent IV (1254), Alexander IV (1255, 1260), Urban IV (1264): consult experienced irreproachable men, 30-80 or more, on credibility, guilt, penalty, usually on the abstract case without the name. Advisory but usually followed, and milder. Inquisitor acts with the diocesan's cooperation. | [CE] |
| 12 | **Sermo generalis** | Charges reread in the vernacular; sermon; secular officials swear obedience; mercies first, then sentences minor to severe; those given death or perpetual prison handed to the civil power. Gui held 11. | [CE]; [W:Bernard Gui] |
| 13 | **Sentences** | Penances (pilgrimage, candles, crusade service, fines, whipping, pillory, crosses); excommunication; perpetual prison for those missing the term, recanting from fear, or relapsing; relaxation to the secular arm (five days to act on pain of excommunication of officials). Clerics degraded first. Scale: Gui 636 persons, at most 45 executed, 307 imprisoned, 143 crosses; Toulouse 1245-46 (Pegg) 5,400 interrogated, 184 crosses, 23 life prison, none burned; Toulouse 1308-23 per [CE] 42 of 930 relaxed. | [CE]; [W:Ad extirpanda]; [W:Bernard Gui]; [W:Medieval Inquisition] |
| 14 | **Confiscation** | Lateran IV c. 3: laymen's property confiscated; condemned clerics' to the churches that paid them. *Ad extirpanda* split it three ways (state; officials who handled the case; bishop and inquisitors) per [W:Ad extirpanda]; [CE] says the royal or imperial fiscus claimed it. **Sources conflict.** Only on perpetual prison or stake; also on the dead (88 of Gui's 636 cases). | [Lat4]; [W:Ad extirpanda]; [CE] |

**Public vs secret:** the sermo, sentence, penances and abjuration are public; accuser and witness names, the *boni viri* vote and the register are secret.

## 2. Crown and national inquisitions

### 2.1 Spanish Inquisition: the stages under a royal bench

Founded 1478 (Sixtus IV, *Exigit sincerae devotionis affectus*); the monarchs choose inquisitors; the Suprema (1483, usually six members, all crown-named) sits above the tribunals under an Inquisitor General (Torquemada, 17 Oct 1483) [W:Spanish Inquisition]. Nalle: a "hybrid religious-secular body under crown authority" (https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/inquisition-spanish). Differences from 1.1 [W:Spanish Inquisition]: *calificadores* (theologians) rule on heresy before arrest; **property sequestered on arrest**; a **voting bench** (inquisitors, bishop's representative, *consultores*) in a *consulta de fe*, with discrepancies and **death sentences to the Suprema**; **counsel assigned**, who must withdraw on judging the client guilty; *abonos* (favourable witnesses) and *tachas* (the unnamed accuser is an enemy; the mortal-enemy rule renewed); torture on "half-proven" cases (Torquemada's 1484 instructions, physician to certify health); revenue funds the institution, 700,000+ ducats claimed for the royal treasury in 1676. **Appeal to Rome was cut:** Innocent VIII provided appeals go to Torquemada (Sixtus IV's 1482 criticism had been suspended when Ferdinand objected). Kamen and others dispute the extent of torture; Lea estimated about a third of Toledo's Protestant defendants 1575-1610 were tortured (both via [W]). Totals about 150,000 prosecuted, 3,000-5,000 relaxed (disputed). **For design:** bench_basis flips from church office-holders to crown appointees and the crown treasury; secrecy stays, the external appeal goes.

### 2.2 Roman Inquisition: a church bench with no crown

Paul III, 1542: a Congregation of cardinals with international consultants overseeing local tribunals, enforcing papal rulings "within states that cooperated with the pope" [W:Roman Inquisition]. Del Col: of 51,000-75,000 Italian cases after 1542, about 1,250 death sentences; Black: torture rare. Galileo (consultants' qualification 1616; "vehemently suspect of heresy" 1633; forced abjuration; house arrest) shows the shape: **consultants qualify, cardinals judge, abjuration at a stated degree of suspicion**. Local-inquisitor dependence on princes: not sourced beyond Naples (3.4).

### 2.3 The Templars: a crown starts a church proceeding

- **Start.** Sealed orders 14 Sept 1307; arrests 13 Oct. Philip's lawyers used **heresy, the one exception to the Order's immunity**; inquisitorial trial in Paris 19 Oct to 24 Nov 1307, 138 testified, nearly all confessed under torture [W:Trial of the Knights Templar]. The Inquisitor of France was William of Paris, Philip's confessor, in office from 1303 [W:William of Paris (inquisitor)]: **a church office held by a crown man**.
- **Papal recovery.** *Pastoralis praeeminentiae* (22 Nov 1307) ordered all monarchs to arrest Templars; Clement V suspended the inquisitors (Feb 1308) [W:Trial]; [W:William of Paris] adds that Philip's contemptuous reply to a complaint from Edward II made Clement reinstate William (single source). Poitiers, 28 June to 2 July 1308: at least 54 told the papal commission their confessions came under torture; *Faciens misericordiam* (12 Aug 1308) absolved Molay and others.
- **Defence.** 14 Mar 1310, 127 articles read to Templars willing to defend the Order (Peter of Bologna, Renaud de Provins). The new Archbishop of Sens took over in May; 54 burned as relapsed on 12 May. **Retraction made relapse, which silenced the defenders**; Molay, who retracted again, was burned 18 Mar 1314.
- **Disposal.** *Vox in excelso* (22 Mar 1312) suppressed the Order by administrative act, not verdict; *Ad providam* gave assets to the Hospitallers, yet Philip took "compensation" for trial costs and 200,000 livres tournois (21 Mar 1313).
- **Control.** Edward II doubted the charges until papal pressure; Aragon and Portugal found no fault; Cyprus acquitted all 76 at first [W:Trial]. Same church procedure, different results where the local crown was not the prosecutor.

### 2.4 Joan of Arc: a bishop's trial under an occupier

Captured at Compiègne in May 1430 and sold by the Burgundians for 10,000 francs; held at Rouen, the English administrative capital. Bishop Cauchon (Beauvais; councillor to Henry VI by 1423) claimed jurisdiction as the capture was in his diocese [W:Trial of Joan of Arc; W:Pierre Cauchon]. Stages: inquiry from 9 Jan 1431 (the Domrémy investigator found nothing; Cauchon refused to pay him); interrogations 21 Feb to 17 Mar; vice-inquisitor Le Maistre, canonically required, came 22 Feb and 13 Mar and later said he was threatened; 70 articles from 26 Mar cut to 12; the University of Paris gave an opinion; abjuration 24 May under threat of burning; relapse 28 May; burned 30 May. **Counsel was denied; her offer to submit to the pope was refused** ("too far away"; a witness later blamed English pressure) [Linder, a popular account, http://law2.umkc.edu/faculty/projects/ftrials/JoanArcAccount.html]. **Reversal is the same entanglement:** Charles VII ordered an inquiry in Feb 1450; Joan's mother petitioned Callixtus III; retrial opened 7 Nov 1455 and annulled the sentence 7 July 1456 as "tainted with fraud", on about 115 witnesses, without ruling on her sanctity; the royal motive was that her condemnation implied a coronation by a heretic [W:Rehabilitation trial of Joan of Arc]. The bench is church; the controlling crown shapes its seats, custody and the appeal refused.

### 2.5 England: the crown checks the church, then lends it the fire

- **Clarendon (1164)** (Avalon, https://avalon.law.yale.edu/medieval/constcla.asp). C. 3: clerks accused are summoned by the king's justice into his court, to answer there what that court says and in the church court what it says, the justice sending to the church court "to see in what manner the affair will there be carried on"; a clerk convicted or confessed, "the church ought not to protect him further" ([W:Constitutions of Clarendon] adds royal officer present, degradation, return for royal punishment). C. 7: no tenant-in-chief excommunicated, no land interdicted, unless the king or his justice is first asked. C. 8: appeals go archdeacon, bishop, archbishop, finally the king, "so that it shall not proceed further without the consent of the lord king". After Becket's murder (29 Dec 1170) Henry revoked the two clauses "against canon law"; the rest stayed [W].
- **Benefit of clergy.** Claim by tonsure and dress, later a reading test (neck verse, Psalm 51:3); church courts used compurgation and by the fifteenth century mostly imposed penance; 1351 extended it to all who could read; Henry VII (1488) allowed lay claimants once, with branding; 1512 made some offences "without benefit of clergy" [W:Benefit of clergy]. **The crown narrowed which offences reached the church bench and capped appeals; it did not remove the bench.**
- **Wycliffe, Lollards.** Pre-1382 the government tolerated Wycliffite ideas that favoured royal superiority over bishops; John of Gaunt sheltered Wycliffe, and the protection "evaporated" when he left in 1386 [W:Lollardy]. Oxford's Hereford and others submitted to Courtenay without lasting penalty.
- **De heretico comburendo (2 Hen. 4 c. 15, assent 10 Mar 1401).** The diocesan may arrest anyone "defamed or evidently suspected", hold him until he clears himself or abjures, and try him through commissaries; books surrendered within forty days of proclamation; the **convicted who refuse to abjure, or relapse, go to the secular court, and sheriffs and mayors must attend when the diocesan requires and have the person burned "in an high place"** (https://www.umsl.edu/~gradyf/medieval/dhc.htm; dates from [W:De heretico comburendo]). Sawtrey burned March 1401; Badby first layman, 1410. Coke (1612) reports a writ lay on conviction before an Ordinary, High Commission being safer. Kelly (cited in [W:Lollardy]) reads the statute as equating heresy with sedition. Oldcastle, Henry V's close friend, tried 1413, escaped, rose in revolt; persecution hardened. **Effect:** relaxation became a compulsory duty of royal officers at the bishop's call.

### 2.6 Philip IV in Languedoc: the crown as second authority

From [Lea, 2.62-2.84]: 13 May 1291, after Carcassonne burghers appealed over inquisitor Nicholas d'Abbeville (who had jailed the notary drawing the appeal), Philip ordered his seneschal **no longer to obey inquisitors' commands to arrest** unless the accused was a confessed heretic or "persons worthy of faith" vouched he was publicly defamed. In 1301 two Reformers (Jean de Picquigny, Richard Nepveu) came; a royal commission's report removed Foulques de Saint-Georges (letters 8 Dec 1301); Bishop Castanet of Albi's temporalities were sequestered and a 20,000-livre fine taken. The **1302 ordinance** put the Toulouse prison under joint control, took away the inquisitor's **arbitrary arrest** (consult the bishop; a majority vote of cathedral and mendicant officials on disagreement; arrests only by the seneschal), and settled bail the same way: "we cannot endure that the life and death of our subjects shall be abandoned to the discretion of a single individual". Arrests "which had been taking place every week" stopped for a time. Motives mixed: Lea cites the quarrel with Boniface VIII, the fine, and that "the confiscated estates of the prisoners were in his hands, and were never released". Philip also reissued St Louis's edict and ordered royal prisons placed at the new inquisitor's disposal. **The crown modulated by controlling the secular arm and adding a vote; it never touched evidence or sentence.**

## 3. Weak local legitimacy: what did the hostile place change?

### 3.1 Narbonne and Toulouse, 1234-38

- **Narbonne** [Lea, 2.12-2.14]. March 1234 a Dominican prior's "volunteer inquisition" jailed a citizen; an artisans' confederation (*Amistance*) rescued him and drove off archbishop and viscount; December, **suspension obtained by appeal to pope, king and legate**; 1235 the people expelled the Dominicans and destroyed the records; the suburb took Raymond VII's protection; civil war to April 1237; peace March 1238 with crusader-service penalties on some rebels. The consuls' letter to Nîmes lists "arbitrary and illegal methods of procedure".
- **Toulouse** [Lea, 2.15-2.21]. Raymond's influence delayed the break. Easter 1235: mass confessions, then seizures; a mob rescued a prisoner from the viguier; burning dead heretics' bodies inflamed the city. The **consuls expelled** inquisitor William Arnaud (a Kyoto University abstract in a search snippet gives 15 Oct 1235) and on 5-6 Nov the Dominican convent; Franciscans who published the excommunication (10 Nov) were also expelled. Gregory's brief of 28 Apr 1236 held the count responsible, recalled his unperformed crusade vow, and asked Frederick II to withhold feudal service. Raymond, summoned by the legate to Carcassonne, promised to restore the friars and did, **on 4 September after ten months**; a Franciscan co-inquisitor was added "as a concession to popular feeling". [W:William Arnaud] says March 1236: **the dates conflict and I did not resolve it.** Avignonet, 28-29 May 1242: Arnaud and eleven others killed from Montségur; the bailiff arranged it, perhaps with Raymond's tacit assent; no murderer punished [W:William Arnaud].
- **Verdict.** Hostility **stopped the proceeding for months and changed its personnel**, but no rule of evidence or sentence. The restoring force was **external**: the threat of another crusade on the count, not local piety. Pelhisson (Dominican eyewitness) and Lea are the two partisan poles; I saw Pelhisson only in a snippet.

### 3.2 Carcassonne and Albi, 1299-1320: the crown joins the locals

[Lea, 2.76-2.85] with [W:Bernard Délicieux]. After the Albi arrests of Dec 1299, the chief citizens bound themselves by **notarial instrument to prosecute Bishop Castanet and d'Abbeville in the king's court**; the Franciscan convents became the opposition's headquarters; after the Reformers came, Albi crowds shouted "Death" at their own bishop. Délicieux read the Carcassonne consuls' 1299 accord with d'Abbeville to the people in Aug 1303; riots followed. Picquigny moved the Albi prisoners to royal prisons but did not free them; d'Ablis excommunicated him (29 Sept 1303); Carcassonne, Albi and Cordes pledged 3,000 livres for his appeal to Rome; Benedict XI, a Dominican, had him turned out at Perugia (17 May 1304) and issued *Ea nobis* (16 Apr 1304) for Délicieux's arrest, not carried out. The Inquisition **recovered the Albi prisoners and held them to the last**. Délicieux was tried at Carcassonne in 1319 (judges Fournier and Mostuéjouls), tortured, convicted of obstructing the Inquisition, sentenced to life solitary confinement; John XXII overrode the judges' leniency [W:Bernard Délicieux]. **Verdict.** Local hostility mattered **only because the crown chose to act on it** (quarrel with Boniface, the fine, the estates). It changed who held the prison keys and how arrests were authorised; it did not change what counted as proof, and when the pope sided with the inquisitors it ended.

### 3.3 Bohemia, 1410-36

Archbishop Zbyněk acted on papal bulls (20 Dec 1409; Wycliffe's books burned 1410; Hus excommunicated; Prague under interdict 1412); Hus appealed on 18 Oct 1412 "to Jesus Christ as the supreme judge". At Constance, under Sigismund's safe-conduct, he was imprisoned from 28 Nov 1414, denied an advocate, heard on 5 and 8 June 1415 on 39 sentences from his writings, asked to recant, degraded and handed to the secular power on 6 July [W:Jan Hus]. The reaction is the case: a **Protestatio signed by about 100 Bohemian and Moravian nobles (2 Sept 1415)**; a search-result summary (page not opened, unverified) says the Council summoned them, they did not come, and they formed a confederacy for free preaching while professing obedience to their own bishops. Martin V proclaimed a crusade (17 Mar 1420); the Four Articles of Prague demanded free preaching and communion in both kinds; papal-backed crusades failed; the **Compacts of 5 July 1436** allowed communion in both kinds; Rome would not recognise the Estates' elected archbishop [W:Hussite Wars]. **Verdict.** The distant council-and-emperor procedure ran to its end, home hostility unable to reverse it; the hostility changed what came after: the church could not summon or execute in Bohemia, and the end was a **negotiated settlement outside the trial form**. Wenceslas IV's stance: not sourced here.

### 3.4 Aragon and Naples

- **Aragon.** The royal inquisition infringed local rights; an inquisitor was killed in Zaragoza cathedral in 1485 [Nalle]. In **1591** Philip II had Antonio Pérez, sheltering under Aragon's fueros, charged with heresy to bring him before the Inquisition; a first transfer (May) was broken up, the second (Sept) caused over thirty deaths; Castilian troops entered; Justicia Juan de Lanuza was beheaded 20 Dec 1591; the Cortes of Tarazona (1592) cut Aragonese rights (Wikipedia "Alterations of Aragon", search summary only; Nalle). **Verdict.** A crown using a church procedure to evade local law got its man by an army; the **local law lost**.
- **Naples, 1547.** Viceroy Toledo tried to introduce the Spanish Inquisition; the landed nobility revolted, reportedly fearing confiscation; Charles V ordered withdrawal [W:Pedro de Toledo (viceroy of Naples)]. **Verdict.** A hostile propertied class blocked the form altogether; their stated fear was confiscation. One encyclopedic source.

### 3.5 What hostility changed

| lever pulled by hostile locals or rulers | cases | effect found | not changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deny the secular arm (arrest, prison, bail, execution) | Philip 1291, 1302; Toulouse 1235 | arrests paused; arrest needs a second authority's assent | evidence, name secrecy, sentence kinds |
| Expel or remove officers | Narbonne, Toulouse 1235; Foulques 1301 | months of halt; co-inquisitor of another order; records destroyed (Narbonne) | next inquisitor used the same procedure |
| Carry it to another authority | Narbonne (pope, king, legate); Albi (king); Picquigny to Rome | a suspension; a royal commission; the pope reversed | never a retry before the same bench |
| Refuse the summons | Bohemia 1415 | proceeding cannot reach them; ends in negotiation | the verdict already given |
| Property fear | Naples 1547; Aragon | institution blocked or fought | stages, since never begun |
| Crown joins with its own motive | Philip IV; Charles VII; Henry IV | bench seats, prisons, bail, appeal ceilings, executors change | the doctrinal question |

**Net:** across all sourced cases, hostility changed **whether, where, by whom and how fast** the proceeding ran, and **who the second authority was**; the bench's internal rules moved only when a crown or pope wrote a new one. The counter-pressure that restored the church was external (a crusade threat, a Dominican pope), not local piety. A **control**: the bailiff of Montaillou, locally powerful and personally hostile, was refused the accuser's name by Fournier; hostility not joined by another authority did not bend the proceeding.

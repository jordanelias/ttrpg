## 1. The Latin canon

### 1.1 The three duties and the three styles

- **Duties.** `Or` 69: the orator must "prove, please and sway (persuade)"; "To prove is the first necessity, to please is charm, to sway is victory; for it is the one thing of all that avails most in winning verdicts." (docere / delectare / movere). `DO` II.115 puts the same triad as persuasion resting "wholly upon three things: the proof of our allegations, the winning of our hearers' favour, and the rousing of their feelings" (probare / conciliare / movere). Note the pairing differs: in `Or` the middle term is *delight*; in `DO` it is *winning favour*. A scholarly dispute over which is Cicero's "own" is not one I can source: `I don't know`.
- **Styles.** `Or` 69: "three styles, the plain style for proof, the middle style for pleasure, the vigorous style for persuasion; and in this last is summed up the entire virtue of the orator." `Or` 70: the master "controls and combines" the three and judges "what is needed at any point." The aptness rule is that style follows duty, and duty follows the point of the case.
- **Mechanism.** Each duty has a different failure: unproved claims (docere), a cold or tedious hearing (delectare), unmoved judges (movere). The skill is selection by moment, not strength in one.

*Engine:* the three duties are three channels, not one stat: docere = **leverage** from proofs; delectare/conciliare = **reception** (a state of the audience, read from the ledger); movere = **leverage** priced by **aptness**; the styles are speech kinds whose **aptness** is set by rung.

### 1.2 The parts of a speech (`DI` I.19-109, `RAH` I.3-17, II.47-50)

`RAH` I analysis lists six: Introduction, Statement of Facts, Division, Proof, Refutation, Conclusion; `DI` I.19 lists exordium, narratio, partitio, confirmatio, reprehensio, conclusio. (`peroratio` is the common Latin name for the conclusio; `Q` VI.1 uses it.)

| part | what it is for | what the speaker may do | what he may not or should not |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exordium** (`DI` I.20-26; `RAH` I.6-11; `Q` IV.1) | `DI` I.20: bring the auditor "into a proper condition to receive the rest": well-disposed, attentive, receptive (benevolus, attentus, docilis). | Classify the cause first: honourable, difficult, mean, ambiguous, obscure (`DI`; `RAH` I.5 uses honourable / discreditable / doubtful / petty). Honourable cause: use the opening or skip it (`RAH` I.6). Doubtful: build on goodwill. Petty: build on attention. Discreditable: use *insinuatio* (below) unless one can win goodwill by attacking the adversary (`RAH` I.6). Four sources of goodwill: our person, the adversaries, the hearers, the facts (`RAH` I.8). `Q` IV.1.7: be believed a good man ("an absolutely reliable witness"), take up the case from duty, plead weakness (natural favour for those struggling). | `Q` IV.1.8-10: avoid appearing abusive, proud or slanderous to persons or bodies who cannot be hurt without the judges' disapproval; nothing against the judge. `Q` IV.1.12: pity touched lightly, "not run to death." |
| **Insinuatio** (subtle approach; `RAH` I.9-11) | Win a hostile or wearied hearer "less obviously." | Three occasions: discreditable cause; hearer already won over by the opponent; hearer wearied by previous speakers (`RAH` I analysis, §§9-10). | `RAH` I.11 lists faulty introductions (I did not read the list closely enough to reproduce it). |
| **Narratio** (`DI` I.27-30; `RAH` I.12-16) | State the facts so the issue appears. `DI` I.19: an exposition of events that have occurred or are supposed to have occurred. | Three virtues in a real cause: brevity, clarity, plausibility (`RAH` I.14). May include a digression to attack, compare or amplify (`DI` I.19). | Narrative is not proof; an unplausible account fails even if true (`RAH` I.16 on plausibility). |
| **Partitio / divisio** (`DI` I.31-33; `RAH` I.17) | `RAH` I.17: set out what is agreed and what is contested, then enumerate what you will argue. | `DI` I.32-33: brevity, completeness, conciseness. | `DI`: no useful argument "introduced late as an addition", "faulty and unseemly in the highest degree." |
| **Confirmatio** (`DI` I.34-77; `RAH` I.18-25) | Proof, by the issue (stasis) in force. | Same inventional sources as refutation (`DI` I.78). | Nothing is said in `DI` I.78 beyond that reuse: no separate prohibition I could source. |
| **Refutatio / reprehensio** (`DI` I.78-96) | `DI` I.78: arguments used "to impair, disprove, or weaken" the opponent's confirmation. | May use the same sources of invention, because "any proposition can be attacked by the same methods of reasoning by which it can be supported." | Aristotle's parallel limit: the "turn the argument back" move is ridiculous if you do what you attack (`A` II.23 line 6). |
| **Peroratio / conclusio** (`DI` I.98-109; `RAH` II.47-50; `Q` VI.1) | `DI` I.98: "the end and conclusion of the whole speech"; three parts: summing-up (enumeratio), exciting indignation against the opponent (indignatio), arousing pity (conquestio). | Vary the summing-up so it does not read as formula (`DI` I.99). Conquestio first softens the hearer by commonplaces on fortune's power and human weakness (`DI` I.106). | `DI` I.109: nothing "dries more quickly than tears", so keep conquestio short. `Q` VI.1.44-47: never attempt tears "without drawing on all the resources of eloquence"; failure is anticlimax; "no halfway house between tears and laughter." `Q` VI.1.40-44: staged props misfire (the wax corpse; the boy who said his tutor pinched him). |

Structural facts: order is a rule (the cause class decides whether the opening is goodwill, attention or stealth), and late additions to the plan are penalised (`DI` I.33).

*Engine:* exordium = a pre-roll that moves **reception** (goodwill, attention, receptiveness: three separable states); narratio = a **ledger-claim** in the speaker's own words (what the audience will later weigh); partitio = declaring the `proofs` to be offered, so undeclared ones price worse; confirmatio/refutatio = the main opposed roll (**pool** vs **obstacle**, **leverage** from proofs); peroratio = a last, high-variance **leverage** move gated by **aptness** (fails symmetrically, per `Q` VI.1.46).

### 1.3 Quintilian: the *vir bonus* and the audience

- **Definition.** `Q` XII.1.1: the orator is Cato's "a good man, skilled in speaking," and "above all he must be a good man." Reason one: eloquence serving crime is the most pernicious thing (`Q` XII.1.1); reason two (`Q` IV.1.7): perceived goodness is "the strongest influence at every point of the case," because the advocate then seems "not so much a zealous advocate as an absolutely reliable witness."
- **Qualification.** `Q` XII.1.38-45 allows the good man to defend a guilty client, to use arguments that "bear a close resemblance to fraud," and to lie when a worthy man would otherwise be condemned (the tyrant-plot case, the commander the state needs). Quintilian says the good man need not often do this (`Q` XII.1.44), and that the definition survives it. He says the view is not strictly his own but that of those whom antiquity held the gravest teachers of wisdom (`Q` XII.1.33-35); whether he endorses lying is read both ways by scholars: `I don't know` the sources for the split.
- **Decorum beats utility.** `Q` XI.1.8-11: "no one can be said to speak appropriately who has not considered not merely what it is expedient, but also what it is becoming to say"; where they conflict "expediency must yield." His example: Socrates would have been acquitted by a submissive forensic defence and refused it as unworthy of his character. This is the strongest ancient statement that a speaker's standing *forbids* some winning moves.
- **Reading the audience.** `Q` IV.1.14: the adversary is attacked by arousing the hearer's emotions against *his class*: "power is generally attended by envy, abject meanness by contempt, guilt and baseness by hatred, three emotions which are powerful factors to alienate the good-will of the judges." `Q` IV.1.16-17: learn the judge's disposition (harsh, mild, pleasant, grave) and either take his nature into the case where it suits or soften it where it conflicts; deal with a judge who is a friend of the adversary or an enemy of ours. This is Aristotle's character-by-fortune table (section 2.4) turned into a procedure.
- **Feel it first.** `Q` VI.2.26-28: "we must be moved ourselves before we attempt to move others"; *phantasiae* (visions) make absent things present. `DO` II.189-190 states the same ("he will not be prompted to compassion unless you have shown him the tokens of your own grief"). Quintilian's pathos/ethos (`Q` VI.2.8-9): pathos is the violent, momentary emotion; ethos the calm, continuing one that wins goodwill; Quintilian notes the "continuous / momentary" distinction has exceptions.

*Engine:* *vir bonus* is **reception** keyed to the speaker's ledger (the audience reads past conduct, not a stored number, per target-model item 7); the **vir bonus qualification** is a licence: some moves (forgery, lies) are legal for an office-holder only at a reputational price. Decorum-over-utility is an **aptness** ceiling that standing sets regardless of expected value. The class-emotion attack is **reception** moved by a ledger-claim about the opponent's *rank* (envy of power, contempt of abjection, hatred of guilt).

## 2. Aristotle's *Rhetoric* as mechanism

### 2.1 Common topics (`A` II.23)

`A` II.23 lists lines of argument. In Roberts' text they are numbered 1-27 with two unnumbered entries (the "logical division" line follows 8; "draw meanings from names" follows 27), giving 28 by count; the numbering in other editions differs, so cite by content, not number.

| group | topoi (II.23 line) |
|---|---|
| **Opposites and relations** | opposites (1); modification of the key-word, "just" does not always mean "beneficial" (2); correlatives, with the check that A being rightly treated does not entail B rightly treating (3); a fortiori / more and less (4); parity |
| **Time and consequence** | time, as when Iphicrates argued the Thebans would have promised the pass beforehand (5); cause present means effect present (24); a better course was available (25); inconsistent past action (26); past mistakes as grounds of accusation or defence (27) |
| **Definition and division** | definition (7); various senses of a word (8); logical division by motives, "of three motives, A and B are out of the question" (unnumbered); parts of a subject; induction (10); meanings from names |
| **Authority and precedent** | a decision already pronounced on the same or a like case (11); rational correspondence (16); the same results imply the same antecedents (17) |
| **Motive and character** | possible motive is the real one (19); inducements and deterrents, "common to forensic and deliberative" (20); things approved openly differ from those approved privately (15); change of choice over time (18); refuting by contrasts and contradictions in the opponent's dates, acts or words (22); why the facts are not as supposed, for the slandered (23); incredible-but-reported things (21) |
| **Turning** | apply to the opponent what he said against you (6), with the limit that you must be sure he is the likelier culprit or "you will make yourself ridiculous", and it is ridiculous if you do the thing yourself |

`A` II.24 then lists topoi of *apparent* enthymemes (fallacies) and II.25 the ways to refute. The mechanism: **an argument is a move on a stored premise (a commonly held opinion), not a derivation**, and every line comes with its own failure test. Five of the lines (3, 6, 7, 22, 26) are explicitly self-limiting.

*Engine:* a topos is an **aptness** row (what premise it needs, what it breaks on); the premise it needs is a **ledger-claim** (a held opinion or a past act). Lines 6, 22 and 26 need a *ledger entry on the opponent* to be usable at all.

### 2.2 Deliberative topics (`A` I.4-8)

`A` I.4 lists the main subjects of political deliberation: ways and means (revenue and expenditure), war and peace (military strength and past wars), national defence (garrisons, fortifications), food supply including imports and exports, and legislation, which the text calls the most important. `A` I.5: the end sought is happiness and its constituents (good birth, friends, wealth, children, honour, health, virtue and the like). `A` I.6: the deliberative aim is utility (the good); `A` I.7 gives tests of the greater good (more self-sufficient, more lasting, rarer, an end rather than a means, chosen by better people, having a greater evil as its opposite). `A` I.8 maps four constitutions to ends: democracy, freedom; oligarchy, wealth; aristocracy, education and national institutions; tyranny, protection of the tyrant.

Mechanism: **the speaker must know the standard the hearer judges by**, and the standard is set by the polity's form. This is the origin of "weight scales with local legitimacy": the same argument has different aptness under different constitutions.

*Engine:* the five subjects are the **matter** classes a deliberative row accepts (`proofs` and `apt_genre`); I.8 is a **reception** weighting keyed to the venue's regime (target-model item 13a).

### 2.3 Forensic topics (`A` I.10-14)

`A` I.10: three things to establish: the incentives to wrongdoing, the state of mind of wrongdoers, and the kind of persons wronged. Wrong-doing is "injury voluntarily inflicted contrary to law" (special/written or general/unwritten law). Seven causes of any action: chance, nature, compulsion (not from the person), habit, reasoning, anger, appetite (from the person). Aristotle says age, wealth and moral type are not independent causes: "it is not through youth that they act accordingly, but through anger or appetite" (`A` I.10). So the prosecutor asks which of the seven fits the adversary; the defender asks which do not.

`A` I.12, wrongdoers' state of mind: they think it can be done by them, undetected or lightly punished, or that gain exceeds penalty; confidence rises with eloquence, friends, money, or good terms with victim or judge; open injuries are easiest because none suppose them possible. Who is wronged: the trusting; the friendless and ineloquent; foreigners and small farmers who will not wait on trial; those who have often wronged others (it seems "almost no wrong is done"); those against whom there is a grievance. (`A` I.13-14, grading of wrongs: not read closely, not reproduced.)

*Engine:* the seven causes are a **motive enum** for a forensic row, and the victim-selection list is exactly the inputs to **obstacle** (defender's circumstance: friends, eloquence, wealth, standing). "Can I escape punishment" is a perceived-**obstacle** term on the *wrongdoer's* side, which is useful for NPC choosers.

### 2.4 The emotions (`A` II.1-11): three heads each

`A` II.1: emotions "so change men as to affect their judgements, and are also attended by pain or pleasure"; for each, three things must be known: "(1) what the state of mind of angry people is, (2) who the people are with whom they usually get angry, and (3) on what grounds ... unless we know all three, we shall be unable to arouse anger in any one." (`A` II.1.) Aristotle also says the orator must have a character that looks right and hearers in the right frame (`A` II.1): the audience's emotional state is a precondition, character a second one. Trust comes from three things: good sense, good moral character, goodwill (`A` II.1).

| emotion (`A` II) | state of mind | toward whom | on what grounds |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anger** (II.2) | feeling pain | those who mock, jeer, slight us without justification | "an impulse, accompanied by pain, to a conspicuous revenge for a conspicuous slight"; slights are of three kinds, contempt being one (II.2) |
| **Calm** (II.3) | not in pain; the opposite of anger | those who did not slight us, or did so involuntarily; those who admit fault, are sorry, humble themselves | a "settling down or quieting of anger" |
| **Friendship / enmity** (II.4) | wishing another good for his sake | those who have treated us well, who did kindnesses unasked | enmity from anger, spite, calumny |
| **Fear** (II.5) | believing a destructive evil is near (not a distant one: "we all know we shall die, but we are not troubled") | those who have power to harm; the "very indications" of such power | "a pain or disturbance due to a mental picture of some destructive or painful evil in the future" |
| **Shame** (II.6) | pain at bad things bringing discredit | those whose opinion matters to us | things disgraceful to ourselves or those we care for |
| **Kindness** (II.7) | helpfulness, not in return for anything | those in need, especially in great need or at a crisis | great if the helper is the only or first to give it |
| **Pity** (II.8) | able to suppose the evil could happen to us or ours; not the utterly ruined, not the over-fortunate | those who suffer undeserved evil, whom we know but are not too close to | pain at undeserved evil that might befall us soon |
| **Indignation** (II.9) | thinking we deserve a thing and others do not | those who get undeserved good fortune | pain at unmerited prosperity; opposite of pity |
| **Envy** (II.10) | falling a little short of having everything; ambition | our equals (birth, age, wealth, distinction) | pain at their good fortune, not to gain it but because they have it |
| **Emulation** (II.11) | thinking we deserve goods we lack, believing them attainable | those like us who hold highly valued goods | spurs us to get the goods; envy spurs us to stop the neighbour having them |

Mechanism: **an emotion needs a precondition in the audience AND a fact about the target.** You cannot make a hearer angry about a slight that did not happen, nor pitying toward a ruined man he thinks past help, nor afraid of a danger that is far off. Note the pity/envy/indignation triangle: whether the same prosperity produces envy, indignation or emulation depends on whether the hearer sees the holder as equal, undeserving or like himself.

*Engine:* each emotion is a speech kind whose apt use requires (1) an audience **reception** state, (2) a named target and (3) a **ledger-claim** (the "grounds") that the audience believes; remove any one and **aptness** is zero. This is a cleaner test than a stored "anger" number. Quintilian's class-emotions (section 1.3) are the same triple at the level of rank.

### 2.5 Character by age and fortune (`A` II.12-17)

For the speaker to fit his speech to the audience's type. `A` II.12 youth: passionate, impulsive, hopeful, trusting, honour-loving. `A` II.13 old age: cautious, cynical, distrustful, small-minded, drawn to gain. `A` II.14 prime of life: the mean between the two. `A` II.15 good birth: ambition and looking down on others. `A` II.16 wealth: "insolent and arrogant... a prosperous fool"; the newly enriched worse. `A` II.17 power: more ambitious and manly, serious, dignified, "if they wrong others, they wrong them... on a great scale."

Mechanism: a type table is a prior, overridden by what the speaker learns of the actual judge (`Q` IV.1.16).

*Engine:* a **reception** prior keyed to the audience member's rung/age/fortune, replaced by the ledger read as the speaker learns more.

## 3. Cialdini's seven principles: mechanism, conditions, record

**Frame.** Cialdini's list is a *practitioner synthesis* (first book 1984: six principles; a seventh, unity, added in *Pre-Suasion*, 2016 per the secondary summaries I found), not one tested theory. The broader "nudge" literature is contested: a meta-analysis found d = .43 (Mertens et al., 2022); a re-analysis found "no evidence for nudging after adjusting for publication bias" (Maier et al., 2022, PNAS, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9351501/). No book-level Cialdini effect size exists, so I give none.

## 4. Four mechanisms of resistance and processing

- **Psychological reactance (Brehm 1966, `[FROM MEMORY, UNVERIFIED]` for the book).** A threat to a freedom produces motivation to restore it, so a forceful or restrictive message can raise the forbidden option's appeal and reduce compliance. Rains 2013 (*Hum. Commun. Res.* 39:47-73; K = 20, N = 4,942) supports reactance as an amalgam of anger and negative cognitions following a freedom threat; a later review reports anger r = -.23 and negative cognitions r = -.18 with persuasion outcomes (from a 2025 meta-analysis noted in search results; I did not open it). Condition: freedom must be perceived as held and the message as controlling. *Engine:* an over-forceful speech kind lowers **reception** among a hearer who holds the contested freedom; **leverage** from pressure has a ceiling and a reversal.
- **Inoculation (McGuire 1961-64, `[FROM MEMORY, UNVERIFIED]`).** Exposing people to a weakened counter-argument, with a refutation, builds resistance to the strong one, as a vaccine does. Banas and Rains 2010 (*Commun. Monogr.* 77:281-311): 54 cases; resistance about equal at immediate and moderate delays and declining after two weeks (per summary); threat and refutational-same vs refutational-different distinctions were examined but I did not retrieve the effect sizes. *Engine:* a prior exposure is a **ledger-claim** that raises the hearer's **obstacle** against that line (decaying).
- **Elaboration likelihood model (Petty and Cacioppo 1986, *Adv. Exp. Soc. Psychol.* 19).** Two routes. Central: when motivated and able, the hearer weighs argument quality. Peripheral: otherwise, cues such as source, number of arguments, or liking. The route depends on motivation and ability. Critique: Kruglanski and Thompson 1999 (*Psychol. Inq.* 10:83-109) argue for a single "unimodel" in which cues and arguments are the same kind of evidence; Manstead and van der Pligt reply for two routes. Unsettled; sources found are abstracts only. *Engine:* **reception** has a route flag: a high-stake bench weighs **leverage** from proofs; a distracted crowd weighs cues (rank, count).
- **Story model (Pennington and Hastie 1986, 1988, 1992).** Jurors build a causal narrative from the evidence, learn verdict categories, then match story to category. A story is accepted by coverage and coherence. In the 1988 mock-juror study (*J. Exp. Psychol.: LMC* 14:521-533), guilty verdicts were 78% when prosecution evidence came in story order and defence in witness order, and 31% in the reverse (secondary sources; 119 statements from a real trial). 1992 (*JPSP* 62:189-206) found equal memory across conditions, so story structure mediated the decision. Critique: I did not retrieve any, `I don't know` the literature beyond Devine 2012 `[FROM MEMORY, UNVERIFIED]`. *Engine:* a judgment is the best-covering **ledger-claim** set; ordering of proofs (`order`) shifts **reception**; coverage of evidence is the **aptness** of an account.

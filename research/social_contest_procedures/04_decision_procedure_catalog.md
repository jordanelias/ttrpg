## 2. The 13 families and the grouping rule

**Grouping rule (the original's, which I keep).** One family per decision MODE, meaning the pair (who is the decider, by what method of choosing). Each family is then placed by the original's second axis, WHERE THE CONTEST SITS relative to the decision. Type 1 is the contest that IS the deciding, Type 2 selects who will decide, and Type 3 removes the contest by design. The Caillois label (agôn, alea, mimicry, ilinx) is the original's third axis and is interpretive; the original calls its own mapping "a generative lens" (Part E.4). I use Type 1/2/3 for placement and set Caillois aside. For the engine, `disposal` is the practical cut: the decider is a third body (`bench`), the parties agree (`mutual`), or a device decides (`none`).

## 3. The procedures, stage by stage

### Family 2: Sortition (disposal: `none`; the pool is fixed by a `bench`)

**Athenian kleroterion (juries, boule, most magistracies).** *Eligible:* citizens who put their token (*pinakion*) in their tribe's section; the age bar (30) is `[FROM MEMORY, UNVERIFIED]`. *Stages:* (1) each deme's candidates drop tokens in the chest of their section; (2) the presiding archon shakes the chest and draws tokens; (3) the first token drawn names the "token inserter" (*empektes*), who is also selected; (4) he draws the remaining tokens and slots each into its tribe's column of the kleroterion (five columns, one per tribe section, each row a *kanonis*); (5) the archon feeds a mix of black and white dice down a side tube, in numbers proportional to the jurors needed; (6) a white die seats the whole row, a black die passes to the next row, until the day's seats are full. *Count rule:* none; chance. *Sources:* Aristotle, *Athenaion Politeia* 63-64 and 64.2, via [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleroterion]. The release mechanism is lost; the page speculates two nails like an airlock. The surviving machines are second-century BCE while Aristotle describes the fourth century, and Dow argued the description fits both. *Prevents:* the page says nothing on this. The original's reading is faction and canvassing (Manin 1997; Sintomer 2023 `[ORIG]`). *Failure mode:* the contest moves upstream to eligibility for the pool (ORIG, Part B.2). *Secret:* the dice and the draw are public; the ordering of tokens in the chest is not recoverable.

**Florence: squittinio, imborsazione, tratta.** *Eligible:* guild members; initially they had to exercise the trade; the age bar was 30 for most offices and 40 for the Gonfaloniere di Giustizia, with birth declarations required from 1429 [https://cds.library.brown.edu/projects/tratte/historicalOverview.html]. The Signoria page says names of guild members over 30 went into eight leather *borse* [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signoria_of_Florence]. *Stages:* (1) a special scrutiny commission of varying composition vets names, ideally every five years, delayed or hastened by politics; (2) approved names go into the *borse* (*imborsazione*), locked in the sacristy of Santa Croce; (3) at each term's start the *borse* are brought to the Palazzo della Signoria and the notary of the Riformagioni draws, before the Gonfaloniere, Priors and colleges; (4) a drawn man is *veduto* ("seen"), and he is *seduto* ("seated") only if he clears the *divieti* (office held too recently, a relative in office, under age, absent, in tax arrears); (5) he serves two months (priors) or three or four (other offices). *Count rule:* the ballot threshold for the scrutiny is not on the page; the repo's source file says white and black beans (*fave*) `[ORIG]`, and I could not source a threshold. *Prevents:* the original says faction and patronage. *Failure modes:* pool-loading (*borsellino*, a purse of loyal Guelf guildsmen from about 1387) and bypass (*a mano* selection by the *accoppiatori*; the *Giornali* note *hic deficit prioratus* in October 1403, 1433, 1434, "might indicate" appointment) [Brown, same URL]. The 1494 Consiglio Maggiore suspended the *tratte* for election by electors. Rubinstein, *Government of Florence under the Medici* (1997) is the source for Medici control, but I read it only through the Brown and Cairn summaries. *Secret:* the contents of the *borse*; the scrutineers alone knew them (Cairn summary of Rubinstein).

**Venice: the doge's election (1268-1797).** *Eligible:* members of the Great Council (a closed patriciate after the *Serrata*, conventionally 1297 `[ORIG]`). *Stages:* 30 chosen by lot; reduced by lot to 9; the 9 elect 40; lot reduces to 12; the 12 elect 25; lot reduces to 9; the 9 elect 45; lot reduces to 11; the 11 elect 41; the 41 elect the doge [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doge_of_Venice]. The count rule is 25 of 41 for the doge, with lower figures at the earlier stages (the page gives "9 of 11 or 12, or 7 of 9"). Before 1268 a committee of 40 was named by four men (from 1172); the electors went from 40 to 41 after a deadlock in 1229. The newly elected doge was presented to the people, who by 1423 heard "Your doge" in place of the earlier conditional formula. After death an *inquisitori* commission reviewed the reign and the estate could be fined. Sources: Britannica 11th ed., Mowbray and Gollmann (2007) HPL-2007-28R1, da Canal. *Prevents:* the page says it minimized the influence of any one great family. *Failure mode:* none at the top (no hereditary dogeship, no *signore*), but the lower magistracies were saturated with *broglio* (below). *Secret:* ballots; the campaign ban is `[ORIG]`.

**Venice: *broglio*.** The semi-licit canvass in the Great Council's courtyard and on the stairs before votes on the hundreds of salaried posts, with "whispered entreaties and threats," deals, money and bets; the *Primi* dominated (Finlay, *Politics in Renaissance Venice*, 1980, as quoted in the 2026-06-28 file `[ORIG]`; I did not open Finlay). It is a failure mode of family 2/5 elections, not an independent procedure.

**Chola *kudavolai*.** Qualified villagers' names on palm-leaf tickets drawn from a pot to staff *sabha* committees (Uttaramerur inscriptions, c. 919-921 CE `[ORIG]`). I did not verify it. The stage sequence beyond "eligibility test, then draw" I do not know.

### Family 3 and Family 4: the athwart pair (disposal: `none`, with the decider a sign or a crowd)

**Roman auspices and *obnuntiatio*.** *Who:* the magistrate took the auspices; the augurs were advisers, and the magistrate had to act, withhold, or debate [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auspices]; the augural college was four patricians until the *lex Ogulnia* (300 BCE) made it nine, five plebeian. *Stages:* (1) the magistrate (with an augur) takes the auspices before an inauguration, an assembly vote, or a campaign, on Roman ground within a marked *templum*; (2) a favourable sign is valid for the day or the act; (3) an adverse sign, whether sought (*impetrativa*) or unlooked-for (*oblativa*), is reported (*obnuntiatio*), and public business stops until the next lawful day; (4) an act done despite an adverse sign is *vitiated*. The *lex Aelia et Fufia* (c. 150 BCE, "presumed") extended the right to report to all magistrates [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lex_Aelia_et_Fufia]; the *leges Clodiae* (58 BCE) repealed it, and scholars dispute how much (Cambridge: *JRS* "Clodius and the Lex Aelia Fufia"; *CQ* "Leges Clodiae and obnuntiatio," via search results, paywalled, not read). In 59 BCE Bibulus claimed he had "watched the sky" (*servare de caelo*) against Caesar's laws; no earlier documented use of *obnuntiatio* against *auspicia impetrativa* is known, and Caesar ignored it (Cicero, *De domo sua*, as reported). *Count rule:* none. *Prevents:* hasty or unsanctioned public action. *Failure mode:* the sign is a blocking move for whoever controls it; the original adds Milo against Clodius `[ORIG]`. *Secret:* the observation is single-witness; the announcement is public.

**Ordeal.** Canon 18 of Lateran IV (1215) forbids clerics to bless ordeals by hot or cold water or hot iron [https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/lateran4.asp]. Stage sequence, who administered, how the verdict was read: I don't know from sources fetched; `[FROM MEMORY, UNVERIFIED]`: priest, oath, trial, three-day inspection of the burn.

**Byzantine acclamation.** No formal rule existed; hereditary succession was custom, not law [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Emperor]. Routes on the page: Justin I "elected by army and people," Jovian and Valentinian I by the army, Procopius by the people of Constantinople, Leo I chosen by Aspar, Anastasius I by Ariadne, and Justin II taking the throne "with the support of army and Senate." Leo I was the first crowned by the Patriarch. *Stages (composite from those cases):* (1) vacancy or coup; (2) proclamation by the army or guard, in the camp or the Hippodrome; (3) raising on a shield (Julian 361, ORIG); (4) the cry *Axios!* (*De Ceremoniis*, ORIG); (5) coronation by the Patriarch from Leo I's time; (6) a co-emperor may be associated. *Count rule:* none. *Prevents:* disputed accession, by making the roar the sign. *Failure mode:* rival simultaneous acclamations; the acclamation often only ratified a fait accompli (ORIG, Part B.4). I did not find the shape of the Senate's role; I don't know it.

**Bayʿah.** The public pledge of allegiance, hand to hand: at Saqifa, Umar pledged to Abu Bakr first and others followed, and the crowd attacked the ill chieftain Sa'd ibn Ubada [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saqifa]. Disputed: Madelung reads Ansar resistance in the violence, Sunni sources call the choice legitimate (the Prophet left it to the community), and Shia sources hold that Ali was designated at Ghadir Khumm. Umar called the hasty pledge a *falta*.

### Family 5: Council deliberation (disposal: `bench`)

**The six-man *shūrā* of 644.** Umar named six Muhajirun (Uthman, Ali, Abd al-Rahman ibn Awf, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas, Zubayr, Talha) to choose among themselves; al-Yaʿqūbī reports a three-day limit and an order to execute dissenters if it split, with Abd al-Rahman excluded from the count. Abd al-Rahman consulted and put conditions to Ali and Uthman (Quran, Sunnah, the two earlier caliphs); Ali accepted only the first two; Uthman was chosen [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman]. The execution order is "reported," not established. Sunni and Shia readings differ. The original calls this a *compromissum* committee; it was a consultation, not a poll.

**Venetian councils.** The only stage-by-stage Venetian procedure in the repo is the 1268 election above. For the Senate, Great Council and Ten, I don't know the stages from this session's sources.

### Family 6: Assembly democracy (disposal: `bench`, the whole body)

**Athenian ostracism.** *Stages:* (1) each year the assembly votes on whether to hold one (the sixth of ten state-business months; Forsdyke 2005 n. 4, via Wikipedia); (2) if the vote carries, the ostracism is held two months later; (3) citizens go to the roped-off *perischoinisma* in the agora and give a name to a scribe, scratched on a potsherd (*ostrakon*), piled face down; (4) the nine archons and the Council of 500 supervise and the archons count by pile; (5) the largest pile is banished if the quorum holds; (6) ten days to leave, death on return, property kept, no loss of status, return after ten years; (7) the assembly may recall early (Cimon, Plutarch *Cimon* 17.2-6). *Quorum, disputed:* at least 6,000 votes cast in total (Plutarch, *Aristides* 7, preferred) or at least 6,000 against the one banished (Philochorus). *No trial*, no charge, no defence. *Prevents:* tyranny by a rival who is too strong. *Failure mode:* Hyperbolus (c. 417 BCE), the last, backfired when Nicias and Alcibiades combined against him; the *graphe paranomon* partly replaced it. Source: [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostracism].

**Roman *comitia*.** Voting by centuries (193) and tribes (35), accept or reject only (*uti rogas* / *antiquo*), debate externalised to the *contiones*, with the presiding magistrate controlling what is put [ORIG]; I did not re-verify.

### Family 7: Consensus and unanimity (disposal: `mutual`)

**Haudenosaunee Great Law.** *Stages (Parker text):* (1) the Mohawk and Seneca Lords pass on the question (§9); (2) they report to the Cayuga and Oneida Lords (§10), who deliberate; (3) the result goes through the Mohawk Lords to the Onondaga Fire Keepers, who decide or confirm if the two bodies agree (§§9-10); (4) the Fire Keepers deliberate in two bodies and the confirming Lord must refuse to confirm unless unanimous (§12); (5) every Onondaga Lord must attend and "agree with the majority without unwarrantable dissent" (§8). It is against the Law to pass a measure after the Mohawk Lords have protested (§6). *Sanction:* a Lord who neglects the people is warned three times through the War Chief and then divested "by order of the women in whom the titleship is vested," who select a replacement (§§18-19, 25) [https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Iroquois_Constitution]. *Date, disputed:* c. 1142 (Mann and Fields 1997) against c. 1450-1600 (most academic historians) `[ORIG]`. Recension variance is real; this is Parker's. *Prevents:* a divided confederacy. *Failure mode:* a holdout or a stalled body; the anti-frivolous-objection clause I could not find.

**Polish *liberum veto*.** Any deputy ends the session by crying *Nie pozwalam!* or *Sisto activitatem!*; deputies were bound by instructions from the *sejmik* that sent them. Until the mid-1600s it struck single laws and objectors were persuaded; later one objection voided the whole session's legislation. First use 1652 (Siciński, who objected to continuation past the statutory limit); a confederated sejm was exempt; 1764-66 reform; abolished by the Constitution of 3 May 1791 [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberum_veto]. Not pre-1600 on the evidence I fetched. Jędruch 1998 p. 128: 53 failed sejms, 32 due to the veto. Failure mode: foreign bribery of one deputy.

**Gadaa (Oromo).** Eleven age-grades of eight years each; five parties in turn, each once per forty years; nine leaders chosen by consensus every eight years; an Abbaa Gadaa serves eight years [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gadaa]. The page does not describe the assembly (*Gumii*), eligibility to vote, or removal. I don't know them from this session; Legesse (1973, 2000) is the cited authority `[ORIG]`. The role of women is debated; dating is "long before the 16th century," no precise date.

### Family 8: Parliaments and estates (disposal: `mutual`, crown and estates)

**Cortes of León 1188.** Alfonso IX summoned the *curia regis* to San Isidoro, with the nobility, higher clergy and, because of the crisis, representatives of merchants and tradesmen of the main cities. The Decreta address property, inviolability of the home, appeal to justice against the king, and a duty to consult the Cortes before war. UNESCO inscribed it as the first documented parliamentary system. The meetings were exceptional and did not produce regular attendance by commoners [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cortes_of_León_(1188)]. Cited scholarship: O'Callaghan 1969, Procter 1980 and 1970 (clause 3). Stage order beyond "summons, assembly, Decreta": I don't know. Supply-for-redress: see §1 defect 1.

### Family 9: Election by designated electors (disposal: `bench`)

**Papal conclave.** *Eligible:* cardinals only since *In nomine Domini* (1059); two-thirds required since Lateran III (1179); the Roman clergy and people elected before [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_conclave]. *Stages:* (1) electors enter locked *cum clave* (*Ubi periculum*, 1274), with two servants each and rations cut on the fourth and ninth days; suspended in 1276 and reinstated in 1294; (2) they take an oath of secrecy and to disregard outside instructions; (3) they proceed by one of the canonical modes: scrutiny (secret ballot), compromise (delegation to a committee), or acclamation *quasi afflati Spiritu Sancto*; Lateran IV canon 24 describes all three, requires "all or the majority" for a chapter election, says "absolutely no appeal" lies, and holds other forms invalid absent unanimity "as if by divine inspiration" [https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/lateran4.asp]; (4) *accessus* (switching to a leader's name) until the dean disallowed it in 1903 and Pius XII abolished it in 1945; (5) from 1621 acclamation only after a secret ballot, and self-election barred; (6) from about 1600 Catholic crowns exercised an exclusive (*jus exclusivae*) once per conclave, last by Austria against Rampolla in 1903; *Commissum nobis* then threatened excommunication for relaying it. *Secret:* ballots and deliberation. *Prevents:* long vacancies, outside pressure, self-election. *Failure modes:* deadlock, ruinous delay, rival claimants. *Appeal:* none at the canon-24 level.

**Sanior et maior pars.** The canonists weighed the "sounder" and the larger part. A scholarly reading of the Châlons and Bayeux cases treats *maior et sanior* as one standard in which *maior* can mean "weightier"; the Bayeux ruling (1231, X.1.6.57) presumes better zeal where the number is larger, and electors' consciences and *auctoritas* could be examined, not only counted (search result summarising a heyjoe.fbk.eu article; I did not open it). The original adds the point that the loser could claim to be the sounder part `[ORIG]`. Whether the Fourth Lateran text itself says "sanior": the Fordham rendering I fetched does not.

**Golden Bull (1356).** Seven electors; the election at Frankfurt, coronation at Aachen, first diet at Nuremberg; a 30-day limit from the electors' oath, then bread and water and no leaving Frankfurt until done (Ch. 2 §3); four of seven suffice, so three can no longer block; Trier votes first, Mainz seventh; electorates indivisible [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Bull_of_1356]. Who summons (I recall Mainz) and the oath wording: `[FROM MEMORY, UNVERIFIED]`. *Prevents:* the double election of 1314 and a split vote. Papal confirmation is not mentioned.

### Family 10: Succession (disposal: `bench`, the kin pool)

**Tanistry.** Eligible: male members of the ruler's *derbfine* (all male-line descendants of a common great-grandfather), often narrowed to the *gelfhine*; blemish of mind or body disqualified. The tanist is elected "in full assembly" in the king's lifetime, often years before, from a rival branch; at the chief's death the tanist succeeds and a new tanist is chosen. Failure: kin war (Athenry 1316, 28 eligible men killed with the king of Uí Maine, *Annals of Connacht*). Abolished by the 1608 *Case of Tanistry* (cited only in a note) [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanistry]; Jaski 1995 p. 127; Byrne 1973. The page names no inauguration rite.

**Kurultai.** Senior members across the empire were required (the phrase "aqa and ini," older and younger brothers); women attended without a vote; it chose the Great Khan, assigned posts and titles, and planned campaigns. A decision without the elites was illegitimate. Enthronement per Schiltberger: raised three times on white felt, carried round, seated, given a golden sword, and sworn [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurultai]. The page has no stage detail on nomination, regency or voting. The original's Ögedei 1229 (over Chagatai) and Möngke 1251 (re-legitimation as Tolui's line) rest on *Secret History* §§242-243, Rashīd al-Dīn and Juvaynī `[ORIG]`; I did not verify them. Ögedei's death in 1241 and Möngke's in 1259 halted campaigns in Europe and Syria (same page).

### Family 13: Competitive examination (disposal: `bench`, the examiners)

**Keju.** Song tiers: prefectural exam on the 15th of the eighth month, metropolitan exam in spring, then the palace exam. A 1067 decree set a triennial cycle that "only existed in nominal terms." Eligibility: merchants and artisans barred from the Sui; Song open to adult Chinese males with some limits; clan sons exempt for minor posts; Yuan ethnic quotas; Qing banner quotas. Anonymity: names hidden from 702 (Tang), anonymous palace exams 992, departmental 1007, prefectural 1032; recopying from 1015 and 1037; examiners barred from their home prefecture from 1037. Quotas from 1009. Content moved from classics to policy in the Song to the eight-legged essay in the Ming. Sanction example: Yangzhou 1711 (Qing, so post-1600), where the chief examiner was executed. Failure modes: rote essays, oversupply (about 1 *shengyuan* per 300 people by 1600), elite capture (about 60% of late-Ming metropolitan graduates from established families, Elman 2009).
